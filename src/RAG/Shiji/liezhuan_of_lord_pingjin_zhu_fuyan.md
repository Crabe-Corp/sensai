# Liezhuan of Lord Pingjin Zhu Fuyan

## 1

The prime minister Gongsun Hong was a native of Xue Xian, Zichuan Guo, Qi, and his courtesy name was Ji. When he was young, he served as an official in the Xue prison, but committed a crime and was dismissed. His family was poor, so he raised pigs by the sea. At more than forty years old, he began to study miscellaneous interpretations of the Spring and Autumn Annals. He was filial and careful in supporting his stepmother.

## 2

In the first year of Jianyuan, when the Son of Heaven had just ascended the throne, he summoned virtuous and learned scholars. At that time Gongsun Hong was sixty years old; he was summoned as a xianliang scholar and appointed as a bo shi. He was sent to the Xiongnu, but when he returned with his report, it did not meet the emperor's expectations. The emperor became angry and considered him incompetent; Gongsun Hong then fell ill, resigned from office, and returned home.

## 3

In the fifth year of Yuanguang, an imperial edict summoned learned scholars; Zichuan Guo again recommended and submitted Gongsun Hong. Gongsun Hong declined, thanking the people of his native state, and said: "I have already once gone west to accept an imperial commission but was dismissed for incompetence. I hope another candidate will be selected instead." The people of the native state insisted on recommending Gongsun Hong, and he went to Taichang. Taichang ordered all the summoned Confucian scholars to submit policy responses; among more than a hundred people, Gongsun Hong ranked lowest. When the policy responses were submitted, the Son of Heaven elevated Gongsun Hong's response to first place. He was summoned for an audience; he had a very handsome appearance and was appointed as bo shi. At that time, the southern route to the southwest Yi tribes was being opened for communication and a commandery was established. The people of Ba and Shu suffered from this; an imperial decree sent Gongsun Hong to inspect the situation. When he returned, he reported on the matter, strongly criticizing the southwest Yi as useless; the emperor did not listen.

## 4

Gongsun Hong was a man of broad and unusual knowledge, often saying that the illness of a ruler lay in not being magnanimous, while the illness of an official lay in not practicing frugality. Gongsun Hong used a plain cotton quilt and ate meat no more than once per meal. When his stepmother died, he observed the mourning period for three years. At every court assembly, he would present the issues clearly and let the ruler decide; he refused to argue or oppose in person before the court. At this time, the Son of Heaven observed that Gongsun Hong had a sincere and upright character, was eloquent in debate, well-versed in literary law and official matters, and further adorned these with Confucian learning; the emperor greatly favored him. Within two years, he rose to become Zuo Neishi. When Gongsun Hong submitted matters for imperial consideration and found something inappropriate, he did not argue about it in court. Once, while Gongsun Hong and Ju An, the Duke of Titles and Marquisates, requested a private audience with the emperor, Ju An spoke first. Gongsun Hong followed up later; the Son of Heaven was always pleased, listened to all that was said, and thus their relationship grew increasingly close and honored. He once made agreements with the high ministers and officials in private discussions, but when they stood before the Son of Heaven, they all broke their previous agreements to follow imperial instructions. Ju An confronted Gongsun Hong in court and said, "Many people from Qi are deceitful and lack sincerity. At first you joined me and the others in proposing this plan, but now you have all broken it; that is disloyal." The emperor asked Gongsun Hong about it. Gongsun Hong replied, "Those who understand me regard me as loyal; those who do not understand me consider me disloyal." The emperor agreed with Gongsun Hong's words. Whenever the favored courtiers slandered Gongsun Hong, the emperor treated him even more kindly and generously.

## 5

In the third year of Yuanshuo, Zhang Ou was dismissed, and Gongsun Hong became Yushi Daifu. At that time the southern route to the western Yi tribes was opened; in the east, Canghai Commandery was established, and in the north, Shuofang Commandery was built. Gongsun Hong repeatedly advised against it, saying that exhausting the central lands to support an unprofitable region was not wise; he wished for these projects to be abandoned. Therefore, the Son of Heaven sent Zhu Maichen and others to challenge Gongsun Hong on the benefits of establishing Shuofang. Ten arguments were presented; Gongsun Hong could not counter even one. Gongsun Hong then apologized and said, "I am a humble man from the east; I did not realize its benefits were so great. I wish to abandon the southwest Yi and Canghai projects in order to focus solely on Shuofang." The emperor then agreed.

## 6

Ju An said, "Gongsun Hong holds a position among the Three Dukes and receives a very generous salary. Yet he uses only a plain cotton quilt; this is deception." The emperor asked Gongsun Hong. Gongsun Hong apologized and said, "That is true. Among the Nine Ministers, none are as kind to me as Ju An; yet today he confronted me in court and indeed struck at my weakness. For a Three Duke official to use only a plain cotton quilt is truly an act of pretense, intended merely to fish for reputation. Moreover, I have heard that Guan Zhong served as prime minister of Qi and had three residences; his extravagance rivaled the ruler's. Duke Huan of Qi became a hegemon through him, yet this was also an act of overstepping the ruler in terms of status. Yan Ying served as prime minister to Duke Jing, ate meat no more than once a meal, and his concubines did not wear silk; yet the state of Qi was well governed. This is an example of lowering oneself to be on par with commoners. Now, I Gongsun Hong hold the position of Yushi Daifu and use only a plain cotton quilt. From the Nine Ministers down to minor officials, there is no distinction; this truly matches what Ju An said. Moreover, were it not for Ju An's loyalty, how could Your Majesty have heard these words?" The emperor regarded this as humility and became even more generous toward him. In the end, Gongsun Hong was appointed prime minister and enfeoffed as Marquis of Pingjin.

## 7

Gongsun Hong was by nature jealous and suspicious; outwardly he appeared broad-minded, but inwardly he was deep and calculating. Those who had previously fallen out with Gongsun Hong were outwardly treated kindly by him, but secretly he would retaliate against them. The killing of Zupu Yan and the relocation of Dong Zhongshu to Jiaoxi were both due to Gongsun Hong's efforts. He ate rice porridge with only one kind of meat, made from coarsely ground grain. His friends and the guests he favored relied on him for clothing and food; Gongsun Hong gave them all his salary, leaving nothing in his household. Scholars also regarded him as virtuous because of this.

## 8

When the states of Huainan and Hengshan plotted rebellion, their party was being investigated with great urgency. Gongsun Hong fell seriously ill and thought to himself that he had been enfeoffed without merit, and as a prime minister should assist the enlightened ruler in governing the state and guiding people along the path of being loyal subjects. Now that some feudal lords have plans to rebel, this is all due to the prime minister's failure in his duties. I fear I may die of shame and guilt before my responsibilities are fulfilled. He therefore submitted a memorial, saying: "I have heard that there are five universal principles for governing the world, and three ways to implement them. They are: ruler and minister, father and son, elder and younger brothers, husband and wife, and the order of seniority between elders and juniors. These five are the universal principles for governing the world. Wisdom, benevolence, and courage—these three are the common virtues of the world, the means by which these principles are implemented. Hence it is said: 'Diligent action approaches benevolence; a love of inquiry approaches wisdom; and the awareness of shame approaches courage.' Understanding these three virtues means understanding how to govern oneself. Knowing how to govern oneself, one then knows how to govern others. There has never been anyone in the world who could not govern himself yet succeeded in governing others; this is a principle that remains unchanged for a hundred generations. Now, Your Majesty personally practices great filial piety, examines the three kings, establishes the ways of Zhou, combines literary and martial virtues, encourages virtuous men with rewards, and assigns offices according to ability. Now I Gongsun Hong am a dull and mediocre man, without any merit from battlefield service. Yet Your Majesty has mistakenly elevated me from the ranks of common soldiers, enfeoffed me as a marquis, and raised me to the position among the Three Dukes. My conduct and ability are insufficient for such a position, and I have long suffered from an illness as weak as that of a man who carries firewood. I fear I will die before Your Majesty, fall into a ditch, and ultimately be unable to repay your kindness or fulfill my duties. I wish to return the marquis seal, request permission to retire from office, and step aside for more virtuous men." The emperor replied: "In ancient times, rewards were given for meritorious service and virtue. Maintaining the dynasty valued literary virtues; in times of crisis, martial prowess was favored. There has never been a change from this principle. I have long aspired to attain the exalted position of ruler, but I fear my inability to bring peace. The only ones who can share in governing with me are you; this is something you should understand. A true gentleman values virtue and abhors vice, (this is what you should know). If you act with care and diligence, you will always remain close to me. If you are unfortunate enough to suffer from an illness caused by exposure to cold and dew, why persist in your ailment? To submit a memorial requesting the return of your marquis title and retirement is to highlight my own lack of virtue. Now that affairs are somewhat less busy, you should rest, conserve your thoughts, concentrate your spirit, and take care with medical treatment." The emperor therefore granted him a leave of absence along with oxen, wine, and various bolts of silk. After several months, his illness improved somewhat, and he resumed official duties.

## 9

In the second year of Yuanshou, Gongsun Hong fell ill and eventually died while still serving as prime minister. His son Du succeeded him as Marquis of Pingjin.  Du served as governor of Shanyang for more than ten years, but was stripped of his marquis title due to legal violations.

## 10

Zupu Yan was a native of Linzi, Qi. He studied the strategies of persuasion and 纵横 (zongheng), but later turned to studying the Yi Jing, Spring and Autumn Annals, and writings from various schools. While traveling among the learned men of Qi, he was not treated with great respect or generosity by any of them. The Confucian scholars of Qi conspired together to exclude him and did not accept him in Qi. His family was poor, and he could not obtain any loans; thus he traveled north to Yan, Zhao, and Zhongshan, but none of them treated him generously. He lived as a guest in great hardship. In the middle of the first year of Yuanguan during the reign of Emperor Wu, thinking that none of the feudal lords were worth visiting, he traveled west through the pass to see General Wei. General Wei repeatedly recommended him to the emperor, but the Son of Heaven did not summon him. With his funds exhausted and staying for a long time, many officials and guests grew tired of him; he then submitted a memorial at the palace gate. In the morning, after submitting his memorial, in the evening he was summoned for an audience with the emperor. The nine matters he presented included eight related to laws and decrees, and one advising against attacking the Xiongnu. His words were:

## 11

I have heard that an enlightened ruler does not resent blunt remonstrations in order to gain broad insight, and a loyal minister dares not avoid severe punishment to offer direct advice. Therefore, affairs are managed without missed strategies, and achievements endure for ten thousand generations. Now I dare not conceal my loyalty or avoid death by offering a foolish plan; I hope Your Majesty will graciously pardon me and examine it carefully.

## 12

The "Sima Fa" says: "Though a state may be large, if it loves war, it will surely perish; though the world may be at peace, forgetting about warfare will certainly bring danger." Now that the world is at peace, the Son of Heaven holds grand celebrations; in spring he conducts hunting expeditions and in autumn military exercises. The feudal lords review their troops in spring and prepare for war in autumn—this is how they remain mindful of warfare. Moreover, anger is a violation of virtue; weapons are instruments of violence; and contention is the last resort. In ancient times, when a ruler became angry, corpses would fall and blood would flow; therefore, sage kings were cautious in taking such actions. Those who pursue military victories and exhaust themselves with warfare have never failed to regret it later. In the past, Emperor Qin Shi Huang relied on his military might, gradually devoured the world, annexed the warring states, unified the realm under one rule, and achieved a feat comparable to that of the Three Dynasties. Yet he never ceased in his pursuit of victory and wished to attack the Xiongnu; Li Si advised, "This is not advisable. The Xiongnu have no walled cities or stored supplies to defend; they move about like birds, and are difficult to capture and subdue. A light force advancing deep into enemy territory will inevitably run out of provisions; Carrying supplies on one's back while marching forward, the burden is too great to accomplish anything. Even if we seize their land, it would not be beneficial; and even if we encounter their people, they cannot be conscripted or made to defend the territory. To kill them upon victory is contrary to being a benevolent parent of the people. It would exhaust our central lands and bring satisfaction only to the Xiongnu, which is not a lasting strategy." Emperor Qin Shi Huang did not listen and thus dispatched Meng Tian to lead troops against the Xiongnu, expanding territory for a thousand li, using the Yellow River as the border. The land was inherently saline and alkaline, unable to produce the five grains. Then he conscripted all able-bodied men from across the realm to defend the northern Yellow River. For more than ten years, troops were exposed and stationed outdoors; the number of deaths was beyond count, yet in the end they could not cross the Yellow River to the north. Was this due to a lack of manpower or insufficient military equipment? It was simply an impossible situation. He also ordered the people of the coastal commanderies of Huang, Zhu, and Langya to transport fodder and grain from the sea, transferring it northward along the Yellow River; for every dan of grain delivered, thirty zhong had to be transported. Men worked hard in farming but still could not produce enough food and provisions; women spun and wove but still could not supply sufficient tents and coverings. The people were exhausted, orphans, widows, the elderly, and the weak could not support one another; corpses lay along the roads in sight of each other—this was when the realm first began to rebel against Qin.

## 13

When Emperor Gaozu pacified the world and seized territory along the borders, he heard that the Xiongnu had gathered beyond Daigu Valley and wished to attack them. The imperial censor Cheng Jin advised, "This is not advisable." "The nature of the Xiongnu is to gather like beasts and scatter like birds; pursuing them is like trying to grasp a shadow. Now, with Your Majesty's great virtue, to attack the Xiongnu—this secretly worries me." Emperor Gaozu did not listen and proceeded northward as far as Daigu Valley, where he was indeed surrounded at Pingcheng. Emperor Gaozu deeply regretted this and thus sent Liu Jing to negotiate a marriage alliance, after which the realm forgot about war. Therefore, the art of war says: "To raise an army of one hundred thousand men costs a thousand gold pieces per day." The state of Qin often maintained a force of hundreds of thousands of troops; although it achieved feats such as destroying armies, killing generals, and capturing the Chanyu, these were merely sufficient to deepen enmity and could not compensate for the cost borne by the realm. Draining the state treasury above, exhausting the people below, yet taking pleasure in foreign affairs—this is not a complete or wise course of action. The Xiongnu are difficult to subdue and control; this has not changed for generations. They live by raiding, invading, and plundering—this is their way of life, an inherent nature. Even during the eras of Yu, Xia, Yin, and Zhou dynasties, they were not governed or supervised; they were treated like beasts, not regarded as human beings. If the ruler does not look back at the traditions of Yu, Xia, Yin and Zhou dynasties but instead follows recent mistakes of later times, this is what I greatly fear, and it is also the source of suffering for the people. Moreover, prolonged warfare breeds change; hardship in affairs leads to shifting plans. This caused the people along the border to become exhausted, distressed, and disaffected; officers and officials began to doubt one another and seek private interests abroad. Hence, Liu An and Zhang Han were able to pursue their own ambitions. The reason why the policies of Qin failed was that power became divided between these two men; this is a clear example of success and failure. Therefore, the "Zhou Shu" says: "Safety or danger depends on issuing commands; survival or destruction depends on whom one employs." I hope Your Majesty will carefully examine this matter, give it some thought, and deliberate thoroughly.

## 14

At that time, Xu Le of Zhao and Yan An of Qi both submitted memorials discussing the affairs of the world, each addressing a single issue. Xu Le said:

## 15

I have heard that the danger to the realm lies in a "soil collapse," not in a "tile disintegration"—this is true both anciently and today. What is meant by "soil collapse"? It was the case in the final days of Qin. Chen She had no rank of a thousand chariots, not an inch of land; he was not a descendant of the noble families or great men, nor did he have local reputation. He was neither as virtuous as Confucius, Mozi, or Zengzi, nor as wealthy as Tao Zhu or Yidun. Yet he arose from a poor alleyway, raised his staff and spear, bared one arm, shouted loudly, and the whole realm followed like the wind—what was the reason for this? It is because the people were suffering while the ruler did not show concern, resentment festered below but was unknown above, and customs had already become corrupt while governance remained uncorrected. These three factors formed Chen She's resources for his uprising. This is what is called "soil collapse." Hence it is said that the danger to the world lies in a "soil collapse." What is meant by "tile disintegration"? It refers to the armies of Wu, Chu, Qi, and Zhao. The Seven States plotted a great rebellion, each declaring themselves as sovereigns of ten thousand chariots. They were armed with hundreds of thousands of soldiers; their power was sufficient to dominate within their borders and their wealth enough to motivate their people. Yet they could not advance even an inch westward but instead were captured in the central plains—what is the reason for this? It was not because their power was less than that of a commoner or their military weaker than Chen She's. At that time, the virtue and grace of the late emperor had not yet waned, and the people were content with their land and customs; therefore, the feudal lords received no support from beyond their borders. This is what is called "tile disintegration," hence it is said that the peril of the realm does not lie in a "tile disintegration." From this we can see that if the realm truly faces a "soil collapse," even an ordinary man in humble circumstances may become the first to commit evil and endanger the whole world, as was Chen She. How much more so if rulers of the three Jin states still existed! Though the realm may not yet be in full order, if it can truly avoid a "soil collapse," even powerful states with strong armies cannot turn around quickly before being captured themselves—this was the case of Wu, Chu, Qi and Zhao. How much less likely would it be for ministers and commoners to rise in rebellion! These two conditions are the clear essentials of safety or danger, matters that a wise ruler must pay close attention to and examine thoroughly.

## 16

Recently, the five grains have failed to yield in Guandong; harvests have not yet recovered, and many people are impoverished. Adding to this the burdens of border affairs, if we calculate and examine the situation logically, it is evident that the people will soon become restless in their dwellings. Instability makes them easily stirred into action. To be easily stirred is the sign of a "soil collapse." Therefore, a wise ruler alone observes the origins of all transformations, understands the critical points between safety and danger, and addresses them from within the court to eliminate potential dangers before they take shape. The key is simply to ensure that the realm has no "soil collapse" situation at all. Therefore, even if there are powerful states and strong armies, Your Majesty may chase down beasts, shoot flying birds, expand the hunting grounds of Yanyu, indulge in unrestrained pleasures, and enjoy the utmost delights of riding and racing—all while remaining at ease. The sounds of metal, stone, silk, and bamboo never cease to reach the ears; private entertainments with dancers, jesters, and midgets are never lacking before one's eyes, yet there is no lingering worry in the realm. Why must a ruler be named Tang or Wu? Why must customs resemble those of Cheng and Kang! Nevertheless, I secretly believe that with Your Majesty's innate virtue and broad-minded benevolence, if you truly take the realm as your priority, then the names of Tang and Wu will not be difficult to match, and the customs of Cheng and Kang can once again flourish. Once these two principles are established, then you will enjoy the reality of supreme dignity and peace; your name and reputation will be widely praised in this age. You will gain the affection of all under heaven and subdue the four barbarian regions. The lingering grace and virtue will bring prosperity for generations to come. Sitting facing south with hands clasped behind the robe, you will bow respectfully to feudal lords—this is what Your Majesty should achieve. I have heard that if one's ambition for kingship does not succeed, its failure at least brings stability. With stability, what could Your Majesty desire that you cannot obtain? What endeavor would fail to succeed? And which people would dare not submit! Yan An submitted a memorial saying:

## 17

I have heard that when the Zhou dynasty ruled the realm, its governance lasted for more than three hundred years; during the reigns of Cheng and Kang it reached its height, with punishments left unused for over forty years. When it declined, this also lasted more than three hundred years, hence the Five Hegemons arose in succession. The Five Hegemons were those who consistently assisted the Son of Heaven by promoting benefits and eliminating harms, punishing violence and prohibiting wickedness, correcting the realm to uphold the dignity of the Son of Heaven. After the Five Hegemons passed away, no sage or virtuous ruler continued their legacy; the Son of Heaven became isolated and weak, with proclamations going unheeded. The feudal lords acted at will, the strong bullied the weak, and the many oppressed the few. Tian Chang usurped power in Qi, while the Six Ministers divided Jin, leading to a division into warring states—this was when the people first began to suffer. At that time, powerful states focused on attacking while weak ones prepared for defense; alliances were formed and broken as chariots clashed in battle. Armor became infested with lice and mites, and the people had nowhere to turn or complain.

## 18

When Emperor Qin Shi Huang came to power, he gradually devoured the realm and annexed all warring states. He declared himself emperor, ruled over the entire land, destroyed the walls of feudal lords, confiscated their weapons, melted them down to cast bells and frames, as a sign that warfare would never be used again. The common people of the land were freed from the warring states and encountered an enlightened emperor; each person felt as if they had been given a new life. Had Qin relaxed its punishments, reduced taxes and levies, decreased corvée labor, valued benevolence and righteousness while devaluing profit-seeking and rights, upheld sincerity from above and discouraged cunning below, transformed customs and reformed the realm's moral climate, then peace would have endured for generations. But Qin did not adopt this path and instead clung to its old customs, promoting those who were cunning and profit-driven while demoting the sincere and loyal. Its laws became severe and governance harsh, flatterers multiplied, daily praise filled the ears of the ruler, leading to swollen ambition and unchecked desires. Desiring to extend his authority beyond the seas, he sent Meng Tian with troops to attack the Xiongnu in the north, expanding territory and advancing borders. Troops were stationed along the northern Yellow River, while fodder and grain were transported from afar to support them. He also sent Wei To Tu Sui to lead a fleet of ships southward against the Baiyue, ordering Jian Lu to dig canals for transporting provisions. They advanced deeply into Yue territory, where the Yue people fled in retreat. After prolonged campaigns and extended time, supplies ran out; the Yue forces attacked them, and the Qin army suffered a great defeat. Qin then sent Wei To to lead troops for garrisoning in Yue. At that time, Qin's troubles were stirred up by the Xiongnu to the north and entangled with the Yue to the south; troops were stationed in useless lands, advancing without possibility of retreat. For more than ten years, able-bodied men were clad in armor while women transported supplies; the hardship was unbearable. People hanged themselves from trees along roads, and corpses could be seen everywhere. When Emperor Qin Shi Huang died, the realm erupted in widespread rebellion. Chen She and Wu Guang raised their forces in Chen, Wu Chen and Zhang Er did so for Zhao, Xiang Liang for Wu, Tian Dan for Qi, Jing Ju for Ying, Zhou Shi for Wei, Han Guang for Yan. From mountain passes to deep valleys, heroes rose up together—too numerous to record fully. Yet none of them were descendants of marquises or dukes, nor high-ranking officials. They had no power or influence, rising from the alleys with staffs and spears. At the right moment they all moved together, without prior planning yet acting in unison, without agreement yet gathering at the same place. They expanded their territory rapidly until reaching the status of kings and warlords—this was due to the circumstances of the time. Though Qin held the title of Son of Heaven and possessed wealth over all under heaven, it was ultimately destroyed with its ancestral sacrifices extinguished—this was the calamity of exhausting itself in warfare. Therefore, Zhou fell due to weakness and Qin perished from excessive strength—both were victims of unchanging misfortunes.

## 19

Now, the plan is to pacify the southern Yi people, bring tribute from Yelang, subdue Qiang and Bo, conquer Huizhou, establish cities and towns, penetrate deep into Xiongnu territory, burn their walled cities—and those who discuss this praise it. This is in the interest of ministers and officials, but not a lasting strategy for the realm as a whole. Now within China there is no alarm of barking dogs, yet resources are drained by preparations for distant frontiers. This exhaustion harms the state and is not a way to care for its people. Pursuing endless desires for personal satisfaction, harboring resentment with the Xiongnu—this is not a way to ensure peace on the borders. Troubles accumulate without resolution, warfare ceases only to resume again; nearby people suffer in sorrow and hardship while distant ones live in fear—this is not a strategy for long-term stability. Now, throughout the realm, armor is forged and swords sharpened, arrows stacked with taut bowstrings, supplies transported and grain moved—there is no sign of rest. This is a shared concern for all under heaven. When warfare lingers, changes arise; when affairs grow complicated, worries emerge. Now the outlying commanderies stretch for thousands of miles, with dozens of walled cities arranged in lines. Their positions are constrained by terrain and surrounded by threats from neighboring feudal lords—this is not in the interest of the imperial house. Looking back at how Qi and Jin fell, it was because their royal houses had become weak and diminished while the six high-ranking ministers grew excessively powerful. Looking downward at how Qin perished, it was due to harsh laws and severe punishments, as well as an insatiable desire for endless expansion. Now the power of commandery governors is no less significant than that of the six high ministers; Their territories stretch for thousands of miles—not merely the resources of a few lanes and alleys. Armored troops, weapons, and equipment are no longer just makeshift tools like thorny sticks: should they face changes in the course of a thousand generations, it would be impossible to conceal or deny this.

## 20

The memorial was presented to the Son of Heaven. The Son of Heaven summoned the three men and said, "Where have you all been?" "Why did we meet so late!" Thus, the Son of Heaven appointed Zhu Fu Yan, Xu Le, and Yan An as Langzhong. Yan was frequently summoned for audiences; he submitted memorials on state affairs, and the emperor issued an edict appointing Yan as Yezhe, later promoting him to Zhongdafa. Within one year, Yan was promoted four times.

## 21

Yan advised the emperor, saying: "In ancient times, feudal lords did not have territories exceeding a hundred li; their strength and weakness were easily controlled. Now, some feudal lords control dozens of walled cities with territories spanning a thousand li. In times of peace, they grow arrogant and indulgent, prone to corruption; in times of crisis, they rely on their strength to unite against the capital." If we now use legal means to strip them of power, rebellious tendencies will arise—this was what happened with Chao Cuo before. Now, the sons and grandsons of feudal lords may number in the dozens. Yet only the legitimate heir succeeds to the fief; the rest, though blood relatives, receive no land or title—thus, the principles of benevolence and filial piety cannot be practiced. I beg Your Majesty to issue an edict allowing feudal lords to bestow their fiefs upon their sons and brothers, granting them the title of marquis. They will all be pleased to receive what they desire; through this act of virtue, the ruler can quietly divide their fiefs. The states will thus be fragmented without direct suppression and gradually weakened." Thus, the emperor followed his plan. He also advised the emperor, saying: "Now that Maoling has been newly established, all powerful families who have accumulated wealth and influence, as well as disorderly commoners, may be relocated there. This would strengthen the capital internally while eliminating troublemakers externally—this is what is called removing harm without resorting to punishment." The emperor once again followed his plan.

## 22

The enshrinement of the Wei Empress, and the exposure of Prince Dingguo of Yan's secret affairs—Zhu Fu Yan had a role in these achievements. Ministers all feared his sharp tongue and bribed him with gifts amounting to thousands of gold pieces. Someone advised Yan, saying: "You have become too overbearing." Zhu Fu replied: "Since I tied my hair and began traveling to study, it has been more than forty years. My ambitions have not yet been fulfilled; my family does not regard me as a son, my brothers do not accept me, and guests abandon me. I have long endured hardship. Moreover, if a man cannot live in luxury with five-tiered meals while alive, then he will be boiled in five-tiered cauldrons after death. I am now old and far from my goal, so I must act recklessly to achieve it."

## 23

Yan strongly argued that the Shuofang region was fertile and abundant, externally blocked by the Yellow River. Meng Tian had built a city there to drive out the Xiongnu; internally, it would reduce the burden of transporting troops and grain, expand the central kingdom, and serve as the foundation for defeating the Hu people. The emperor reviewed his proposal and submitted it to the ministers and high officials for discussion; they all said it would be inconvenient. Gongsun Hong said: "During the Qin dynasty, thirty thousand men were often mobilized to build a northern river defense; in the end it could not be completed and was eventually abandoned." Zhu Fu Yan strongly argued for its benefits, and the emperor ultimately followed Zhu Fu's plan, establishing the Shuofang Commandery.

## 24

In Yuanshuo 2nd year (127 BC), Zhu Fu reported that King Qi was indulging in licentious behavior and deviating from proper conduct. The emperor appointed Zhu Fu as the prime minister of Qi. Upon arriving in Qi, he summoned all his brothers and guests, distributed five hundred gold pieces among them, and said: "In my time of poverty, my brothers did not provide me with food or clothing, and my guests would not even let me enter their gates; Now that I am the prime minister of Qi, you all welcome me from as far as a thousand li away. I will have nothing more to do with any of you; none of you should ever enter my door again!" He then sent someone to accuse the king of having an incestuous relationship with his sister, which shook the king. The king believed he would never escape punishment and feared being executed like Prince Yan had been; thus, he committed suicide. The officials reported this to the emperor.

## 25

When Zhu Fu was still a commoner, he had once traveled in Yan and Zhao; after attaining power, he exposed matters concerning Yan. King Zhao feared that Zhu Fu would become a threat to the state and wished to submit a memorial exposing his secret misdeeds. However, because Zhu Fu held power at court, they dared not act. When he became prime minister of Qi and left the pass, people immediately submitted a memorial accusing Zhu Fu Yan of accepting gold from feudal lords, which was why many sons and brothers of feudal lords had received titles. When King Qi committed suicide, the emperor heard about it and was furious, believing that Zhu Fu had forced his king to commit suicide. The emperor summoned him for interrogation by officials. Zhu Fu confessed to accepting gold from the feudal lords, but in truth he had not forced the king to commit suicide. The emperor wanted to spare him, but at that time Gongsun Hong was the Grand Censor and said: "King Qi committed suicide with no heir; his state has been abolished as a commandery and incorporated into Han. Zhu Fu Yan was originally the chief villain. If Your Majesty does not execute Zhu Fu Yan, there will be no way to justify this to all under heaven." Thus, they exterminated the entire family of Zhu Fu Yan.

## 26

When Zhu Fu was in power and favored, he had thousands of guests; but when his family was exterminated, not a single person came to collect the bodies. Only Xiao Kongche took charge of their burial. The Son of Heaven later heard about this and considered Kongche to be an upright and virtuous man.

## 27

Grand Historian said: Although Gongsun Hong's conduct and righteousness were commendable, he also benefited from the times. More than eighty years had passed since the founding of Han; now the emperor was turning toward literature and scholarship, seeking talented men to broaden Confucianism and Mohism. Gongsun Hong stood at the forefront among them. Zhu Fu Yan, when in power, was praised by all the officials; but after his reputation collapsed and he was executed, scholars competed to speak ill of him. How tragic!

## 28

The Taihuang Taichun issued an edict to the Grand Tutor and Grand Works Minister: "I have heard that the way of governing a state begins with enriching its people; The key to enriching the people lies in frugality. The Classic of Filial Piety says, 'To pacify superiors and govern the people, nothing is better than rites.' "Between extravagance and frugality, rites prefer frugality." In the past, Guan Zhong served as prime minister to Duke Huan of Qi and dominated the feudal lords. He had the great achievement of uniting them nine times and restoring order once; yet Confucius called him ignorant of rites, because his extravagance was comparable to that of a ruler. Xia Yu built humble palaces and wore plain clothes; later sages did not follow this example. From this, it may be said that in times of good governance, virtue is at its height, and nothing surpasses frugality. Frugality transforms the customs of the people; thus, the order between superiors and inferiors is maintained, familial bonds grow stronger, and disputes cease. Is this not the foundation for every household to be well-provided-for and for punishments to become unnecessary? How important it is, then! The Three Dukes are the leaders of all officials and the exemplars for the people. There has never been a case where an upright standard produces a crooked shadow. Did Confucius not say, "If the superior leads with uprightness, who would dare to be otherwise"? "Promoting virtue and teaching those lacking ability will encourage improvement." Since the founding of Han, among those close and trusted ministers who personally practiced frugality, valued righteousness over wealth, and whose conduct was clearly exemplary, none could compare to the former prime minister Pingjin Hou Gongsun Hong. Though holding the position of prime minister, he used a coarse cloth quilt and ate plain rice with no more than one dish of meat. Therefore, for his friends and favored guests, he distributed portions of his salary to support them, leaving nothing over. Truly, he restrained himself internally while outwardly adhering to the regulations. Ji An questioned him about this, and it was reported at court; this may be called a reduction within the system that is still feasible. Superior virtue leads to action; otherwise, it ceases. This differs greatly from those who indulge in extravagance internally while wearing deceptive finery externally to seek hollow praise. When he requested retirement on grounds of illness, Emperor Wu immediately issued an edict: "Reward the meritorious and honor the virtuous; commend good deeds and condemn evil ones—this you should know well. You must reduce your worries, preserve your spirit, and be assisted by medicine." He was granted leave to recover his health and received oxen, wine, silk, and other goods. After several months, he recovered somewhat and returned to duty. He eventually passed away peacefully in his position as prime minister during Yuanshou 2nd year. There is no one who knows a subject better than the ruler—this was clearly demonstrated. Gongsun Hong's son Du inherited his title, later serving as governor of Shanyang; he lost the marquisate due to legal violations. Displaying virtue and upholding righteousness are ways to lead customs and encourage moral transformation; this is the established system of sage kings, an unchanging principle. Therefore, I shall bestow the title of Guannei Hou upon Gongsun Hong's descendants who are next in line to succeed him. They will receive a fief of three hundred households and be summoned by official carriage to present their names at the Ministry of Works; I myself will personally attend their investiture."

## 29

Ban Gu remarked: Gongsun Hong, Bu Shi, and Er Kuan all had the wings of a great goose but were trapped among sparrows and starlings, their lofty aspirations obscured by mundane affairs. Had they not encountered their time, how could they have reached such positions? At that time, more than sixty years had passed since the Han dynasty was founded. The realm was peaceful and its treasuries full, yet the four barbarian groups remained un-subjugated and many systems were incomplete. The emperor urgently desired to employ both literary and military talents, seeking them as if they could not be found soon enough. Initially, he sent a carriage with padded wheels to welcome Mei Sheng; upon meeting Zhu Fu Yan, he sighed in admiration. Ministers envied and aspired to emulate them, and outstanding individuals emerged together. Bu Shi was tested in herding and feeding livestock, Sang Yang rose from a merchant's apprentice, Wei Qing advanced from slavery, and Ri Di emerged from among surrendered captives—these were also the kind of men who once built walls or tended cattle. The Han dynasty's acquisition of talent was most flourishing at this time. In terms of Confucian learning and elegance, there were Gongsun Hong, Dong Zhongshu, and Er Kuan; in steadfast conduct, Shi Jian and Shi Qing; in integrity and straightforwardness, Ji An and Bu Shi; in recommending talent, Han Anguo and Zheng Dangshi; in establishing laws, Zhao Yu and Zhang Tang; in literary excellence, Sima Qian and Sima Xiangru; in wit and humor, Dongfang 朔 and Mei Gao; in eloquence and response, Yan Zhu and Zhu Maichen; in astronomy and calendrics, Tang Du and Luoshahong; in musical tuning, Li Yanyang; in strategic planning, Sang Hongyang; in diplomatic missions, Zhang Qian and Su Wu; and in generalship, Wei Qing and Huo Qubing. As for those who received imperial trust and carried out the late emperor's will, they were Huo Guang and Jin Ridi. The rest are too numerous to record in full. Thus, the achievements and institutions established left a legacy of writings that later generations could not surpass. Emperor Xuan inherited the throne and continued to build upon great achievements. He also discussed the Six Arts, recruited outstanding talents, and thus Xiao Wangzhi, Liangqiu He, Xiahou Sheng, Wei Xuancheng, Yan Pengzu, and Yin Gengshi advanced through Confucian scholarship, while Liu Xiang and Wang Bao became prominent for their literary excellence. Among generals and ministers were Zhang Anshi, Zhao Chongguo, Wei Xiang, Bing Ji, Yu Dingguo, and Du Yannian; among those who governed the people were Huang Ba, Wang Cheng, Gong Sui, Zheng Hong, Shao Xinchen, Han Yanshou, Yin Wengui, and Zhao Guanghan—each of them left notable achievements recorded for posterity. To list their names among the distinguished ministers is also appropriate.
