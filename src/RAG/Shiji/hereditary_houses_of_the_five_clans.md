# Hereditary Houses of the Five Clans

## 1

The Emperor Xiaojing had thirteen sons who became kings, and five mothers; those with the same mother were considered blood relatives. Li Ji's sons were Rong, De, and Eyu. Cheng Ji's sons were Yu, Fei, and Duan. Jia Funvren's sons were Pengzu and Sheng. Tang Ji's son was Fa. Wang Funvren Erxu's sons were Yue, Ji, Cheng, and Shun.

## 2

Hejian Xianwang De was appointed King of Hejian as a son of the emperor in the second year before Emperor Xiaojing's reign. He loved Confucian learning, and even in his daily attire and conduct, he always followed the ways of Confucians. Many Confucian scholars from Shandong often accompanied him on his travels.

## 3

He died in the twenty-sixth year, and his son Gongwang Buhai was installed as ruler. He died in the fourth year, and his son Gangwang Ji succeeded him. He died in the twelfth year, and his son Qingwang Shou succeeded him.

## 4

Linjiang Aiwang Eyu was appointed King of Linjiang as a royal prince in the second year before the reign of Emperor Xiaojing. He died in the third year, without any descendants; his fief was abolished and became a commandery.

## 5

Linjiang Minwang Rong was made Crown Prince in the fourth year before Emperor Xiaojing's reign, but he was deposed at four years old and later appointed King of Linjiang using his former status as a deposed crown prince.

## 6

In the fourth year, for encroaching on the temple's outer wall to build a palace, Emperor Wu summoned Rong. As Rong was traveling, he paid respects at the northern gate of Jiangling. After getting into his carriage, the axle broke and the vehicle became unusable. The elders of Jiangling wept in secret and said: "Our king will not return!" When Rong arrived, he went to the office of the Central 尉 (Central Marshal) for registration. Zhidi, the Central 尉, interrogated the king and held him accountable; fearing punishment, the king committed suicide. He was buried in Lantian. Tens of thousands of swallows carried soil and placed it on his tomb, and the common people felt compassion for him.

## 7

Rong was the eldest; upon his death without descendants, his fief was abolished, and its territory was incorporated into the Han dynasty as Nan Jun.

## 8

The above three kings were all sons of Li Ji.

## 9

Lu Gongwang Yu was appointed King of Huaiyang as a royal prince in the second year prior to Emperor Xiaojing's reign.  In the second year, after the defeat of Wu and Chu in their rebellion, he was transferred from his former position as King of Huaiyang to become King of Lu in the third year before Emperor Xiaojing. He loved building palaces, gardens, and keeping dogs and horses. In his later years he enjoyed music but did not like eloquent speech or debate. He was a man who ate voraciously.

## 10

He died in the twenty-sixth year; his son Guang succeeded him as king. Initially, he loved music and carriages with horses; In his later years, he became stingy, always fearing that his wealth would not be sufficient.

## 11

Jiangdu Yiwang Fei was appointed King of Ru'nan as a royal prince in the second regnal year before Emperor Xiaojing took the throne. During the rebellion of Wu and Chu, Fei was fifteen years old; he had talent and strength, and submitted a memorial requesting to fight against Wu. Emperor Xiaojing bestowed on Fei the seal of a general and ordered him to attack Wu. After Wu was defeated, two years later he was transferred to become King of Jiangdu, governing the former territory of Wu, and was granted imperial banners and flags as a reward for his military achievements. In the fifth year of Yuanguang, when the Xiongnu launched a large-scale invasion and caused havoc in Han territory, Fei submitted a memorial expressing his willingness to fight against the Xiongnu, but the emperor did not approve. Fei loved martial prowess and constructed palaces and pavilions; he attracted heroes from all directions, becoming very arrogant and extravagant.

## 12

After reigning for twenty-six years, Fei died, and his son Jian succeeded him as king. He committed suicide in the seventh year. During the time when Huainan and Hengshan plotted rebellion, Jian somewhat learned of their plans. He believed that his fief was close to Huainan, and feared that one day it might be attacked and annexed. Thus, he secretly prepared weapons, often wearing the seal of a general granted by his father and carrying imperial banners when going out. Before King Yiwang's body had been buried, Jian seduced Nao Ji, a favored concubine of the late king. At night he sent people to fetch her and committed adultery with her in his private residence. When the Huainan affair was uncovered, those involved in the conspiracy were investigated, and Jiangdu Wang Jian was also implicated to some extent. Jian was afraid, so he sent many people with large sums of money in an attempt to resolve the case and have it dismissed. Moreover, Jian believed in witchcraft and diviners, and had people make prayers and perform rituals with false claims. Jian also committed adultery with all his sisters. When these matters became known, the Han officials and ministers requested that Jian be arrested and punished. The Son of Heaven was reluctant to act harshly and instead sent high-ranking ministers to interrogate King Jian directly. King Jian confessed to his crimes, and then committed suicide. His fief was abolished, its territory incorporated into the Han as Guangling Jun.

## 13

Jiaoxi Yuwang Duan was appointed King of Jiaoxi as a royal prince in the third regnal year before Emperor Xiaojiing, after Wu and Chu's Seven States Rebellion had been crushed. Duan was a cruel and vicious person; he also suffered from impotence, and whenever he approached women, he would fall ill for months. He had several favored young men who served as attendants (lang). One of these attendants was caught in an affair with the concubines, and Duan captured and executed him, along with his son and mother. Duan repeatedly violated imperial laws; Han officials and ministers frequently requested his execution, but the Son of Heaven refrained out of compassion for their familial bond. However, Duan's misconduct became even more severe. The officials again petitioned to reduce his fiefdom, stripping him of two-thirds of it. Duan was furious and thus became reckless and lawless. The treasuries were ruined, leaking completely; rotting valuables amounted to tens of thousands, yet they could never be recovered or relocated. He ordered the officials not to collect taxes and levies. Duan dismissed all his guards, sealed off the gates of his palace, and went out through only one gate for travel. He frequently changed his name and surname, dressed as a commoner, and traveled to other commanderies and states.

## 14

Whenever the regional governor or officials of the rank of 2,000 dan came to his territory, they administered according to Han law; Duan would then seek out their faults and report them. If no fault was found, he would falsely accuse them and have them poisoned. Thus, through deception and schemes, his strength was sufficient to resist remonstrance, and his cunning enough to cover up his wrongdoings. If the regional governor or 2,000-dan officials followed King Duan's governance, then Han law would punish them accordingly. Thus, although Jiaoxi was a small fiefdom, the number of 2,000-dank officials it killed or injured was very large.

## 15

After reigning for forty-seven years, Duan died without a male heir to succeed him; his fief was abolished, the territory incorporated into Han as Jiaoxi Jun.

## 16

The above three kings were all children of Cheng Ji.

## 17

Zhao Wang Pengzu was appointed King of Guangchuan as a royal prince in the second reign year before Emperor Xiaojing ascended the throne. After King Zhao Suo's rebellion was crushed, Pengzu became king of Guangchuan. In the fourth year, he was transferred to become the King of Zhao. In the fifteenth year, Emperor Xiaojing died. Pengzu was a cunning and sycophantic person; outwardly respectful but inwardly harsh and calculating. He loved legal codes, using fallacious arguments to entrap others. Pengzu had many favored concubines and descendants within his household. If the regional governor or officials of 2,000 Dan wished to govern according to Han law, they would be harmed by the royal family. Therefore, whenever a new regional governor or 2000-dan official arrived, Pengzu would dress in plain black cloth robes and personally go to greet them. He would prepare their residence with many suspicious arrangements to provoke reactions, recording any slip of the tongue or mention of forbidden topics by these officials. If a 2000-dank official wished to govern effectively, Pengzu would use these recorded offenses to intimidate and coerce them. If they refused to comply, he would submit a memorial to report them, or smear them with accusations of corruption and illicit gains. Pengzu reigned for more than fifty years; no regional governor or 200-dan official could serve for two full years without being removed on charges of guilt. The most serious cases resulted in death, while lesser ones led to corporal punishment. Because of this, no 2000-danke official dared to govern effectively. Meanwhile, King Zhao wielded unchecked power and sent envoys to the counties to monopolize commercial activities for profit, generating more revenue than the state's regular taxes. Because of this, King Zhao's household had abundant wealth; however, all these riches were eventually spent on his concubines and sons. Pengzu took Nao Ji, the favored concubine of King Yiwang of Jiangdu who had been seduced and defiled by Wang Jian, as his own concubine and loved her very much.

## 18

Pengzu did not enjoy building palaces or engaging in superstitious rituals; he preferred to deal with bureaucratic affairs. He submitted a memorial expressing his wish to oversee the suppression of bandits and thieves within the state. He often traveled at night with foot soldiers, patrolling the area around Handan. All envoys and travelers passing through feared Pengzu's treachery and dared not stay in Handan.

## 19

His crown prince Dan had an affair with his daughter and paternal aunt, and fell out with his guest Jiang Chong. Jiang Chong reported on Dan's misconduct, and as a result, Dan was deposed. Zhao then appointed a new crown prince.

## 20

Zhongshan Jingwang Sheng was appointed King of Zhongshan as a royal prince in the third year before Emperor Xiao Jing's reign began. In the fourteenth year, Emperor Xiao Jing died. Sheng was a man who loved wine and women, and had over 120 descendants and relatives. He often quarreled with his elder brother, King Zhao, saying: "My elder brother is a king but monopolizes the affairs of officials and governs alone. A king should indulge in music, beauty, and pleasures every day." King Zhao also criticized him, saying: "The King of Zhongshan merely indulges in debauchery every day and does not assist the Son of Heaven in governing and comforting the people. How can he be called a vassal minister!"

## 21

After reigning for forty-two years, Sheng died; his son Aiwang Chang succeeded him as ruler. He died in the first year of his reign, and his son Kunchi succeeded him as King of Zhongshan.

## 22

The above two kings were both sons of Madam Jia.

## 23

Changsha Dingwang Fa's mother, Tang Ji, was originally a servant to Cheng Ji. Emperor Jing summoned Cheng Ji, but Cheng Ji had her period and did not wish to attend; instead, she dressed her servant Tang Er up and sent her in at night. The emperor was drunk and unaware, mistook her for Cheng Ji and had relations with her, so she became pregnant. Later he realized that it was not Cheng Ji. When the child was born, he was therefore named Fa. Fa was appointed King of Changsha as a royal prince in the second year before Emperor Xiao Jing's reign. Because his mother was lowly and not favored, he ruled over a humble, poor, and damp state.

## 24

After reigning for twenty-seven years, Fa died; his son Kangwang Yong succeeded him as ruler. In the twenty-eighth year of his reign, Yong died; his son Fuju succeeded him as King of Changsha.

## 25

The above one king was a son of Tang Ji. Guangchuan Huwang Yue was appointed King of Guangchuan as a royal prince in the middle second year of Emperor Xiao Jing's reign.

## 26

He died in his twelfth year, and his son Qi succeeded him as king. Qi had a favored minister named Sang Ju. Later, when Qi committed crimes and wanted to execute Sang Ju, Sang Ju fled; the king then captured his clan. Sang Ju resented King Qi and therefore submitted a petition accusing King Qi of committing incest with his full sibling. From then on, King Qi repeatedly submitted petitions to report and accuse the Han officials such as the ministers and favored courtiers like Suozhong.

## 27

Jiaodong Kangwang Ji was appointed King of Jiaodong as a royal prince in the middle of the second year of Emperor Xiao Jing's reign. He died in his twenty-eighth year. When the King of Huainan plotted rebellion, Ji faintly heard about it and secretly built siege towers, prepared arrows and weapons for defense, waiting for the uprising by the King of Huainan. When officials investigated the matter of Huainan's rebellion, Ji was implicated in their accusations. Ji was the most closely related to the emperor; he was deeply distressed, fell ill and died. He did not dare to appoint a successor, so the emperor learned of it. Ji had an eldest son named Xian, whose mother was not favored; his younger son was named Qing, and his mother was loved and favored. Ji often wanted to appoint him as heir, but since this would be out of order, he refrained from doing so due to past mistakes, and thus said nothing further. The emperor took pity on the situation and therefore appointed Xian as King of Jiaodong to succeed the Kangwang line, while granting Qing a fief in the former Hengshan region, making him King of Liuan.

## 28

King Jiaodong Xian reigned for fourteen years before dying and was posthumously honored as Aiwang (the "Ailing King"). His son Qing became king.

## 29

Liuanwang Qing was appointed King of Liuan in the second year of Yuanshou as a son of King Jiaodong Kangwang.

## 30

Qinghe Aiwang Cheng was appointed King of Qinghe in the third year of Emperor Xiao Jing's middle reign as a royal prince. He died after reigning for twelve years, and since he had no heir, the state was abolished; its territory was incorporated into Han as Qinghe Commandery.

## 31

Changshan Xianwang Shun was appointed King of Changshan in the fifth year of Emperor Xiao Jing's middle period as a royal prince. Shun was the most favored, being the emperor's youngest son; he was arrogant and indolent, often committing lewd acts and repeatedly violating laws. The emperor usually pardoned him leniently. After reigning for thirty-two years, Shun died; the crown prince Bo succeeded him as king.

## 32

Initially, King Xianwang Shun had a son named Zhuo born to one of his less-favored concubines. Zhuo was not favored by the king because his mother lacked favor. The queen, Xiu, gave birth to Crown Prince Bo. King Shun had many concubines; the favored ones bore sons Ping and Shang, while the queen rarely received his favor. When King Xianwang fell seriously ill, the favored concubines often attended to his illness. Therefore, the queen, out of jealousy, did not frequently attend him and would return to her quarters instead. When physicians presented medicine, Crown Prince Bo did not personally taste the medicine nor stay overnight to attend his father's illness. After the king died, the queen and crown prince finally arrived. King Xianwang had never regarded his eldest son Zhuo as a person of merit, and upon his death, he did not leave him any property. Some palace attendants advised the crown prince and queen to have all their sons share the wealth with the eldest son Zhuo, but the crown prince and queen refused. After Crown Prince Bo succeeded as king, he still did not show any care or support for Zhuo. Zhuo resented the queen and the crown prince. When Han envoys inspected the mourning rites for King Xianwang, Zhuo reported that during his father's illness, the queen and Crown Prince Bo had not attended to him. After the king's death, they left their residence six days later. Crown Prince Bo secretly committed adultery, drank alcohol, played games of chance, struck a zhu (a type of musical instrument), rode with women in carriages through the city, circled the walls, passed by markets, and even entered the prison to visit prisoners. The Son of Heaven sent Da Xing Qian to investigate the queen and question King Bo, requesting that they arrest those involved in adultery with Prince Bo along with all evidence. The king concealed them again. The officials urgently sought to capture Prince Bo, beating and torturing those sent after him, while arbitrarily releasing prisoners suspected by the Han court. The responsible officials requested that Queen Xiu of King Xianwang and King Bo be executed. The emperor considered that Xiu had always behaved improperly, leading Zhuo to accuse her of crimes, and that Bo lacked good mentors; thus, he could not bear to execute them. The responsible officials requested the queen Xiu be deposed and that King Bo along with his family be relocated to Fangling. The emperor approved this request.

## 33

After ruling for several months, Bo was moved to Fangling, and the state ceased to exist. More than a month later, the Son of Heaven, moved by their close relationship, issued an edict to the responsible officials: "The King Xianwang of Changshan died young. His queen and concubines were not harmonious; legitimate and illegitimate sons falsely accused each other in disputes, leading them into injustice and causing the state's destruction. I deeply pity this situation. Therefore, grant King Xianwang's son Ping a fief of thirty thousand households as King of Zhenping; and grant his son Shang a fief of thirty thousand households, making him the King of Sishui."

## 34

Zhenpingwang Ping was appointed as King of Zhenping in the fourth year of Yuandeng, as a son of Changshan Xianwang.

## 35

Sishui Siwang Shang was appointed King of Sishui in the fourth year of Yuanding as a son of Changshan Xian Wang. He died after eleven years, and his son Aiwang Anshi succeeded him as king. After eleven years of reign, he died without a male heir. The emperor pitied the extinction of the Sishui royal line and therefore appointed He, Anshi's younger brother, as King of Sishui.

## 36

The above four kings were all sons of Wang Fu Ren Erxu. Later, the Han dynasty further granted fiefs to his collateral descendants as rulers of two states: Liuanwang and Sishuiwang. In total, Erxu's descendants have become six kings up to the present day.

## 37

Grand Historian said: During Emperor Gaozu's time, all the feudal lords collected taxes and had the authority to appoint officials below the rank of Neishi (Minister in Charge), but only Han was granted a Chancellor with a golden seal. The feudal lords could appoint their own Yushi, Tingwei Zheng, and Bohu, positions that were analogous to those of the Son of Heaven. After the rebellion by Wu and Chu, during the five generations of kings from the imperial clan, Han appointed officials at the rank of Erqianshi (a high-ranking official title), replacing "Chengxiang" with "Xiang," who bore silver seals. The feudal lords were allowed to collect only land rents and taxes, their authority being stripped away. Later on, some of the poorer feudal lords had to ride in ox-drawn carts.
