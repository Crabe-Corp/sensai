# Table of Years for Those Enfeoffed as Marquesses Since the Jianyuan Era

## 1

The Grand Historian said: The Xiongnu cut off peace and marriage alliances, and attacked the strategic passes. Minyue acted on its own to launch attacks, while Dongou requested surrender. The two barbarian groups launched joint invasions during the flourishing era of the Han dynasty; from this we can know that meritorious officials received enfeoffments equal to those of their ancestors. Why is that? Why is that? The Classic of Poetry and the Classic of Documents speak of the Three Dynasties, saying "They repelled the Rong and Di barbarians, and pacified Jingta." The Grand Historian said: Duke Huan of Qi crossed into Yan to attack the Shanrong; King Wuling with his small state of Zhao subdued the Chanyu. Duke Mu of Qin used Bai Li Xi to dominate the western 戎 barbarians, while rulers of Wu and Chu as feudal lords subjugated the Baiyue. How much more so, when the Middle Kingdom is unified under one rule and a sage emperor reigns above, combining virtue with martial prowess, sweeping across all directions, uniting within millions of people—could it be that peace prevails without any military campaigns along the borders? From then on, troops were dispatched northward to campaign against the powerful Hu and southward to punish the formidable Yue; generals and soldiers in turn received enfeoffments.

## 4

A later Confucian scholar,褚 Xiansheng, said: The Grand Historian recorded events up to the reign of Emperor Wu; therefore, I have compiled a record of meritorious officials enfeoffed as marquises since Emperor Zhao, arranging them on the left side, so that future scholars with an interest in history may review their successes and failures, their rise and fall across generations, and thus take warnings for themselves. The virtuous men of their time exercised discretion and adapted to changing circumstances, measured the times and applied appropriate measures, sought opportunities to take action, thereby establishing merit, acquiring fiefdoms, being enfeoffed as marquesses, and earning fame in their own age—was this not a glorious achievement! Observing their ways of maintaining success and preserving achievements, they were all unyielding and arrogant, vying for power, fond of boasting about their reputations, knowing only how to advance but not how to retreat; in the end, they perished by losing their lives and destroying their states. They gained power through three achievements, yet lost it within a single lifetime; they could not pass on their accomplishments to future generations or allow benevolence and virtue to flow down to descendants—how sorrowful is that! Lord Longluo once served as the former general. He was upright and kind in his dealings with society, dignified, sincere, and cautious; he did not involve himself in political affairs, and was modest and loving toward others. His ancestors rose to prominence during the time of the six ministers of Jin. Since the time of hereditary lords ruling their fiefdoms, they became kings and marquesses; their descendants have succeeded one another without interruption. Spanning many years across generations until now, covering more than a hundred years—how could they possibly be compared to meritorious officials who lost everything within a single lifetime? How tragic! Future generations should take this as a warning!
