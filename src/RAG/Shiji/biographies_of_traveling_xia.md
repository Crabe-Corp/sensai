# Biographies of Traveling Xia

## 1

Hanzi said: "The Ru scholars confuse law with literature, while xia violate prohibitions through martial action." Both are criticized, yet learned men are often praised in the world. As for those who attain positions of prime minister, high ministers, and senior officials through their skills, assisting and supporting their sovereigns in the current age, with both achievements and reputation recorded in the Spring and Autumn Annals, there is certainly nothing to say about them. As for Ji Ci and Yuan Xian, they were common people from the lanes and alleys. They read books and cherished the virtue of solitary virtuous men; out of righteousness, they did not carelessly align themselves with their contemporaries, who in turn laughed at them. Therefore, Ji Ci and Yuan Xian spent their entire lives in empty rooms with thatched doors, wearing coarse clothes and eating simple food without complaint. More than four hundred years after they died, their disciples continued to remember them tirelessly. Now, the xia of today, although their conduct may deviate from righteousness, always speak with credibility and act decisively. Once they make a promise, they fulfill it sincerely; they do not cherish their lives but rush to assist scholars in distress, risking life and death without hesitation. Yet they do not boast about their abilities or shamelessly proclaim their virtues—there is indeed much to commend them.

## 2

Moreover, times of urgency are something everyone occasionally encounters. Grand Historian said: In the past, Yu Shun was in distress at a well and granary; Yi Yin carried a cauldron and 俎 (a ritual vessel); Fu Yue hid at Fu Xian; Lü Shang suffered hardship at Ji Jin; Yiwu was shackled; Bai Li fed oxen; Zhong Ni feared Kuang, and his face showed hunger in Chen and Cai. These were all learned men considered to be virtuous and benevolent, yet they still suffered such misfortunes. How much more so for those of average ability who find themselves in the later stages of a chaotic age? How can their sufferings possibly be fully recounted!

## 3

A vulgar person once said: "Who truly knows benevolence and righteousness? Those who have enjoyed their benefits are considered virtuous." Therefore, Boyi despised the Zhou dynasty and starved to death on Shouyang Mountain, yet Wenwang and Wuwang did not debase their kingship because of this. Zhi Qiao was violent and cruel, yet his followers endlessly recited righteousness. Judging from this, "He who steals a hook is punished; he who steals a country becomes a marquis. In the gate of a marquis, benevolence and righteousness are preserved"—this is not an empty saying.

## 4

Now, some narrow-minded scholars cling to a tiny bit of righteousness and remain isolated from the world for long periods. How could this be better than lowering one's standards to conform with common society, drifting along with the times in order to gain honor and reputation! Yet commoners, when setting their principles of giving and receiving, making promises they keep, will recite righteousness for a thousand miles and face death without regard for the world. In this, there is indeed something admirable; it is not merely arbitrary or haphazard. Therefore, when a scholar finds himself in dire straits and entrusts his fate to others, is this not what people call the virtue of a virtuous hero? If indeed the xia from the local lanes and alleys were to be compared with Ji Ci and Yuan Xian in terms of authority, strength, and contributions to their age, they could not even be mentioned on the same day. In summary, if one's achievements are evident and words trustworthy, how can the righteousness of xia guests possibly be considered insignificant!

## 5

Ancient commoner xia have long been lost to history. In recent times, figures such as Yanling, Mengchang, Chunshen, Pingyuan, and Xinling were all relatives of kings. Relying on the wealth and power of high-ranking ministers who held fiefs, they attracted virtuous men from around the world and gained fame among feudal lords; one cannot say they were not virtuous. For example, shouting with the wind does not make your voice any louder, but its effect is amplified by the force of the wind. As for xia from the lanes and alleys, those who cultivate their conduct and polish their reputation, whose fame spreads throughout the world, are all called virtuous—this is truly difficult. Yet both Ru and Mo schools of thought exclude and do not record them. Before the Qin dynasty, xia among commoners were lost to obscurity; I deeply regret this. From what I have heard, since the rise of the Han dynasty there were figures such as Zhu Jia, Tian Zhong, Wang Gong, Ju Meng, and Guo Jie. Although they occasionally resisted the legal nets of their time, in terms of private righteousness, integrity, modesty, and yielding, there was much to commend them. Their reputations were not falsely established, nor did scholars falsely attach themselves to them. As for those who formed factions and powerful clans, colluded in cliques, used wealth to exploit the poor, committed violent acts against the weak, indulged their desires for personal satisfaction—such xia also despised them. I lament the failure of the vulgar world to understand their intentions, and how they recklessly grouped Zhu Jia, Guo Jie, and others together with violent ruffians as if they were of the same kind, laughing at them jointly.

## 6

Lu Zhu Jia was a contemporary of Emperor Gaozu. The people of Lu were all taught by Confucianism, yet Zhu Jia became famous for his xia conduct. He sheltered and saved hundreds of virtuous men; the number of ordinary people he aided was too numerous to count. Yet he never boasted about his abilities or took pride in his virtue; for all the kindnesses he had shown, he only feared they would be discovered. When helping those in need, he always began with the poor and lowly. His family had no surplus wealth; his clothes were not complete or colorful, his food was simple without variety, and the carriage he rode did not exceed a cart pulled by two oxen. He devoted himself entirely to others' urgent needs, even more so than his own private affairs. In secret, he rescued General Ji Bu from a dire situation; when Ji Bu later attained high rank and honor, Zhu Jia never met with him for the rest of his life. From Guan Pass to the east, no one did not stretch their necks in longing and wish to associate with him.

## 7

Chu Tian Zhong was known for his xia conduct; he loved swordsmanship and regarded Zhu Jia as a father figure, believing that his own conduct did not match Zhu Jia's. After Tian Zhong died, Luoyang had Ju Meng. The people of Zhou relied on commerce as their foundation, while Ju Meng became prominent among feudal lords through his role and xia conduct. During the rebellion of Wu and Chu, Lord Tao Hou was serving as Taiwei. As he traveled by imperial envoy carriage toward Henan, upon obtaining Ju Meng, he rejoiced and said: "Wu and Chu have launched a major uprising without seeking out Ju Meng; I now know they are incapable of achieving anything." When the world was in turmoil, to obtain someone like Ju Meng meant for a prime minister as if he had gained an entire rival state. Ju Meng's conduct was largely similar to Zhu Jia, but he loved gambling and often engaged in games of chance for many years. Yet when Ju Meng's mother died, people from afar came to mourn her, with carriages numbering as many as a thousand. When Ju Meng died, his family had no remaining wealth exceeding ten gold pieces. Meanwhile, Wang Meng of Fuli was also renowned for his xia conduct in the region between the Jiang and Huai rivers.

## 8

At that time, Jinnan Jian Shi and Chen Zhouyong were also known for their boldness. Emperor Jing heard of them and sent envoys to execute all such individuals. Afterward, figures like Bai of Daidai, Han Wupi of Liang, Xue Xiong of Yangzhi, and Han Ru of Shan again emerged in succession.

## 9

Guo Jie was a native of Zhi; his courtesy name was Wengbo, and he was the granddaughter's son of Xu Fu, an expert in physiognomy. Guo Jie's father had been involved in xia conduct and was executed during the reign of Emperor Xiaowen. Guo Jie was short but strong-willed, and did not drink alcohol. In his youth, he was secretly cruel; whenever something displeased him, he personally killed many people. He used his body to avenge friends, harbored fugitives, and committed crimes such as robbery and assault. He even cast coins and dug up graves—such deeds were too numerous to count.  By sheer luck, whenever he was in dire straits, he often escaped as if encountering a pardon. As Guo Jie grew older, he reformed his conduct and became frugal; he repaid grievances with virtue, giving generously while expecting little in return. Yet he took even greater pride in being a xia. After saving someone's life, he did not boast of his achievements; however, the resentment and cruelty that had taken root in his heart eventually erupted over a trivial grievance. Young men admired his conduct and would often take revenge on his behalf without letting him know. Guo Jie's nephew took advantage of Guo Jie's influence; while drinking with others, he forced someone to chew something. When the person was unable to bear it, they were forcibly made to drink until drunk. The man became angry, drew a sword and killed Guo Jie's nephew before fleeing. Guo Jie's sister was furious and said: "Because of Wengbo's righteousness, someone killed my son, yet the criminal cannot be found." She left her son's body on the road without burial, intending to humiliate Guo Jie. Guo Jie sent someone secretly to find out where the criminal was hiding. The criminal, cornered and desperate, turned himself in and fully confessed the truth to Guo Jie. Guo Jie said: "You killed him; it was indeed justified. My nephew was at fault." He then released the criminal, blamed his own nephew, and retrieved the body to give him a proper burial. When the public heard of this, they all praised Guo Jie's righteousness and became even more attached to him.

## 10

Whenever Guo Jie went out or returned home, people would avoid him. One man alone sat with his legs spread wide and looked at him defiantly; Guo Jie sent someone to inquire about the man's name and surname. Guo Jie's guest wanted to kill him. Guo Jie said: "To live in the same town and not be shown respect means my virtue is insufficient; what crime has he committed!" He secretly instructed the local officials: "This man is someone I urgently need to protect; when it's time for his conscription duty, let him go free." Each time he was due for conscription service, the official passed by several times without summoning him. The man found this strange and asked why; it turned out that Guo Jie had arranged for his release. The defiant man then stripped to the waist and apologized for his offense. Young men hearing of this became even more admiring of Guo Jie's conduct.

## 11

In Luoyang, there were people with a grudge against each other; more than ten virtuous and powerful men in the town tried to mediate between them, but they would not listen. A guest then went to see Guo Jie. At night, Guo Jie met with the family of his enemy, and they eventually yielded to Guo Jie's persuasion. Guo Jie then said to the family: "I have heard that many of the virtuous men in Luoyang are here, yet they were unable to resolve this matter. Now that you have been fortunate enough to listen to me, how could I possibly take away the authority of a virtuous gentleman from your own town by coming from another county!" He then left at night without informing anyone, saying: "Since it is of no use, (wait for me) let me go first. I will have the powerful men in Luoyang mediate between you, and only then will you listen."

## 12

Guo Jie maintained a respectful demeanor and dared not ride in a carriage when entering the court of his native county. When he traveled to neighboring commanderies and states, if someone asked him to handle an affair for them, and if the matter could be resolved, he would do so. If it was impossible to resolve, he would still satisfy their intentions as much as possible before daring to accept food or drink from them. Because of this, the public held him in great respect and competed to serve him. Youth from his native town and virtuous, powerful men from nearby counties would often visit his door in the middle of the night, with more than ten carriages arriving at a time, requesting to be housed as guests in Guo Jie's residence.

## 13

When the authorities ordered the relocation of wealthy and powerful families to Maoling, Guo Jie's family was poor and did not meet the property requirements. The officials were afraid and dared not fail to relocate them anyway. General Wei spoke on their behalf, saying: "The Guo Jie family is poor and does not meet the criteria for relocation." The emperor said: "A commoner's influence has reached the point of making a general speak on his behalf; this family is not poor." Thus, Guo Jie's household was relocated. The public who came to see him off gave more than ten thousand in gifts. Yang Jizhu of Zhi had a son who served as a county official and was responsible for relocating Guo Jie's family. Guo Jie's nephew beheaded the Yang family official. Because of this, the Yang and Guo families became enemies.

## 14

When Guo Jie entered Guan Pass, both known and unknown virtuous men in Guanzhong heard of his reputation and competed to befriend him joyfully. Guo Jie was small in stature, did not drink alcohol, and when he went out, never rode a horse. He then killed Yang Jizhu. When the family of Yang Jizhu submitted a petition, someone else killed them at the palace gates. Upon hearing this, the emperor ordered officials to arrest Guo Jie. Guo Jie fled and left his mother and family in Xiayang, going himself to Linjin. Linjin's official Jishao Gong had never known Guo Jie; Guo pretended to be someone else and requested to leave the pass. After Jishao Gong allowed Guo Jie to leave, Guo moved into Taiyuan and wherever he went, he would inform the household owners of his presence. The officials pursued him, tracking him back to Jishao Gong. Jishao Gong committed suicide, and the case was dropped. After a long time, Guo Jie was finally captured. When officials thoroughly investigated the crimes he had committed, they found that all those killed by Guo Jie were before a general pardon. A Confucian scholar from Zhi, who was seated serving the envoy, heard a guest praising Guo Jie and said: "Guo Jie specialized in committing crimes against public law; how can he be called virtuous!" Upon hearing this, one of Guo Jie's followers killed the scholar and cut out his tongue. The officials held Guo Jie responsible for this, but in fact he did not know who had committed the killing. The killer was never found either; no one knew who it was. The officials reported to the emperor that Guo Jie was innocent of the crime. The Grand Censor Gongsun Hong argued: "Guo Jie, as a commoner, acted with the authority of a xia and committed murder over a trivial grievance. Even though Guo did not know about it, this crime is more serious than if he had killed them himself." He should be judged as committing great treason and acting without morality." Thus, the entire Guo Jie clan, including his grandfather Gongsuo Bo, was exterminated.

## 15

After this incident, there were many who became xia, but they were arrogant and of no real significance to be counted. However, in Guanzhong Chang'an there was Fan Zhongzi; Huaili had Zhao Wangsun; Changling had Gao Gongzi; Xhe had Guo Gongzhong; Taiyuan had Lu Gongru; Linhuai had Er Zhangqing; and Dongyang had Tian Junru. Although they were xia, they behaved with humility and exhibited the gentlemanly virtue of yielding gracefully. As for the Yao family of the northern route, the various Du families of the western route, Chou Jing of the southern route, Zhao Ta and Yu Gongzi of the eastern route, and followers like Zhao Diao of Nanyang—these were merely common thieves living among the people. How worthy are they to be mentioned! These were precisely the disgraceful figures that Zhu Jia had spoken against earlier.

## 16

Grand Historian said: "When I observed Guo Jie, his appearance was not even average, and his words were not worth recording." Yet people throughout the world—whether virtuous or unvirtuous, whether they knew him or not—all admired his reputation, and those who spoke of xia all cited him as a name to follow. A proverb says: "A person's appearance may be humble, but can their reputation ever truly fade?" Alas! How regrettable!
