# Chaoxian Liezhuan

## 1

Chaoxian Wang Manzhe was originally a native of Yan. During the early reign of the state of Yan, it had previously exercised some control over Jinpan and Chaoxian, appointing officials there and building defensive barriers. After the Qin dynasty destroyed Yan, these areas came under the jurisdiction of Liaodong's outer frontier. When the Han dynasty rose to power, because these areas were distant and difficult to defend, they restored the old fortifications of Liaodong, extending them up to the Pian River as a boundary, placing them under Yan. When the Yan king Lu Wan rebelled and fled into the Xiongnu, Manzhe went into hiding and gathered a band of more than a thousand followers. He tied his hair in a topknot like the local barbarians and led them eastward beyond the frontier, crossing the Pian River to settle in the abandoned territory between the upper and lower fortifications from the Qin dynasty. Gradually, he subjugated the Jinpan, Chaoxian barbarians, as well as former Yan and Qi rebels who had fled, establishing himself as their ruler with his capital at Wangxian.

## 2

During the reign of Emperor Xiaohui and Empress Dowager Gao, when the empire had just been pacified, the governor of Liaodong made an agreement with Manzhe to serve as a vassal ruler. In return, Manzhe was to protect the frontier from the barbarians beyond the pass and prevent them from raiding the borders; any chieftains of the barbarian tribes who wished to pay homage to the Son of Heaven should not be prevented. This was reported to the throne, and the emperor approved it. As a result, Manzhe used his military power and wealth to conquer nearby small towns by force or persuasion; Jinpan and Lintun all submitted to him in allegiance, covering an area of several thousand li.

## 3

The position was passed down from father to son until Sun Youqu, who attracted even more Han defectors and had never paid homage in person; the various states near Jinpan wishing to submit petitions or pay homage to the Son of Heaven were also obstructed by Youqu and could not proceed. In the second year of Yuangfeng, the Han dynasty sent She He to admonish Youqu; he ultimately refused to obey the imperial edict. She He went as far as the frontier, arriving at the banks of the Pian River. There he ordered his driver to kill Chang, a minor Korean general who had sent assassins after him. Then She He crossed the river and rushed back through the pass, returning to report to the Son of Heaven, saying, "I have killed a Chaoxian general." The emperor, pleased by She He's reputation for valor, did not investigate the matter further and appointed him as Commandant of the Eastern Division of Liaodong. Chaoxian resented She He and sent troops to launch a surprise attack, killing him.

## 4

The Son of Heaven recruited convicts to campaign against Chaoxian. That autumn, the emperor dispatched Louchuan General Yang Pu from Qi to sail across the Bohai Sea; with an army of 50,000 men. At the same time, General Zuo Jiangjun Xun Zhi marched out from Liaodong: both forces were sent to campaign against Youqu. Youqu raised troops and resisted at a strategic pass. The commander of the left general's forces, Zuo Jiangjun Zu Zhengduo, led the Liaodong troops forward first but was defeated and scattered. Duomanyu fled back in retreat and was executed for violating military law. The Louchuan General, leading 7,000 troops from Qi, arrived at Wangxian first. Youqu's forces defended the city and observed that the Louchuan army was small in number. They then sallied out to attack it, defeating and scattering the Louchuan troops, who fled in disarray. General Yang Pu lost his men and took refuge in the mountains for more than ten days, gradually gathering up scattered soldiers to regroup. The left general attacked the Chaoxian forces stationed west of the Pian River but failed to break through or advance further.

## 5

As the emperor saw that neither general had achieved success, he sent Wei Shan to take advantage of the military presence and go to Youqu for negotiations. Youqu bowed deeply before the envoy in apology, saying: "I wish to surrender, but I fear both generals may falsely kill me; now that I see your official credentials of authority, I will submit and surrender." He sent his crown prince to express gratitude, presenting 5,000 horses as well as provisions for the army. More than 10,000 people accompanied the prince, armed with weapons and preparing to cross the Pian River. The envoy and General Zuo suspected treachery, believing that since the crown prince had already pledged surrender, he should have been ordered not to carry arms. The crown prince also suspected that the envoy and General Zuo would falsely kill him, so he did not cross the Pian River but instead withdrew back. Wei Shan returned to report this to the Son of Heaven; the emperor executed Wei Shan.

## 6

The left general defeated the Chaoxian army on the western bank of the Pian River, advanced forward, and reached the city walls, encircling its northwest side. The Louchuan forces also arrived to join him, taking position south of the city. Youqu then resolutely defended the city, and for several months it could not be captured.

## 7

General Zuo had long served as an imperial attendant and was favored by the emperor; he commanded troops from Yan and Dai, who were fierce warriors. Riding on a string of victories, his army grew arrogant. The Louchuan general led Qi soldiers into the sea; naturally, many had already perished or been lost in battle; after an earlier defeat at the hands of Youqu, many soldiers had been disgraced and lost their lives. The remaining troops were frightened, and the general felt ashamed; thus, when encircling Youqu, he often adopted a conciliatory approach. General Zuo launched an urgent attack, and the Chaoxian ministers secretly sent envoys to negotiate a private surrender with Louchuan. Messages were exchanged back and forth, but no final decision had yet been made. General Zuo repeatedly set dates for joint battles with the Louchuan forces, but the Louchuan commander wished to quickly fulfill his agreement and did not show up; General Zuo also sent envoys seeking a secret arrangement to surrender Chaoxian, but the Chaoxian refused and instead secretly leaned toward Louchuan: for this reason, the two generals were unable to cooperate effectively. General Zuo suspected that Louchuan had previously committed a military offense by losing his troops, and now was secretly friendly with Chaoxian while refusing to surrender. He doubted that Louchuan might have an ulterior plan of betrayal and dared not act openly. The Son of Heaven said that the generals were incompetent, and had previously sent Wei Shan to urge Youqu's surrender. Youqu sent his crown prince as a gesture, but Wei Shan failed to make a decisive judgment and miscalculated with General Zuo, ultimately causing the agreement to collapse. Now that these two generals are besieging the city but acting inconsistently, this is why a decision has been delayed for so long. The emperor sent Gongsun Sui, the governor of Jinan, to go and correct the situation, granting him authority to act as he saw fit. When Gongsun Sui arrived, General Zuo said: "Chaoxian should have surrendered long ago; the reason it has not is because of certain circumstances." He explained that the Louchuan forces had repeatedly failed to meet as scheduled and told Sui in detail what he had long suspected, saying: "If we do not act now like this, I fear it will cause great harm. It is not only a problem with Louchuan; they may also join forces with Chaoxian to destroy our army." Sui also agreed with this view, and using his imperial credentials summoned the Louchuan general to General Zuo's camp for a military conference. He then ordered General Zuo's subordinates to arrest the Louchuan general, seize his troops, and report back to the Son of Heaven. The Son of Heaven executed Gongsun Sui.

## 8

After General Zuo had combined both armies, he immediately launched a swift attack on Chaoxian. Chaoxian's prime minister Lu Ren, prime minister Han Yin, Nixi prime minister Can, and General Wang Yi discussed together: "Initially we wanted to surrender to Louchuan, but now that Louchuan has been captured, only General Zuo remains in command. The fighting is growing more urgent, and I fear we cannot hold out much longer; the king also refuses to surrender." Han Yin, Wang Yi, and Lu Ren all fled and surrendered to the Han dynasty. Lu Ren died along the way. In summer of the third year of Yuangfeng, Nixi prime minister Can sent men to kill King Youqu of Chaoxian and surrender to Han. The city of Wangxian had not yet been captured, so Youqu's former minister Chengsi rebelled again and attacked Han officials. General Zuo sent Youqu's son Changjiang and the son of Lu Ren, named Zui, to pacify the people with orders to execute Chengsi. Thus, Chaoxian was finally subdued and divided into four commanderies. Can was enfeoffed as Hou of Huqing, Yin as Hou of Diju, Yi as Hou of Pingzhou, and Changjiang as Hou of Ji. Zui was enfeoffed as the Hou of Wenyang because his father died in service and he had rendered considerable merit.

## 9

General Zuo was summoned to court, but due to quarrels over credit, jealousy, and miscalculations in strategy, he was executed in the public square. The Louchuan general was also punished for having arrived at Liekou and, while waiting for General Zuo to arrive,擅自 advanced without orders. This led to heavy losses among his troops; he should have been executed but instead paid a ransom to be reduced to commoner status.

## 10

The Grand Historian said: Youqu relied on the strength of his fortified position, and as a result, his state was destroyed and its ancestral sacrifices ceased. She He falsely claimed credit for killing an enemy general, becoming the instigator of military action. The Louchuan commander was narrow-minded and thus could not escape blame when difficulties arose. Regretting the loss of Panyu, he became instead suspected by others. Xun Zhi quarreled over credit and was executed along with Gongsun Sui. Both armies suffered disgrace, and no generals or commanders were enfeoffed as marquises.
