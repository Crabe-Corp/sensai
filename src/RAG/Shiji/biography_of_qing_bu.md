# Biography of Qing Bu

## 1

Qing Bu was a native of Liu, and his surname was Ying. During the Qin dynasty he was an ordinary commoner. When young, a guest who examined his physiognomy said: "You will be subjected to corporal punishment and then become king." When he grew up, he was punished with facial branding for breaking the law. Bu smiled happily and said; "People have predicted that I will be subjected to corporal punishment before becoming a king, is this about to come true?" Those who heard of it laughed at him mockingly. Bu had already been sentenced to penal servitude at Lishan. There were dozens of thousands of convicts at Lishan, and Bu formed connections with the leaders and prominent figures among them. He then led his fellow convicts and escaped into the rivers and lakes to become a bandit leader.

## 2

When Chen Sheng rose in rebellion, Bu went to see Fan Jun and joined forces with him in rebelling against the Qin. They gathered several thousand soldiers. Fan Jun gave his daughter in marriage to Bu. When Zhang Han destroyed Chen Sheng's forces and defeated the army of Lü Chen, Bu led his troops north to attack Qin's left and right divisions, defeating them at Qingbo, then leading his troops eastward. Hearing that Xiang Liang had pacified Jiangdong and Huiji, Bu crossed the river and moved westward. Chen Ying, because the Xiang clan had long served as generals of the state of Chu, handed over his troops to Xiang Liang and crossed south of the Huai River. Ying Bu and Pu Jiangjun also submitted their forces to Xiang Liang.

## 3

Xiang Liang crossed the Huai River westward, attacking Jing Ju and Qin Jia et al., and Bu was often at the forefront of these campaigns. When Xiang Liang arrived at Xue, he heard that King Chen had definitely died, and so they enthroned the Marquis of Huai. Xiang Liang was given the title Wuxin Jun, and Ying Bu was granted the title Dangyang Jun. After Xiang Liang's defeat and death at Dingtao, King Huai moved the capital to Pengcheng, and all the generals, including Ying Bu, also gathered for protection in Pengcheng. At that time, Qin forces were urgently besieging Zhao, which repeatedly sent envoys to request reinforcements. King Huai appointed Song Yi as supreme general, Fan Zeng as the lowest-ranking general, Xiang Ji as deputy general, Ying Bu and Pu Jiangjun both as generals, all subordinate to Song Yi, heading north to rescue Zhao. When Xiang Ji killed Song Yi by the river, King Huai therefore appointed Ji as supreme general, and all the other generals came under Xiang Ji's command. Xiang Ji ordered Bu to cross the river first and attack Qin forces. Bu achieved several victories, so Ji led all his troops across the river to follow him, thus defeating the Qin army and causing Zhang Han et al. to surrender. The Chu forces were often victorious, with their achievements surpassing those of all the feudal lords. All the feudal lord's armies submitted to Chu because Bu frequently defeated larger forces with fewer troops.

## 4

When Xiang Ji led his army westward to Xin'an, he again ordered Bu and others to attack at night and massacre more than 200,000 Qin soldiers under Zhang Han in a pit. When they reached the Guan Pass, they could not enter, so Xiang Ji again sent Bu and others ahead through a secret route to defeat the Qin forces stationed below the pass, thus enabling them to enter and reach Xianyang. Bu was often at the vanguard of the army. Xiang Wang enfeoffed the generals, and established Bu as King of Jiangxiu, with his capital in Liu.

## 5

In the fourth month of Han Year One, all the feudal lords withdrew from their positions under Xiang Wang and returned to their respective fiefdoms. The Xiang clan enthroned King Huai as Yi Di, moved the capital to Changsha, and secretly ordered Jiangxiu Wang Bu et al. to attack him on their way. In the eighth month, Bu sent his generals to attack Yi Di and killed him in Chenzhou Xian.

## 6

In the second year of Han, Qi Wang Tian Rong rebelled against Chu. Xiang Wang went to attack Qi and summoned troops from Jiangxiu. King Bu of Jiangxiu claimed illness and did not go in person, but sent a general with several thousand men instead. When Han defeated Chu at Pengcheng, Bu again pretended to be ill and did not assist Chu. Xiang Wang thus resented Bu and repeatedly sent envoys to scold and summon him, which made Bu increasingly fearful and unwilling to go. Xiang Wang was at that time preoccupied with worries to the north from Qi and Zhao, and concerns to the west from Han. The only one he could rely on was King Jiangxiu Bu, who also had many talents; thus, Xiang wanted to keep him close and use him, which is why he did not attack him.

## 7

In the third year of Han, King Han attacked Chu and engaged in a major battle at Pengcheng. The Han forces suffered defeat, withdrew from Liang territory, and reached Yu, where he said to his attendants: "Men like those are not worth considering for planning affairs concerning the empire." The court official Sui He stepped forward and said, "I do not understand what Your Majesty means." King Han said, "Who can be sent by me to Huainan to persuade its ruler to raise troops against Chu and keep Xiang Wang tied up in Qi for several months? Then I will have a hundred percent chance of securing the empire." Sui He said, "I shall go on this mission." He thus set out with twenty men and went to Huainan. Upon arrival, he was put under the charge of the Tai Zai, and after three days, he still could not meet with the ruler. Sui He thus persuaded the Tai Zai, saying: "The King's refusal to see me must be because he considers Chu strong and Han weak. This is precisely why I have been sent as an envoy." "If the King allows me to speak, and what I say proves correct, it will be exactly what Your Majesty wishes to hear; if my words are not valid, then let me and these twenty men be executed in the market of Huainan with an axe, to demonstrate that Your Majesty has betrayed Han and allied with Chu." The Tai Zai then conveyed this to the King, who received Sui He. Sui He said: "King Han has sent me to respectfully present a letter for Your Majesty. I secretly wonder why Your Majesty is so closely allied with Chu." King of Huainan said: "I have served him as a subject, facing northward." Sui He said: "Your Majesty and Xiang Wang are both listed as feudal lords, yet you serve him facing northward. You must believe Chu is strong enough to entrust your state to." "When Xiang Wang attacked Qi, he personally carried wooden boards and tools for building fortifications, leading the soldiers. Your Majesty should have mobilized all of Huainan's forces under your personal command to serve as the vanguard for Chu's army. Yet now you only sent four thousand men to assist Chu." "Is serving someone by facing north really supposed to be like this?" "When King Han fought at Pengcheng, Xiang Wang had not yet left Qi. Your Majesty should have stirred up the forces of Huainan to cross the Huai River and joined in battle day and night beneath Pengcheng. With a force of ten thousand men under your command, not a single soldier crossed the river; instead, you sat idly by watching who would win." "Is entrusting one's state to another really supposed to be like this?" "Your Majesty holds only an empty title while facing Chu, yet wishes to place great trust in it. I secretly think this is not a wise course for Your Majesty." "Yet the reason why Your Majesty does not betray Chu is because you consider Han to be weak." "Although Chu's forces are strong, the whole world has branded them with a name of injustice because they broke their oaths and killed King Yi." "However, King Chu relies on his military victories to assert strength, while King Han has gathered the feudal lords and returned to defend Chenggao and Yingyang. He receives grain from Shu and Han rivers, digs deep trenches and fortifications, deploys troops to guard passes and blockades. When the Chu forces return, they are separated by Liang territory and have advanced hundreds of miles into enemy land. They wish to fight but cannot find an opportunity; when attacking cities, their strength is insufficient. The elderly and weak must transport provisions from a thousand li away." "When the Chu forces reach Yingyang and Chenggao, Han holds firm in defense without moving. Advancing brings no opportunity to attack; retreating offers no relief." "Therefore it is said that the strength of Chu's forces cannot be relied upon." "If Chu defeats Han, then the feudal lords will naturally feel threatened and come to each other's aid. "The strength of Chu is precisely sufficient to draw the armies of all under heaven against it." "Therefore, Chu is not as strong as Han; this situation is easy to see." "Now, Your Majesty does not ally with the invincible Han but entrusts yourself to the doomed Chu. I secretly find this puzzling for Your Majesty." "I do not claim that the forces of Huainan are sufficient to destroy Chu." "If Your Majesty raises troops and betrays Chu, Xiang Wang will certainly remain behind; if he stays for several months, Han's victory over the empire can be assured with absolute certainty." "I request to accompany Your Majesty, sword in hand, and return to Han. King Han will certainly grant you territory and enfeoff you as a king; even more so for Huainan, which must surely belong to Your Majesty." "Therefore, King Han respectfully sent me with this humble strategy; I hope Your Majesty will give it your attention." King of Huainan said: "I shall follow the order." He secretly agreed to betray Chu and side with Han, but did not dare reveal it yet.

## 8

Chu's envoys were still present, urgently demanding that Ying Bu raise troops, and they stayed in the guesthouse. Sui He entered directly, took a seat above that of the Chu envoys, and said: "King Jiangxiu has already submitted to Han. How can Chu possibly command him to raise troops?" Bu was startled. The envoys of Chu stood up. Sui He then persuaded Bu, saying: "The matter is already set in motion; you may as well kill the envoys of Chu now and prevent them from returning. Then hurry to Han and join forces quickly." Bu said: "As your envoy instructs, I shall raise my troops and attack them immediately." Thus he killed the envoys and raised his army to attack Chu. Chu sent Xiang Sheng and Long Ju to attack Huainan, while Xiang Wang remained behind to attack Xi Yi. After several months, Long Ju attacked Huainan and defeated Bu's army. Bu wanted to lead his troops to flee to Han but feared that the King of Chu would kill him, so he secretly traveled with Sui He and returned to Han.

## 9

When the King of Huainan arrived, King Han was sitting on a couch washing his feet. He summoned Bu to enter for an audience. Bu (was) extremely angry, regretted coming, and wanted to commit suicide. After leaving the palace and returning to his quarters, attendants provided him with food, drink, and court officials just as they did for King Han. Bu was once again overjoyed beyond expectation. At this point, he sent someone into Jiangxiu. Chu had already dispatched Xiang Bo to seize the forces of Jiangxiu and killed all of Bu's wife and children. Bu's envoys managed to gather some old friends and favored officials, leading several thousand men back to Han. Han further reinforced Bu's troops and marched north with him together, gathering forces up to Chenggao. In the seventh month of the fourth year, Bu was enfeoffed as King of Huainan and joined in attacking Xiang Ji.

## 10

In the fifth year of Han, Bu sent men into Jiangxiu and captured several counties. In the sixth year, Bu and Liu Jia entered Jiangxiu, lured in the Grand Marshal Zhou Yin, who then defected from Chu. They raised the forces of Jiangxiu to fight alongside Han against Chu, defeating them at Gaixia.

## 11

After Xiang Ji's death and with the empire pacified, King Han held a banquet. King Han downplayed Sui He's contribution, calling him an old-fashioned Confucian scholar and asking what use such a man was for the stability of the empire. Sui He knelt and said: "When Your Majesty led troops to attack Pengcheng, King Chu had not yet left Qi. With five thousand infantrymen and five thousand cavalry that Your Majesty dispatched, could you have captured Huainan?" The emperor said: "No." Sui He said: "Your Majesty sent me and twenty men to Huainan, where I succeeded in doing exactly as Your Majesty wished. Thus my contribution surpasses that of fifty thousand infantrymen and five thousand horsemen." "Yet now, Your Majesty calls me an old-fashioned Confucianist and asks what use such men are for the empire's peace. Why is that?" The emperor said: "I am just considering your merits." He then appointed Sui He as the Commander of the Guard Army. Bu was thus enfeoffed with a fief and became King of Huainan, with his capital at Liu. The commanderies of Jiangxiu, Lujiang, Hengshan, and Yuzhang all came under Bu's jurisdiction.

## 12

In the seventh year, he paid a court visit to Chen. In the eighth year, he made a court appearance in Luoyang. In the ninth year, he paid homage at Chang'an.

## 13

In the eleventh year, Empress Dowager Gao executed Duke Huaiyin, and Bu became fearful as a result. In summer, Han executed Prince Liang Peng Yue, minced him into meat paste, and distributed the paste among all the feudal lords. When it reached Huainan, King of Huainan was just hunting when he saw the meat paste and became greatly alarmed. He secretly ordered men to gather troops and await any sudden emergencies in neighboring commanderies.

## 14

One of Bu's favored concubines fell ill and requested to see a physician. The physician lived across the street from Zhong Dadafu Ben He, and the concubine visited him frequently. Considering himself as an Attendant-in-Ordinary, Ben He gave lavish gifts to the concubine and drank with her at the physician's home. When the concubine served King Bu, in a casual conversation she praised Ben He as an upright man. The king angrily asked: "How do you know this?" She gave a detailed account of the situation. The king suspected that she had been involved in disorderly conduct. Ben He became frightened and claimed to be ill. The king grew even angrier and wanted to arrest Ben He. Ben He spoke of a change in the situation and traveled by imperial carriage to Chang'an. Bu sent men to pursue him, but they did not catch up. When Ben He arrived, he reported the change in circumstances and said that Bu had signs of plotting rebellion; it was advisable to execute him before his plot could be carried out. The emperor read his report and discussed it with Xiao Xiangguo. Xiao Xiangguo said: "Bu should not have such intentions; I fear he is being falsely accused by his enemies." "I request to investigate Ben He and send men secretly to verify the King of Huainan's actions." King Bu of Huainan saw that Ben He had fled due to his crimes and submitted a report, so he was already suspicious about the secret affairs of his state being mentioned. When Han envoys came again and verified some things, Bu exterminated Ben He's family and raised troops to rebel. After news of the rebellion reached the emperor, he pardoned Ben He and appointed him as a general.

## 15

The emperor summoned his generals and asked: "Bu has rebelled; what should we do about it?" They all said: "Send troops to attack him; it's just a matter of burying this scoundrel." "What can he do!" Marquis of Ruyin, Teng Gong, summoned the former Lord Chancellor of Chu to ask him. The Lord Chancellor said: "He was bound to rebel." Teng Gong asked: "His Majesty granted him territory and made him a king, bestowed noble titles upon him and honored him highly. He was seated as the ruler of ten thousand chariots facing south. Why would he rebel?" The Lord Chancellor said: "Last year Peng Yue was executed, the previous year Han Xin was killed. These three men were of equal merit and status." "He suspected that calamity would befall him, so he rebelled." Teng Gong reported to the emperor: "I have a guest, Xue Gong, former Lord Chancellor of Chu. He is a man with strategic planning skills and can be consulted." The emperor then summoned him for an audience to ask Xue Gong. Xue Gong replied: "Bu's rebellion is not surprising." łoż If Bu acts according to the best strategy, Shandong will no longer belong to Han. If he follows a moderate plan, it is uncertain whether victory or defeat will follow; "If he adopts the worst strategy, Your Majesty can sleep soundly." The emperor asked: "What is meant by the best plan?" The Lord Chancellor replied: "If he takes Wu to the east, Chu to the west, and captures Lu together with Qi, issues proclamations to Yan and Zhao, firmly holds these territories, then Shandong will no longer be Han's." "What is meant by a moderate plan?" "It means taking Wu to the east and Chu to the west, capturing Wei together with Han, seizing the grain at Aogu, blocking the entrance of Chenggao Pass; then it is uncertain whether victory or defeat would follow." "What is meant by the worst plan?" "It means taking Wu to the east, Xiacai to the west, entrusting his treasures to Yue, and personally returning to Changsha. Your Majesty can then sleep soundly; Han will have no further troubles." The emperor asked: "Which of these plans do you think he will adopt?" The Lord Chancellor replied: "He will adopt the worst plan." The emperor asked: "Why would he discard the best and moderate plans in favor of the worst one?" The Lord Chancellor said: "Bu was originally a bandit of Lishan, who rose to become the ruler of ten thousand charioteers. All his actions are for himself alone; he does not consider the well-being of the people in future generations. Therefore, it is said that he will adopt the worst plan." The emperor said: "Well spoken." He bestowed a fief of one thousand households upon Xue Gong. The emperor then enfeoffed the crown prince, Zhang, as King of Huainan. The emperor thus mobilized troops and personally led them eastward to attack Bu.

## 16

When Bu first rebelled, he told his generals: "The emperor is old now and tired of warfare; he surely will not come in person." "He sent out his generals, but the only ones they feared were Duke Huaiyin and Peng Yue; now both are dead. The rest are not worth fearing." That is why he rebelled. As Xue Gong had predicted, Bu attacked Jing to the east; King Liu Jia of Jing fled and died at Fuling. He seized all his troops, crossed the Huai River, and attacked Chu. Chu mobilized its forces to fight him between Xu and Tong, dividing them into three armies in an attempt to support each other with unexpected maneuvers. Someone advised the Chu generals: "Bu is skilled at using troops and has long been feared by the people." "Moreover, according to military strategy, when feudal lords fight on their own territory, it is called 'scattered ground.'" "Now they are divided into three. If he defeats one of our armies, the rest will flee; how can they possibly come to each other's aid!" They did not listen. Bu indeed defeated one of their armies, and the other two fled in disarray.

## 17

He then moved westward and encountered the emperor's forces at Qixi, where they met near Chui. Bu's troops were highly disciplined and well-equipped; the emperor thus encamped at Yongcheng, observing that Bu's army was arranged like Xiang Ji's forces. The emperor disliked this sight. Facing each other across the battlefield, the emperor called out to Bu from a distance: "Why go to such lengths and rebel?" Bu replied: "I simply want to be an emperor." The emperor angrily cursed him, and a great battle ensued. Bu's army was defeated and fled across the Huai River. Though they made several attempts to halt and fight, each time with no success, Bu escaped with more than a hundred men to the south of the Yangtze River. Bu had previously married into the family of Fanjun, and because of this connection, King Ai of Changsha sent men to deceive Bu. They pretended to be fugitives and lured him toward Yue, so Bu trusted them and followed them to Fanyang. The people of Fanyang killed Bu in a peasant's house at Zixiang, thus ending the life of Qing Bu.

## 18

The emperor enfeoffed Crown Prince Zhang as King of Huainan and bestowed the title of Qisi Hou on Ben He; many generals were also granted fiefs for their merits.

## 19

Grand Historian said: "Qing Bu, was his ancestor perhaps the Ying and Liu mentioned in the Spring and Autumn Annals as having been destroyed by Chu? Was he not a descendant of Gao Tao?" "Having once suffered corporal punishment, how suddenly did he rise to power!" "The Xiang clan killed tens of thousands in their campaigns, and Bu was often the chief executioner." "His achievements surpassed those of all the feudal lords; through this he became a king. Yet, in the end, he could not escape being disgraced and killed." "Disaster arose from his beloved concubine's jealousy; envy bred trouble, and in the end it led to the destruction of his state!"
