# River Canal Book

## 1

The "Xia Shu" says: Yu controlled the great floods for thirteen years, passing by his home but not entering the gate. On land, one travels in a cart; on water, in a boat; through mud, by treading on downy feathers; over mountains, by crossing bridges. To distinguish the Nine Provinces, to follow the mountains and dredge rivers, and to assign tribute according to the land. He connected nine roads, constructed embankments around nine lakes, and measured nine mountain ranges. Yet the Yellow River's floods overflowed extensively, causing especially severe harm to Central China. This was his sole concern. Thus, the Yellow River flowed from Jishiqi through Longmen, south to Huayin, east past Dishu, and down to Mengjin and Luorui, reaching as far as Dapi. At this time, Yu considered that the terrain from which the river originated was high and the water swift and fierce, making it difficult to flow through flat land. It frequently caused damage; therefore, he dug two channels to divert the river. One channel directed the water northward to high ground, passing through Jiangshui and reaching Dalu. It spread into Nine Rivers, converging as a reverse-flowing river, entering Bohai. After the nine rivers were dredged and the nine lakes drained, all of Zhuxia enjoyed peace and stability; his achievements benefited the Three Dynasties.

## 2

After this, from Xingyang the river was led southeast into Honggou to connect Song, Zheng, Chen, Cai, Cao, and Wei, converging with the Ji, Ru, Huai, and Si rivers. In Chu, to the west channels were dug connecting the Hanshui and Yunmeng marshes; to the east they connected Honggou between the Jianghuai region. In Wu, channels were opened connecting the Three Rivers and Five Lakes. In Qi, channels connected between Zai and Ji rivers. 龙头 In Shu, the governor Bing excavated Liluo, eliminated the hazards of Moshui, and channeled two rivers through the center of Chengdu. These canals were all navigable for boats; when there was surplus water, it was used for irrigation, and the people benefited from them. As to the areas these channels passed through, they often diverted water for additional irrigation canals, numbering in the tens of thousands; yet none could be fully counted.

## 3

Ximen Bao led Zhangshui to irrigate Ye, enriching Wei's Hedong region.

## 4

The state of Han, hearing that Qin favored ambitious projects, wished to stop it and prevent Qin from advancing eastward. Thus, they sent a water engineer named Zheng Guo to secretly advise the State of Qin to excavate a channel diverting Jing Shui from Zhongshan's western side at Hukou, running along the northern mountains toward the east into Luo for more than 300 li, intending to use it for irrigation. When the project was halfway done, Qin discovered the plot and wanted to execute Zheng Guo. Zheng Guo said: "Initially I acted as a spy, but once the channel is completed, it will also benefit Qin." Qin considered this true and ultimately allowed him to complete the canal. Once completed, the channel directed water from Yuzhen to irrigate more than 40,000 qing of saline and barren land, yielding one zhong per mu. Thus, Guanzhong became fertile fields with no years of famine; Qin grew rich and powerful, eventually unifying the feudal states. The channel was therefore named Zhengguo Canal.

## 5

In the 39th year of Han's founding, during Emperor Wendi's reign, the Yellow River broke out at Suanzao and overflowed eastward to Jindi. Thus, a large number of conscripted soldiers were mobilized in Dongjun to block it up.

## 6

More than forty years later, during the Yuanguang period of the current emperor, the Yellow River burst its banks at Huzi and flowed southeast into Juye, connecting with the Huai and Si rivers. At this time, the emperor sent Ji An and Zheng Dangshi to mobilize people and laborers to block it, but they were repeatedly damaged. At that time, Marquis of Wu'an Tian Fen was the prime minister, and his fiefdom included Shu. Shu lay north of the Yellow River; if the river broke out to the south, then Shu would suffer no water disaster and its fiefdom's harvests would be abundant. Tian Fen said to the emperor: "The breaking of rivers and lakes is a matter of Heaven, not easily controlled by human effort. Even if we try to block them forcefully, it may not necessarily conform with Heaven's will." Those who observed atmospheric omens and used numerology also agreed. Thus, for a long time the emperor did not take action to block it again.

## 7

At that time, Zheng Dangshi was the Da Nong (Grand Agriculture Officer), and he said: "In previous times, grain transport from Guandong to Chang'an followed the Wei River route, taking about six months to complete. The waterway for 漕 transport was more than 900 li long, with frequent difficulties along the way." If we excavate a canal from Chang'an through the Wei River, running parallel to the southern mountains down to the Yellow River for more than 300 miles, it would be direct and easier for grain transport. It could reduce the journey to about three months; and more than 10,000 qing farmland below the canal could also be irrigated. This would reduce grain transport costs and labor while enriching Guanzhong land and increasing grain production." The emperor agreed, ordering Qi native water engineer Xu Bo to survey the site and mobilizing tens of thousands of conscripts to excavate the grain transport canal. It took three years before it was completed. Once completed, it facilitated grain transport with great convenience. Later, as more grain was transported through the canal, the people living along its route also benefited significantly from irrigation.

## 8

Later, Fan Xi, the governor of Hedong, said: "The grain transport route from east and west of Shandong carries over a million dan annually. It must pass through the perilous stretch at Dishu, where many losses occur, and it is also costly and laborious." If we excavate canals to divert Fen Shui to irrigate Pishi and Fenyin, and also lead the Yellow River water to irrigate Fenyin and Puban, it is estimated that 50,000 qing could be cultivated. These 50,000 acres were previously all abandoned land along the Yellow River floodplain, used only for growing reeds and grazing. Now, if they are irrigated as farmland, it is estimated to yield over two million dan of grain. Grain transported via the Wei River would be no different from that in Guanzhong, and thus there would be no need for further grain transport east of Dishu." The emperor considered this reasonable and mobilized tens of thousands of laborers to construct the canal farmland. lords After several years, the Yellow River shifted its course, making the canals unproductive; as a result, those who cultivated the land could not even recoup their seed costs. After some time, the canal farmland in Hedong was abandoned and given to Yue people, with the Small Treasury collecting a small amount of revenue from it.

## 9

Later, someone submitted a memorial proposing to open up the Baosixa Road and related grain transport matters, which was referred to Yushi Daifu Zhang Tang. Zhang Tang inquired about the matter and then said: "Traveling to Shu via the old road is difficult because it has many steep slopes and is circuitous." Now, if we excavate a route through Baosixa, there will be fewer slopes, covering about 400 li; the Bao River connects to the Mian River and the Xia River connects to the Wei River—both can be used for boat transport of grain. Grain could be transported from Nanyang up the Mian River into Bao, then across land between Bao and Xia for over 100 li by cart, before descending via Xia to the Wei River. In this way, grain from Hanzhong could be transported; Shandong's grain via the Mian River would not face the constraints of Dishu and would be more convenient than the Dishu route. Moreover, Baosixa is rich in timber, bamboo, and arrows, comparable to Ba and Shu." The emperor considered this reasonable, appointed Zhang Tang's son Zhang Ang as the governor of Hanzhong, and mobilized tens of thousands to construct more than 500 li of the Baosixa Road. The road proved indeed convenient and shorter; however, the water was swift and rocky, making grain transport by boat impossible.

## 10

Later, Zhuang Xiongpi said: "The people of Linjin wish to excavate a canal through Luo Shui to irrigate over 10,000 mu of old saline land east of Zhongquan. If water is successfully obtained, it could yield ten dan per mu." Thus, more than 10,00th conscripts were mobilized to excavate a canal, diverting Luo Shui from Zheng to the foot of Shangyan Mountain. The riverbanks were prone to collapse; therefore, wells were dug, some over 40 zhang deep. Wells were frequently constructed, with underground channels connecting them for water flow. The water flowed downward to bypass Shangyan and extended eastward through the mountain ridges over 10 li. The practice of well canals began from this time. A dragon bone was found during the excavation, so it was named Longshou Canal. After more than ten years of construction, the canal was somewhat completed but had not yet achieved its expected benefits.

## 11

More than twenty years after the Yellow River broke at Huzi, harvests continued to be poor year after year, and the regions of Liang and Chu were especially affected. After the emperor had performed the Fengshan ceremony and toured to worship mountains and rivers, the following year was marked by drought with little rain at Qianfeng. The emperor then dispatched Ji Ren and Guo Chang to mobilize tens of thousands of conscripts in an effort to block the breach at Huzi. At this time, after the emperor had conducted ceremonies at Wanli Sha, he returned to personally oversee the river breach. He sacrificed a white horse and jade 璧 into the Yellow River, ordering all officials from generals downward to carry firewood to help block the breach. At that time, Dongjun was experiencing a grass fire, so there was little firewood available; therefore, bamboo from Qi Yuan was used to make stakes.

## 12

After the emperor personally visited the breached river and lamented that the efforts had not succeeded, he composed a song: "The Huzi breach! What can be done?" "Day after day, we toil endlessly for the river!" "Toiling for the river makes the land unrest; ceaseless labor until my mountain is leveled." "My mountain leveled, Juye overflows; fish boil in turmoil on pine-covered winter days." "Rivers deviate from their course and abandon the usual flow; dragons gallop, venturing far away." "Return to your old riverbed! O mighty river of power. Without Fengshan ceremonies, how could I know the outside world!" "On my behalf, speak to Hebo: Why are you so unkind? Cease your flooding and cease troubling our people?" "Nibi floats; Huai and Si rivers overflow. The waters have not receded for a long time, their flow delayed." Another verse said: "The Yellow River surges and churns; crossing northward is difficult as the muddy current resists dredging." "Pulling long reeds, we sink fine jade; Hebo agrees but firewood remains scarce." "The lack of firewood is the fault of Wei's people. The land lies barren—alas, how can we control the waters!" "Fallen bamboo and stones block the disaster; when Xuanfang is sealed, ten thousand blessings will come." Thus, they finally blocked the Huzi breach, built a palace on its site, and named it Xuanfang Palace. They then redirected the Yellow River to flow northward through two channels, restoring Yu's ancient course; as a result, Liang and Chu regions were once again at peace, free from water disasters.

## 13

After this, officials vied to speak about the benefits of water management projects. Shuofang, Xhe, Hexi, and Jiuquan all diverted Yellow River and mountain stream waters for irrigation; while in Guanzhong Fuqu and Lingzhi channeled Du Shui; Runan and Jiujiang diverted the Huai River; lando Donghai diverted Ju Ding; and beneath Mount Tai, the Wen River was channeled: all these regions excavated canals for irrigation, each covering more than 10,0 mu. Other smaller canals that traversed mountains and valleys were too numerous to mention. Yet the most notable among them was at Xuanfang.

## 14

Grand Historian said: I traveled south to Mount Lu, observed Yu's dredging of the Nine Jiang rivers, and eventually reached Kuaiji Taikang. I climbed Gusu Hill and gazed at the Five Lakes; I looked eastward toward Luorui and Dapi, faced the Yellow River, and traveled along the Huai, Si, Ji, Lao, and Luo canals; I gazed westward at Shu's Min Mountain and Liluo; and north from Longmen to Shuofang. He said: How great is the benefit and harm caused by water! I once carried firewood to block the breach at Xuanfang, moved by the poem on Huzi and thus composed this River Canal Book.
