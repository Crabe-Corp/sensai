# Biographies of Ji and Zheng

## 1

Ji An, courtesy name Zhangru, was a native of Puyang. His ancestors had been favored by the ancient ruler of Wei. By Ji An's time, for seven generations they had served as high officials and gentlemen. Ji An was appointed through his father's recommendation; during the reign of Emperor Jing, he served as a Wushima (attendant to the crown prince) and was feared for his solemn demeanor. After Emperor Jing's death, the crown prince ascended the throne, and Ji An became a Yezhe (an official of the imperial court). When conflicts arose among the Dongyue tribes, the emperor sent Ji An to inspect the situation. He did not go all the way; instead, he went as far as Wu and returned, reporting: "The Yue people attacking each other is a matter of their customary behavior. It is unworthy to trouble an envoy of the Son of Heaven over such matters." A fire broke out in Hedong, spreading and burning more than a thousand homes. The emperor sent Ji An to investigate the situation. He returned with a report saying: "A family's fire spread from house to house, but it is not worth worrying about." "I passed through Henan and found that more than ten thousand poor families had been affected by floods and droughts, with some even eating their own parents or children. I have taken the liberty to use my authority as an envoy to open the granaries of Henan to distribute grain in relief of the impoverished people." "I now request to return my official seal and submit myself to punishment for having acted without imperial authorization." The emperor, recognizing his virtue, pardoned him and promoted him to the position of magistrate of Yingyang. Ji An considered it a disgrace to serve as a county official and resigned on grounds of illness, returning to his hometown. Upon hearing this, the emperor summoned him and appointed him Zhongdafa (an imperial court official). Because he frequently offered blunt remonstrances, he could not remain in the palace for long and was transferred to serve as governor of Donghai. Ji An studied the doctrines of Huang-Lao (the Yellow Emperor and Laozi), governing officials and managing people with a preference for tranquility, selecting his assistants carefully and entrusting them with responsibilities. His administration focused on the major principles alone, without being overly strict about minor matters. Ji An often fell ill and remained in his private quarters, not going out. After more than a year, Donghai was well governed. They praised him for it. The emperor heard of this and summoned him to serve as Zhujue Duyi, ranking among the Nine Ministers. His governance emphasized non-interference, focusing on major principles rather than being bound by formal rules and regulations.

## 2

Ji An had a proud personality, was lacking in courtesy, openly criticized others, and could not tolerate other people's mistakes. He treated those who agreed with him kindly, but could not bear to see those who did not conform to his views; thus, scholars also refrained from attaching themselves to him. Yet he was fond of learning, associated with wandering knights-errant, and upheld principles and integrity. Within himself, he cultivated virtue and purity; he favored direct remonstration, often defying the emperor's will, and always admired the character of Fu Bo and Yuan Ang. He was on good terms with Guan Fu, Zheng Dangshi, and Liu Qi, the Director of the Imperial Clan Office. He also lost his position after a short time due to frequent blunt remonstrations.

## 3

At that time, the empress dowager's younger brother, Hou Wu'an Fu Fen, was prime minister. When officials of the rank of Zhong Erqianshi came to pay their respects, Fu Fen did not offer them courtesy. Yet when Ji An met Fu Fen, he never bowed but only paid him a slight bow of respect. When the Son of Heaven was seeking to recruit scholars and Confucianists, the emperor said he wished to do this or that. Ji An replied: "Your Majesty is filled with inner desires yet outwardly practices benevolence and righteousness; how can you hope to emulate the rule of Tang and Yu?" The emperor fell silent, became angry, turned his face in displeasure, and dismissed court. All the high ministers were afraid for Ji An's sake. After retiring, the emperor said to those around him: "How stubborn and obtuse Ji An is!" Some court officials criticized Ji An, but he said: "The Son of Heaven appoints ministers and high officials to assist and advise him. Would it be right for them merely to flatter and follow his wishes, thus leading the ruler into injustice?" "Moreover, having held such a position, even if I were to love my own life, how could I bear to disgrace the imperial court?"

## 4

Ji An often suffered from illness; he was on sick leave for nearly three months, and the emperor frequently granted him extended leave, but his condition never improved. On his last illness, Zhuang Zhu requested a further extension of his sick leave on Ji An's behalf. The emperor asked: "What kind of man is Ji An?" Zhuang Zhu replied: "If Ji An were to hold an office and perform duties, he would not surpass others. Yet when it comes to assisting a young ruler, defending the city with unwavering resolve, not coming even if summoned and not leaving even if ordered away, even someone as strong as Bi Yu could not take that from him." The emperor said: "Indeed. "In ancient times there were ministers devoted to the state and its people; as for Ji An, he is close to being such a minister."

## 5

Grand General Qing, while serving in the imperial court, saw the emperor squatting on the toilet. Chancellor Hong met with him during a banquet; at times, the emperor did not even wear his hat. As for Ji An's audiences, however, if the emperor was not wearing his crown, he would not receive him. Once, when the emperor was seated inside a military tent, Ji An approached to present a report. Seeing that the emperor had not yet put on his hat, he caught sight of Ji An and retreated into the tent, sending someone else to approve his petition. Thus was the respect shown to him.

## 6

At that time, Zhang Tang was serving as Tingwei (Minister of Justice), reforming and establishing new laws. Before the emperor, Ji An repeatedly confronted and rebuked Zhang Tang, saying: "You hold a high official position; above, you cannot extol the achievements of former emperors, below, you cannot suppress the evil intentions of people throughout the land. You fail to strengthen the state or enrich its people, nor do you manage justice so well that prisons are empty. You accomplish neither of these two things." "You do not impose hardship on people to force them into compliance, nor encourage analysis and effort toward achieving success. Why then do you so recklessly alter the laws and decrees of past emperors?" "Because of this, your descendants will come to an end." At the time, Ji An often debated with Zhang Tang. Zhang Tang's arguments were usually based on intricate and petty legalistic interpretations, while Ji An remained unbowed in his upright stance. In anger, he scolded: "The world says that scribes who handle documents cannot become high ministers or officials; it is indeed true." "It must be because of Zhang Tang that people throughout the land now stand with both feet together in fear and cast sidelong glances!"

## 7

At this time, Han China was waging war against the Xiongnu and seeking to pacify the four foreign tribes. Ji An advocated for reducing affairs, taking advantage of the emperor's leisure time, and often advised that peace should be made with the Hu tribes without resorting to military action. The emperor was inclined toward Confucianism at this time and honored Gongsun Hong. As affairs became increasingly numerous, officials and commoners resorted to cunning and manipulation. The emperor began distinguishing legal procedures meticulously, and Zhang Tang and others frequently submitted judgments in order to gain favor. Meanwhile, Ji An often criticized Confucianism and openly accused Gongsun Hong and others of merely harboring deceitful intentions, using their intelligence to flatter the ruler for personal gain. He also condemned scribes who specialized in interpreting laws with excessive severity and skillfully fabricated accusations, ensnaring people in crimes from which they could not clear themselves, taking credit for convictions as achievements. The emperor grew even more favorably disposed toward Gongsun Hong and Zhang Tang. In turn, Gongsun Hong and Zhang Tong harbored deep resentment toward Ji An; however, the Son of Heaven also did not like him and wished to find a pretext to punish him. Gongsun Hong, as prime minister, said to the emperor: "The area under the jurisdiction of You Neishi contains many nobles and members of the imperial clan; it is difficult to govern. Only a long-trusted official can be entrusted with this post. I request that Ji An be transferred to serve as You Neishi." After serving as You Neishi for several years, he managed government affairs without failure.

## 8

Grand General Qing had become even more respected; his sister was the empress. Yet Ji An treated him with equal courtesy. Some people advised Ji An, saying: "The Son of Heaven wishes all ministers to show deference to the Grand General. The Grand General is becoming increasingly respected and powerful; you cannot afford not to bow." Ji An replied: "If a great general has guests who bow only slightly, does that mean he is less respected?" Upon hearing this, the Grand General regarded Ji An as even more virtuous and frequently invited him to consult on matters of state and court that were uncertain, treating him with greater respect than ever before.

## 9

When the Prince of Huainan plotted rebellion, he feared Ji An and said: "He is fond of direct remonstration and upholds his principles to the point of sacrificing for righteousness; it would be difficult to deceive him with falsehoods. "As for persuading Chancellor Hong, it is as easy as removing a blindfold or shaking loose dead leaves."

## 10

After the emperor achieved repeated successes in waging war against the Xiongnus, Ji An's advice was increasingly disregarded.

## 11

Initially, when Ji An had been ranked among the Nine Ministers, Gongsun Hong and Zhang Tang were still low-ranking officials. As Gongsun Hong and Zhang Tan gradually rose in status to hold positions equal to Ji An's, Ji An continued to criticize them. Later, Gongsun Hong became prime minister and was ennobled as a marquis; Zhang Tang rose to the position of Yushi Daifu (Minister of Supervision); Thus, at that time, officials who had once served under Chancellor Hong were now ranked equally with Ji An or even held positions of greater authority and influence. Ji An, being narrow-minded, could not help but feel some resentment. When he saw the emperor, he said before him: "Your Majesty uses your ministers like stacking firewood—those who come later are placed on top." The emperor fell silent. After some time, when Ji An left court, the emperor remarked: "Indeed, people cannot do without learning; observe how Ji An's words grow increasingly extreme."

## 12

Not long after, the Xiongnu King Hunye led his followers to surrender, and Han China dispatched twenty thousand carts. The imperial treasury had no money and borrowed horses from commoners. Some people concealed their horses, so the required number was not met. The emperor became angry and wanted to execute the magistrate of Chang'an. Ji An said: "The magistrate of Chang'an is innocent; only if you behead me will the people willingly bring out their horses. "Moreover, the Xiongnu have defected from their ruler and surrendered to Han. The Han can gradually transport them through the counties at a leisurely pace; why provoke unrest throughout the realm and exhaust the resources of China for the sake of these foreign people?" The emperor fell silent. When King Hunye arrived, merchants who had traded with him were arrested and sentenced to death—over 500 people in total. Ji An requested an audience and, upon meeting the emperor at Gaomen Gate, said: "The Xiongnu attacked key frontier passes and severed peace treaties. The Chinese raised armies to punish them, resulting in deaths and injuries too numerous to count, with expenses reaching hundreds of thousands." In my humble opinion, Your Majesty should enslave the Xiongnu people and distribute them as servants to the families of those who died in military service; and give their captured spoils directly to them, so that you may atone for the hardships endured by the people and satisfy the hearts of your subjects. Now, even if you cannot do this, King Hunye has led tens of thousands to surrender. To empty the imperial treasury for rewards and conscript innocent civilians to serve them is like catering to a spoiled child. How can ignorant commoners know that buying goods in Chang'an would be interpreted by legal officials as illegally transporting property across border checkpoints? "Even if Your Majesty cannot obtain the Xiongnu's wealth to atone for your people, it is wrong to use petty legal technicalities to execute more than 500 innocent people. This is what is called 'protecting the leaves while harming the branches.' I secretly consider this unwise on the part of Your Majesty." The emperor fell silent and refused, saying: "I have not heard Ji An's words for a long time; now he speaks again in vain." Several months later, Ji An was charged with a minor legal offense and happened to be pardoned and relieved of his post. At this time, Ji An withdrew into seclusion on his estate.

## 13

After several years, when the five-zhu coin was changed, many people began to illegally cast coins; this was especially severe in the Chu region. The emperor considered Huaiyang to be on the outskirts of the Chu territory, and thus summoned Ji An and appointed him governor of Huaiyang. Ji An knelt to express his thanks but refused the seal of office; after repeated imperial decrees insisting, he finally accepted and obeyed the order. The emperor summoned Ji An for an audience, and Ji An wept before the Son of Heaven, saying: "I had thought I would be buried in a ditch and never see Your Majesty again. I did not expect that Your Majesty would once more take me back into service." "I have long suffered from a chronic illness, and my strength is insufficient to handle the affairs of a commandery. I wish instead to serve as Zhonglang (an imperial attendant), entering and exiting the palace gates, correcting mistakes and offering advice—this would be my greatest wish." The emperor said: "Do you consider Huaiyang an insignificant post?" "I have now summoned you." "However, the officials and people of Huaiyang are not getting along well. I merely want to take advantage of your prestige so that you may govern them while lying in bed." After Ji An had taken his leave and departed, he passed by Li Xi, a senior official of the imperial court, and said: "I have been dismissed from my post in the commandery and am no longer able to participate in court deliberations. Yet the Grand Censor Zhang Tang is intelligent enough to resist remonstrances and deceitful enough to cover up his faults. He is devoted to flattery, employing eloquent and frequent arguments. He will not dare to speak for the people of the world but instead focuses solely on catering to the emperor's wishes. Whatever the Son of Heaven does not desire, he therefore denounces it; whatever the Son of Heaven desires, he praises accordingly. He likes to initiate projects and manipulate legal codes. Internally, he harbors deceit to control the emperor's mind; externally, he relies on corrupt officials to establish his authority and prestige. You hold a position among the Nine Ministers; if you do not speak out against him early, you will share in his punishment." Li Xi feared Zhang Tang and ultimately dared not say anything. Ji An governed the commandery as he had before, and the administration of Huaiyang became clear and orderly. Later, Zhang Tang was indeed disgraced; upon hearing that Ji An had spoken these words to Li Xi, the emperor punished Li Xi for his crime. The emperor ordered Ji An to reside in Huaiyang with the rank of a marquis's chancellor. Seven years later, he died.

## 14

After his death, because of Ji An's virtue, the emperor appointed his younger brother Ji Ren to a position among the Nine Ministers and his son Ji Yan as chancellor to a marquis. Ji An's aunt's grandson, Sima An, was also young when he served with Ji An as a Xima (attendant) to the Crown Prince. Sima An was eloquent, shrewd, and skillful in officialdom; he held four positions among the Nine Ministers before dying as governor of Henan Commandery. Because of Sima An's influence, ten members of his family simultaneously attained posts with salaries equivalent to 2000 dou (shi). Duan Hong of Puyang initially served Gai Hou Xin; he was trusted by Xin, and Duan Hong also twice attained a position among the Nine Ministers. Yet officials from Weishu all held Ji An in awe and regarded themselves as his inferiors.

## 15

Zheng Dangshi, courtesy name Zhuang, was a native of Chen. His ancestor Zheng Jun once served as a general under Xiang Ji; after Xiang Ji's death, he subsequently submitted to the Han dynasty. Emperor Gaozu ordered all former subjects of Xiang Ji to name their allegiance to him, but Zheng Jun alone did not obey the decree. The emperor decreed that those who named their allegiance to Xiang Ji should all be appointed as Daifu (a rank of official), and Zheng Jun was expelled. Zheng Jun died during the reign of Emperor Xiaowen.

## 16

Zheng Zhuang took pride in his role as a patron of chivalry and righteousness, rescuing Zhang Yu from disgrace while he was wandering between the states of Liang and Chu. During the reign of Emperor Xiaojing, Zheng Zhuang served as a Sheren (attendant) to the crown prince. Every five days, during his leave for washing and rest, he would often station relay horses at the suburbs to visit old friends, greet guests, and express gratitude. He continued these visits day and night until dawn, always fearing that he might not have visited everyone thoroughly. Zheng Zhuang was fond of the teachings of Huang-Lao (the Yellow Emperor and Laozi), and his admiration for elder sages seemed as if he feared never meeting them. Though young and holding a low official rank, the friends and associates with whom he traveled were all of his grandfather's generation—renowned men of high reputation throughout the land. When Emperor Wu ascended the throne, Zheng Zhuang gradually advanced to positions such as Lu Zhongwei (commandery military commissioner), governor of Jian'nan Commandery, chancellor of Jiangdu, and eventually attained a position among the Nine Ministers as You Neishi (Right Minister of the Interior). Because of his participation in discussions during the time of Marquis Wu'an Weiqi, he was demoted from his rank and appointed as Zhanshi (an official responsible for imperial affairs), later transferring to serve as Da Nong Ling (Minister of Agriculture).

## 17

When Zheng Zhuang served as Tai Shi, he gave instructions to his subordinates: "Whenever guests arrive, regardless of their status or rank, no one is to be detained at the gate." He strictly observed the etiquette between host and guest, always treating others with humility despite his own high position. Zheng Zhuang was upright and did not manage any private estates; he relied solely on imperial stipends to support the public officials. Yet when he gave gifts to others, they were no more than simple utensils and food. Each time he attended court, whenever he found an opportunity during the emperor's leisure moments, his conversations always included mentions of the virtuous men of the world. When he recommended scholars and officials, as well as his subordinates such as chancellors and clerks, if they truly had the qualities he described in their words, he often cited them as more virtuous than himself. He never addressed his subordinates by name when speaking to them; instead, he referred to them in a respectful manner, as if fearing that he might hurt their feelings. Whenever he heard someone speak well of another's virtues, he would recommend it to the emperor, always afraid of being too late. The learned men and officials from Shandong all praised Zheng Zhuang highly for this reason.

## 18

Zheng Zhuang was sent to inspect the river dyke, and he personally requested five days of travel time to prepare. The emperor said: "I have heard that 'When Zheng Zhuang travels, he needs no provisions for a thousand li.' Why then did you request time to prepare?" Yet while Zheng Zhuang was in court, he often followed the emperor's will and avoided expressing strong opinions on right or wrong. In his later years, during the Han dynasty's campaigns against the Xiongnu and its efforts to pacify the four barbarian groups, the state incurred heavy expenses, leading to increasing financial shortages. Zheng Zhuang appointed his guests as tenants for the Ministry of Agriculture's transport operations, and many of them fell into arrears and debt. Sima An, who was then governor of Huaiyang, exposed this matter, and Zheng Zhuang thus fell into disgrace. He was later pardoned through a ransom payment and demoted to the status of an ordinary commoner. Soon after, he served as Shou Changshi (an assistant official). The emperor considered him old and appointed Zheng Zhuang as governor of Ru'nan Commandery. Several years later, he died while still in office.

## 19

Zheng Zhuang and Ji An were initially appointed among the Nine Ministers; both were upright and cultivated moral integrity within themselves, maintaining personal purity. After these two men fell from favor, their families became poor, and their guests gradually drifted away. When they later served in the commanderies, after their deaths, there were no remaining assets or wealth left in their families. Because of Zheng Zhuang's virtue, six or seven members of his brothers' and sons' lineages attained posts with salaries equivalent to two thousand dou (shi).

## 20

Grand Historian said: "Even men as virtuous as Ji An and Zheng Zhuang, when they held power, attracted ten times more guests; but without influence, their circles dwindled. How much more so for ordinary people!" Zhai Gong of Xiangu had once said: At first, when Zhai Gong served as Tingwei (Minister of Justice), his door was crowded with guests; but after he fell from power, the area outside his gate could have been used to set a sparrow's trap—so desolate it became. When Zhai Gong was once again appointed as Tingwei, his former guests wished to visit him. In response, Zhai Gong had a notice posted at his gate that read: "Only through one death and one life can the true nature of friendship be known. One poverty and one wealth reveal the true attitude of friends. One honor and one disgrace expose the depth of a friendship." This was also true for Ji An and Zheng Zhuang—how sad!
