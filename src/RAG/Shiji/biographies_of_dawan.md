# Biographies of Dawan

## 1

The records of Dawan first appeared during Zhang Qian's time. Zhang Qian was a native of Hanzhong. During the Jianyuan period, he became an officer in the imperial court. At that time, the emperor asked those among the Xiongnu who had surrendered about the situation. They all said that the Xiongnu had defeated the king of the Yuezhi and used his head as a drinking vessel. The Yuezhi had fled and harbored constant resentment toward the Xiongnu but found no one to join them in attacking the Xiongnu. The Han dynasty was just about to take action against the Hu people, and upon hearing this report, decided to send envoys. Since the route had to pass through Xiongnu territory, they sought out a capable envoy. Zhang Qian volunteered as an officer and was sent to the Yuezhi, traveling together with Gan Fu, a former Xiongnu slave of the Tangyi family, departing from Longxi. Passing through Xiongnu territory, Zhang Qian was captured by the Xiongnu and taken to their Chanyu. The Chanyu detained him, saying: "The Yuezhi are north of us; how can the Han possibly send envoys there?" "If I wanted to send an envoy to Yue, would the Han allow it?" Zhang Qian was detained for more than ten years. During this time he married and had a son, yet Zhang Qian always kept the Han envoy's staff intact without losing it.

## 2

While living in Xiongnu territory, conditions gradually eased, and Zhang Qian took the opportunity to escape with his subordinates toward the Yuezhi. They traveled westward for several dozen days until they reached Dawan. Dawan had heard of the Han's wealth and wished to establish relations but could not do so. Upon seeing Zhang Qian, they were pleased and asked: "Where are you headed?" Zhang Qian said: "I am a Han envoy sent to the Yuezhi but was blocked by the Xiongnu from proceeding." "Now that I have escaped, I only ask Your Majesty to send someone to guide me on my way." "If I can indeed reach the Yuezhi and return to Han, the gifts and treasures that Han will bestow upon Your Majesty are beyond description." Dawan believed this to be true and sent Zhang Qian, providing guides who escorted him all the way to Kangju. From there, Kangju arranged for his passage until he reached the Da Yuezhi. The king of the Da Yuezhi had already been killed by the Hu people, and his crown prince was now installed as the new king. Having already become a vassal of Daxia and settled there, the land was fertile and abundant with few bandits. The people were content and at ease, and since they considered themselves far from Han, they had no intention whatsoever of retaliating against the Xiongnu. Zhang Qian traveled from Yuezhi to Daxia but ultimately failed to obtain a clear understanding of what the Yuezhi desired or needed.

## 3

He stayed there for more than a year and then returned, following the southern mountain range, intending to pass through Qiang territory back home. However, he was once again captured by the Xiongnu. After being detained for another year, the Chanyu died. The Left Gouli Wang attacked the crown prince and declared himself ruler, causing chaos within the Xiongnu state. Zhang Qian then fled back to Han with his Xiongnu wife and Tangyi Fu. The Han court appointed Zhang Qian as Taizhong Dafu (a high-ranking official title) and Tangyi Fu as Fengshi Jun (Envoy Officer).

## 4

Zhang Qian was known for his strength, broad-mindedness, and trustworthiness; the barbarians and non-Han peoples loved him. Tangyi Fu was originally a Hu person, skilled in archery, who, when in dire straits, shot birds and beasts to provide food. Initially, Zhang Qian had set out with more than a hundred people; after thirteen years away, only two of them returned.

## 5

Zhang Qian personally visited Dawan, Da Yuezhi, Daxia, and Kangju. He also described to the emperor about five or six major neighboring countries known by reputation in those regions. He said:

## 6

Dawan lies to the southwest of Xiongnu, directly west of Han, about ten thousand li away from Han. Their customs involve settled agriculture; they cultivate fields and grow rice and wheat. They have putoao wine (grape wine). They have many fine horses, whose sweat looks like blood; these are said to be heavenly horse offspring. They have walled cities and houses. Its subordinate towns, large and small, number more than seventy, with a population of about several hundred thousand. Their soldiers use bows, spears, and mounted archery. To its north is Kangju; to the west, Da Yuezhi; to the southwest, Daxia; to the northeast, Wusun; and to the east, Guizi and Yutian. To the west of Yutian, all rivers flow westward into the Western Sea; those to its east flow eastward and empty into the Salt Lake. The Salt Lake flows underground, and from beneath it to the south originates the source of the Yellow River. There are many jade stones there; the river flows into China. Loulan and Gushi also have walled towns, located by the Salt Lake. The Salt Lake is about five thousand li from Chang'an. To the right side of Xiongnu territory lies the area east of the Salt Lake, extending to the Longxi Great Wall. To its south is Qiang, which blocks the Han route.

## 7

Wusun lies about two thousand li to the northeast of Dawan; it is a nomadic state, following their herds and sharing customs with the Xiongnu. They have tens of thousands of archers who are brave in battle. Therefore, they were once subject to the Xiongnu; but when Wusun became strong, it took control of those who had been loosely affiliated with them and refused to attend court assemblies at the Xiongnu's request.

## 8

Kangju lies about two thousand li northwest of Dawan; it is also a nomadic state and shares largely the same customs as the Yuezhi. It has approximately eight or nine ten-thousands of archers. It is a neighboring state to Dawan. Its country is small, loosely subjecting itself to the Yuezhi in the south and to the Xiongnu in the east.

## 9

Yancai lies about two thousand li northwest of Kangju; it is also a nomadic country, sharing largely the same customs as Kangju. It has more than one hundred thousand archers. They live near a vast lake with no visible banks, which is probably the Northern Sea.

## 10

Da Yuezhi lies about two to three thousand li west of Dawan and resides north of the Gui River. To its south is Daxia, to its west Anxi, and to its north Kangju. It is also a nomadic people, moving with their herds, sharing the same customs as the Xiongnu.  It has about one or two hundred thousand archers. In the past, it was strong and looked down on the Xiongnu. When Modu became ruler, he attacked and defeated the Yuezhi; by the time of the Xiongnu Chanyu Laoshang, they killed the king of Yuezhi and used his skull as a drinking vessel. Originally, the Yuezhi lived between Dunhuang and Qilian. After being defeated by the Xiongnu, they fled far away, passed through Dawan, moved westward to attack Daxia and subjugated it, then settled north of the Gui River, establishing their royal court there. The smaller groups who could not leave remained in the southern mountain Qiang region and were called Xiao Yuezhi (Little Yuezhi).

## 11

Anxi lies about several thousand li west of Da Yuezhi. Their customs involve settled life; they cultivate fields, grow rice and wheat, and make putoao (grape) wine. They have walled cities like those of Dawan. Its subordinate towns and cities, large and small, number in the hundreds; its territory spans several thousand li, making it the largest country. It is located along the Gui River and has markets; its people engage in commerce using carts and boats, traveling to neighboring countries for distances of several thousand li. They use silver as currency; the coins resemble the face of their king, and when a king dies, they issue new coins with the image of the new ruler's face. They write records by painting on leather in horizontal rows. To its west is Tiaozhi, and to the north are Yancai and Luxuan.

## 12

Tiaozhi lies several thousand li west of Anxi and borders the Western Sea. It is hot and humid. They practice agriculture, growing rice. There are large birds there whose eggs are as big as pots. Its population is very numerous, and it often has small chieftains; however, Anxi dominates them and considers them as foreign vassals. The country is known for its skill in illusion or magic tricks. Elders of Anxi had heard through tradition that Tiaozhi has the Weak Water and the Queen Mother of Heaven to the West, but they had never actually seen them.

## 13

Daxia lies more than two thousand li southwest of Dawan, south of the Gui River. Their customs involve a settled lifestyle; they have walled cities and buildings, sharing similar customs with Dawan. They do not have great kings or chieftains but instead often appoint minor officials in their cities and towns. Their military is weak, and they fear battle. They are skilled in commerce and trade. When the Da Yuezhi migrated westward, they attacked and defeated Daxia, subjugating it as their vassal state. The population of Daxia is large, numbering about one or two million. Its capital is Lan Shi Cheng (Blue Market City), where various goods are bought and sold in the marketplace. To its southeast lies Shendu Kingdom.

## 14

Zhang Qian said: "While I was in Daxia, I saw 邛 bamboo canes and Shu brocade. I asked them: 'Where did you get these?' The people of Daxia replied: "Our merchants bought them in Shendu. Shendu lies about several thousand li southeast of Daxia. Their customs involve a settled life, largely similar to those of Daxia, but the land is low-lying, damp, and very hot. Its people ride elephants in battle. Their country borders a great river." Judging from Zhang Qian's estimation, Daxia is twelve thousand li away from Han and lies to its southwest. Now that the Shendu Kingdom is several thousand li southeast of Daxi, yet they have goods from Shu, this suggests it must not be far from Shu. Now if we were to send envoys to Daxia through Qiang territory, the route is dangerous and the Qiang people are hostile. If we go slightly northward, we would be captured by the Xiongnu; Traveling from Shu would be a more direct route and without bandits." The emperor had already learned that Dawan, as well as Daxia and Anxi, were all major countries with many rare goods. Their people led a settled life and engaged in activities somewhat similar to those of China, yet their military was weak and they valued Han treasures; To the north were Da Yuezhi and Kangju, among others, whose military strength was formidable enough to be approached with gifts and incentives for diplomatic purposes. Moreover, if we could genuinely bring them under our influence through righteousness and diplomacy, it would extend our territory by ten thousand li, involve multiple layers of translation, introduce us to exotic customs, and spread the power and virtue of Han throughout the four seas. The emperor was pleased and accepted Zhang Qian's suggestion as sound. He then ordered Zhang Qian to dispatch secret envoys from Shu through Jianwei, sending out four groups simultaneously: one departing from Mang, another from Ran, a third from Xu, and the fourth from Qiong and Bi; each group traveled about one or two thousand li on their respective routes. The northern route was blocked by Di and Zuo, while the southern route was obstructed by Xui and Kunming. The people of Kunming had no chieftain, were prone to banditry, and often killed or robbed Han envoys; as a result, the route was never successfully opened. However, it was heard that about a thousand li to the west of Kunming lay a country called Dianyue where elephants were ridden. Some Shu merchants and traders occasionally reached there; thus, Han's attempt to find a route to Daxia first connected with the Dian Kingdom. Initially, when Han sought to open communications with the southwestern Yi tribes, it cost a great deal and the routes remained impassable; thus, the effort was abandoned. When Zhang Qian suggested that a route to Daxia could be opened through this area, efforts to connect with the southwestern Yi were resumed.

## 15

Zhang Qian served as a Jiao Wei (a military rank) under the Grand General in an attack against the Xiongnu, and he knew where water and grass were available so that the army never lacked supplies. As a result, Zhang Qian was enfeoffed as Hou of Bofang. This year was Yuanshuo sixth year. The following year, Zhang Qian served as Weiyi and joined General Li in an expedition from Youbeiping to attack the Xiongnu. The Xiongnu surrounded General Li's army, which suffered heavy losses; Zhang Qian was also delayed and should have been executed for this offense, but he paid a ransom to avoid execution and was instead reduced to the status of a commoner. In this year, Han sent Piqiji to defeat tens of thousands of Xiongnu forces in the western region (city), advancing as far as Qilian Mountain. The following year, Hunye Wang led his people in surrendering to Han, and the area from Jincheng and Hexi westward along the southern mountain range to Yanzze was left empty of Xiongnu forces. Occasionally, some Xiongnu scouts still came through, but they were now very rare. Two years later, Han defeated the Chanyu and drove him north of Mubei.

## 16

After this, the emperor frequently asked Zhang Qian about Daxia and its neighboring regions. After Zhang Qian had lost his title of marquis, he said: "While I was in the midst of the Xiongnu, I heard that the Wusun king is called Kunmo. Kunmo's father was a small state on the western border of the Xiongnu." The Xiongnu attacked and killed his father, and Kunmo was born and abandoned in the wild. A vulture fed him flesh, and a wolf went to nurse him. The Chanyu found this strange and considered it divine, so he took Kunmo in and raised him as his own. When he grew up, the Chanyu made him a military commander and gave him several victories; thus, the Chanyu granted Kunmo his father's former people and ordered him to govern them in the western region (city). Kunmo gathered and protected his people, attacked neighboring small towns, commanded tens of thousands of archers, and became skilled in warfare. After the Chanyu died, Kunmo led his people far away to a distant location, maintaining a neutral stance and refusing to attend court assemblies of the Xiongnu. The Xiongnu sent special forces to attack him, but failed to defeat Kunmo; they regarded him as a divine figure and kept their distance. As a result, they only loosely controlled him without launching major attacks. Now that the Chanyu has recently been troubled by Han forces and the former Hunye territory is empty of people, the barbarian tribes are greedy for Han treasures. If we now take this opportunity to offer generous gifts to the Wusun and invite them further eastward, settling in the former Hunye territory, and establish a brotherly relationship with them, they should be willing to listen. If they do, it would effectively sever the right arm of the Xiongnu." Once we have established a connection with the Wusun, all the western regions such as Daxia can be invited to become our vassals." The emperor thought this plan sound and appointed Zhang Qian as Zhonglangjiang, giving him command of 300 men, each with two horses, tens of thousands of cattle and sheep, and carrying gold, coins, and silk worth several thousand million. He also dispatched many envoys holding imperial tokens to accompany the mission; wherever a route was feasible, they were sent to other neighboring countries as gifts.

## 17

After Zhang Qian arrived at Wusun, King Kunmo of the Wusun received the Han envoys with a ceremony equal to that for the Chanyu. Zhang Qian felt greatly ashamed and realized how greedy these barbarians were; he then said: "The emperor has sent generous gifts. If you do not bow in gratitude, I will return them." Kunmo rose to accept the gifts with a bow, but otherwise treated the envoys as before. Zhang Qian instructed his envoys, saying: "If the Wusun can move eastward to settle in the former Hunye land, Han will send a daughter of an elder prince as Kunmo's wife." The Wusun state was divided, its king old and distant from Han. Its true strength was unknown; for a long time it had been accustomed to being subject to the Xiongnu by force. Moreover, since they were close to the Xiongnu, their ministers feared the Hu people and did not wish to move. The king could not make independent decisions. Zhang Qian failed to achieve his main objective. lington Kunmo had more than ten sons, among whom was a son named Dalu, who was strong and skilled in leading troops. He commanded a separate force of over 10,000 cavalrymen. Dalu's elder brother was the crown prince, who had a son named Cenqu. However, the crown prince died at an early age. On his deathbed, he told his father Kunmo: "You must appoint Cenqu as the crown prince and not allow anyone else to take his place." Kunmo was moved by this request and agreed. In the end, he appointed Cenqu as crown prince. Dalu was angry that he had not been made the crown prince in place of Cenqu, so he gathered his brothers and led his forces to rebel, plotting to attack both Cenqu and Kunmo. Kunmo, being old and weak, was always afraid that Dalu would kill Cenqu. Therefore, he gave Cenqu over 10,00 troops to live separately, while Kunmo himself kept another 10,00 for his own protection. The Wusun people were thus divided into three factions, with the largest group loosely under Kunmo's control. Because of this situation, Kunmo also dared not make exclusive agreements with Zhang Qian.

## 18

Zhang Qian therefore dispatched his subordinate envoys separately to Dawan, Kangju, Da Yuezhi, Daxia, Anxi, Shendu, Yutian, Wusun, and other neighboring countries. The Wusun provided guides and interpreters to escort Zhang Qian back. Zhang Qian, together with the Wusun envoys, sent dozens of people and several dozen horses as gifts in return for their hospitality, while also allowing them to observe Han's vastness and strength.

## 19

After Zhang Qian returned, he was appointed Da Xing (Grand Administrator) and ranked among the Nine Ministers. A year or so later, he died.

## 20

After the Wusun envoys saw the large population and wealth of Han people, they returned to their country and reported it. As a result, their nation began to place even greater value on Han. A year or so later, many of the envoys Zhang Qian had sent to establish contact with Daxia and other regions returned together with people from those lands. Thus, for the first time, the western and northern countries began to open communication with Han. However, Zhang Qian's pioneering mission was the first of its kind; thereafter, all envoys sent abroad were called "Hou of Bofang," using his title as a guarantee to foreign nations. As a result, these foreign countries came to trust them.

## 21

After the death of Marquis Bofang Zhang Qian, the Xiongnu learned that Han had established communication with Wusun. They became angry and wanted to attack them. When Han envoys traveled to Wusun, if they went southward and reached Dawan and Da Yuezhi in succession, the Wusun became alarmed. They sent envoys offering horses and expressed a wish to marry a daughter of an imperial prince as a sister-in-law, thereby establishing a brotherly relationship with Han. The emperor consulted his ministers for their opinions and suggestions; they all said, "We must first receive the marriage gifts before sending our daughter." Initially, when the emperor consulted the Book of Changes (Yijing), it stated: "A divine horse will come from the northwest." When they obtained good horses from Wusun, they were named "Tianma," meaning heavenly horses. Later, when they acquired the blood-sweating horses from Dawan, which were even stronger, they renamed the Wusun horses "Xijie" and called the Dawan horses "Tianma." At this time, Han began constructing fortifications west of Lingju and initially established the Jiuquan commandery to facilitate communication with the western and northern countries. As a result, Han dispatched more envoys reaching Anxi, Yancai, Luxuan, Tiaozhi, and Shendu. The emperor favored the horses from Dawan so much that envoys could be seen traveling along the roads continuously. Each group of envoys sent to foreign countries was large, with hundreds in the biggest groups and over a hundred in smaller ones. The amount each person carried was generous, following the precedent set during Marquis Bofang's time. Later on, as these missions became more routine, their frequency and scale gradually declined. Han usually sent envoys once a year, with as many as more than ten missions in some years and at least five or six in others. The longest journeys took eight to nine years, while the shorter ones returned after several years.

## 22

At this time, after Han had already subdued Yue, both Shu and the southwestern Yi peoples were greatly impressed and requested officials to be appointed for governance and sent envoys to court. Thus, Han established the commanderies of Yizhou, Yuexi, Zangke, Shenli, and Wenshan in order to extend its territory and connect with Daxia as previously planned. They then dispatched envoys such as Baishichang and Lüyueren, sending more than ten groups each year from these newly established commanderies toward Daxia. However, all of them were again blocked by Kunming, where they were killed or had their gifts and treasures stolen; ultimately, no envoy succeeded in reaching Daxia. As a result, Han mobilized convicts from the three Fu regions and tens of thousands of soldiers from Ba and Shu, sending two generals—Guo Chang and Wei Guang—to attack the Kunming people who were obstructing Han envoys. They killed or captured tens of thousands before withdrawing. Afterward, when more envoys were sent, the Kunming people continued to attack them; ultimately, no route was successfully opened. Meanwhile, along the northern route from Jiuquan to Daxia, as more envoys were dispatched, foreign countries gradually became tired of Han gifts and no longer valued their goods.

## 23

Since Marquis Bofang had pioneered the route to foreign lands and brought honor, afterward, many of his subordinates and soldiers competed to submit memorials describing strange customs, benefits, and dangers in foreign countries, seeking opportunities to be envoys. The emperor, recognizing that these foreign lands were extremely distant and not places people would willingly go to, listened to their proposals, granted them imperial tokens, and recruited officials and commoners without regard for where they came from. He provided full support in terms of personnel and resources so as to expand the diplomatic routes. However, some of these envoys returned having failed to avoid theft or loss of gifts and goods, or they deviated from their mission objectives. The emperor, aware that many had become accustomed to such practices, would investigate them thoroughly and impose severe punishments in order to provoke anger and force them to pay fines for redemption, thereby encouraging them to seek new missions once again. The opportunities for embarking on diplomatic missions seemed endless, leading many to lightly break the law in pursuit of such chances. Even their subordinates and soldiers often exaggerated the resources of foreign countries, claiming that those who spoke highly of them would be granted imperial tokens as envoys while those with lesser claims would serve only as assistants. Thus, many deceitful individuals without integrity vied to emulate this behavior. These envoys were mostly the sons of poor people who secretly used government-supplied goods to buy cheaply and profit from them in foreign lands. 𝑙 Foreign countries also grew tired of the Han envoys, who each made exaggerated or inconsistent claims. Judging that Han troops were too distant to intervene, they imposed restrictions on food supplies in order to harass and inconvenience the Han envoys. The Han envoys suffered from shortages and accumulated grievances, eventually leading them to attack each other. Loulan and Gushi were merely small states that controlled a key but otherwise empty route, yet they launched particularly severe attacks on Han envoys such as Wang Hui. Meanwhile, the Xiongnu frequently sent special forces to intercept and attack those Han envoys heading westward to foreign countries. The envoys competed with one another in reporting disasters among the foreign peoples, claiming they all had walled cities and weak armies that could be easily attacked. Thus, the emperor decided to send General Chongbiao Hou Pulu, leading several thousand cavalry from vassal states and local troops, as far as the Xionghe River, intending to attack the Hu (Xiongnu). However, upon their arrival, the Hu had already retreated. The following year, they attacked Gushi. Pulu and a light cavalry force of more than 700 arrived first, captured the king of Loulan, and thus defeated Gushi. They then used their military might to pressure Wusun, Dawan, and other neighboring states into submission. Upon his return, Pulu was enfeoffed as the Marquis of Chuye. Wang Hui, who had been sent on multiple missions and was particularly troubled by Loulan, reported this to the emperor. The emperor dispatched troops and ordered Wang Hui to assist Pulu in defeating them. For his contribution, Wang Hui was enfeoffed as Marquis of Hao. At this time, beacon towers and defensive outposts were established along the Jiuquan route all the way to Yumen Pass.

## 24

The Wusun offered a thousand horses as dowry for the marriage of a Han princess. In return, Han sent a daughter from the imperial clan, Lady Jiangdu Wengzhu, to marry the Wusun; King Kunmo of Wusun made her his Right Concubine. The Xiongnu also sent a daughter to be married to Kunmo, who was appointed as the Left Concubine by King Kunmo. Kunmo said, "I am old," and thus arranged for his grandson Cenqu to marry the Han princess as his wife. The Wusun had many horses; some wealthy individuals even owned four or five thousand of them.

## 25

Initially, when Han envoys arrived at Anxi, the king of Anxi sent 20,000 mounted troops to meet them on the eastern border. The distance from the eastern frontier to the capital city was several thousand li. By the time they arrived, their journey passed through dozens of cities with a large and continuous population. After the Han envoys returned, Anxi later sent its own envoys following them to observe the vastness of Han. They presented large bird eggs and illusionists from Luxuan as gifts to Han. In addition, small states west of Dawan such as Huanqian and Dayi, and those to the east like Gushi, Wusun, Suxie, among others, all followed Han envoys in presenting themselves before the emperor. The emperor was greatly pleased.

## 26

Meanwhile, Han envoys traced the source of the Yellow River. The river's origin was found to be in Tian, a region where many jade and stones were found; samples were brought back. Referring to ancient maps and records, the emperor named the mountain from which the river originated as Kunlun.

## 27

At that time, the emperor was frequently touring by the sea, and thus invited all foreign guests to accompany him. When passing through major cities with large populations, he distributed wealth and silk as rewards, providing generous supplies to demonstrate Han's prosperity and abundance. At this time, grand performances were held, featuring unusual and bizarre entertainments that attracted large crowds to watch. Rewards and gifts were given out freely; wine pools and meat forests were set up. Foreign guests were taken to view the accumulated stores in various granaries, treasuries, and government warehouses, so they could witness the vast wealth of Han and be astonished by it. With added performances by illusionists, these grand spectacles and unusual entertainments changed and grew more elaborate each year, becoming increasingly popular from this time onward.

## 28

Diplomats from the western and northern foreign states continued to come and go in succession. To the west of Dawan, all those states considered themselves distant and remained arrogant and complacent; they could not yet be subdued by diplomatic courtesies or controlled through indirect rule. From the Wusun to the west as far as Anxi, these states were close to the Xiongnu. Because the Xiongnu had subdued the Yuezhi, whenever a Xiongnu envoy carried one letter from the Chanyu, each country along the way would provide food and transportation without delay or hardship. But when it came to Han envoys, they could not obtain food unless they offered gifts of silk and cloth; they could not ride or use horses without purchasing them. The reason for this was that these states were distant from Han, and since Han had abundant wealth, they could only obtain what they wanted through trade. However, they feared the Xiongnu more than they respected the Han envoys. The people of Dawan and its neighboring regions made wine from grapes, and wealthy individuals stored as much as ten thousand dou of wine; some aged for several decades without spoiling. The local custom was to love drinking alcohol, while the horses were fond of alfalfa. Han envoys brought back their seeds; thus, the emperor began cultivating alfalfa and grapes in fertile and abundant lands. With an increasing number of heavenly horses and foreign envoys coming to Han, grapevines and alfalfa were planted extensively around the palaces and pavilions, stretching as far as the eye could see. From Dawan to the west all the way to Anxi, although these countries had somewhat different languages, their customs were largely similar and they could understand each other. Their people all had deep-set eyes and beards; they were skilled in commerce, always competing over every tiny fraction of a coin. In their culture, women were highly valued; matters were decided by the men only after hearing what the women had to say. These regions lacked silk and lacquer, and did not know how to cast metal tools or coins. When Han envoys' escaped soldiers surrendered there, they taught the locals to cast and make other types of weapons. Whenever they obtained yellow or white gold from Han, they immediately made it into tools rather than using it as currency.

## 29

As Han envoys traveled there in large numbers, those who were young and followed them often became familiar with the emperor. They reported to him, saying: "Dawan has fine horses in Ershi City, but they are hiding them and refusing to give them to Han envoys." The emperor had already taken a liking to the horses of Dawan. Upon hearing this, he was delighted and sent strong men such as Che Ling with a thousand gold pieces and a golden horse to request the fine horses from Ershi City from King of Dawan. Dawan had abundant Han goods and discussed together, saying: "Han is far from us. The saltwater lake has caused several defeats for them. Going north would face the Hu raiders, while going south lacks water and grass." Moreover, along the way there were often deserted towns, and many people suffered from a lack of food. Hundreds of Han envoys came in groups, but they often lacked food and more than half died; how could such a situation allow for the arrival of a large army? We cannot be troubled by them. "Moreover, the horses of Ershi are our precious treasure." Thus they refused to give them to the Han envoys. The Han envoys became angry and spoke recklessly, smashing the golden horse and leaving. The nobles of Dawan were furious and said: "The Han envoy has shown great contempt for us!" They sent the Han envoys away, ordering their eastern border city of Yucheng to intercept and kill them, seizing their belongings. Upon hearing this, the emperor was furious. Those who had previously been sent to Dawan, such as Yao Dinghan, reported that the Dawan forces were weak. They claimed that if Han troops of no more than 3,000 men attacked with strong crossbows, they could easily capture and destroy Dawan completely. The emperor had previously sent Zhuyeh Hou to attack Loulan, where 700 cavalry arrived first and captured its king. Taking this as confirmation of what Dinghan and others said, he wished to honor his favorite concubine Lady Li by appointing her brother Li Guangli as the General of Ershi. He mobilized six thousand cavalry from vassal states and tens of thousands of wicked young men from various commanderies and kingdoms for a campaign against Dawan. The plan was to arrive at Ershi City and obtain the fine horses, hence he was given the title "General of Ershi." Zhao Shicheng served as the military judge, and Houhou Wang Hui was sent to guide the army. Li Duo served as a commander in charge of military affairs. This year marked the first year of Taichu era. At the same time, locusts swarmed in large numbers from east of the Guan region and flew as far west as Dunhuang.

## 30

After General Ershi's army passed the western side of Yanshui, the small states along the route were alarmed. Each fortified their cities and refused to provide food supplies. They attacked them but could not capture any city. Those cities that surrendered were given food, while those that did not surrender would be abandoned after several days. By the time they reached Yucheng, only a few thousand soldiers had arrived, all of them starving and exhausted. When they attacked Yucheng, the defenders dealt a heavy defeat to the Han forces, killing or wounding many soldiers. General Ershi and Duo, Shicheng, and others discussed: "We were still unable to conquer Yucheng; how can we possibly reach its capital city?" They withdrew their troops. The campaign lasted for two years. When they returned to Dunhuang, the number of surviving soldiers was no more than one or two out of every ten. They sent envoys to submit a report stating: "The route is long and food supplies are scarce; moreover, the soldiers do not fear battle but fear starvation. The number of troops is too small to conquer Dawan." We request that the campaign be temporarily suspended, and more forces be raised for a renewed expedition." The emperor heard this and was furious. He ordered envoys to block the Yumen Pass, declaring that any soldier who dared return would be executed on the spot! General Ershi was terrified and thus remained in Dunhuang.

## 31

That summer, more than 20,000 Han troops from the Zhuyeh campaign were lost to the Xiongnu. The ministers and officials all wished to abandon the campaign against Dawan and instead concentrate their efforts on attacking the Xiongnu. The emperor, however, had already committed to punishing Dawan. If such a small state as Dawan could not be subdued, the neighboring states like Dayu would look down on Han, and the fine horses of Dawan would no longer come, making it even harder for envoys sent to Wusun or Lun Tou; this would bring ridicule from foreign nations. He thus punished those who had particularly argued against attacking Dawan, such as Deng Guang. He released convicts and craftsmen from the penal labor force, and further mobilized tens of thousands more wicked young men and border cavalrymen; after over a year's preparation, 60,000 troops left Dunhuang, not counting those who went privately with them. Ten thousand oxen, more than 30,000 horses, and tens of thousands of donkeys, mules, and camels were also mobilized. They carried a large amount of provisions and were well-equipped with weapons and crossbows; the whole country was stirred into motion, as troops from all over successively joined the campaign against Dawan, totaling more than fifty commanders. The city of Dawan had no wells within its walls, and all the water was drawn from a stream outside the city. Taking advantage of this, Han forces sent water engineers to divert the underground water sources beneath the city in an attempt to drain it out and weaken the defenders. They further mobilized 180,000 more soldiers and garrison troops to the north of Jiuquan and Zhangye, establishing Jiuyan and Xitoo as defensive outposts to protect Jiuquan. At the same time, they conscripted convicts from seven categories across the empire, along with provisions, to supply General Ershi's campaign. The convoys of carts and laborers were linked together all the way to Dunhuang. They also appointed two horse experts as Commanders of Horse Management, in preparation for capturing and selecting the finest horses from Dawan.

## 32

Thus General Ershi launched another campaign with a large force, and wherever he went, the small states all welcomed him, providing food supplies for his army. When they reached Lun Tou, its defenders refused to surrender; after several days of attack, the city was captured and destroyed. From there westward, the Han army advanced steadily toward Dawan's capital city, with 30,000 soldiers arriving at its gates. The defenders of Dawan came out to attack the Han forces; however, they were defeated by Han archers and retreated into their city for defense. General Ershi's forces had originally intended to attack Yucheng first, but fearing that delaying the campaign would allow Dawan more time to devise tricks, they instead moved directly toward Dawan and cut off its water supply. By diverting the flow of water, the defenders were already in a state of distress and despair. They surrounded the city and attacked it for more than forty days, during which time its outer walls were breached. The Han forces captured Jianmi, a brave general among Dawan's nobles. The defenders of Dawan grew greatly alarmed and retreated into the inner city for refuge. Dawan's nobles discussed among themselves, saying: "The reason Han attacked Dawan was because King Wugu hid the fine horses and killed their envoys. If we now kill King Wugu, hand over the good horses, then Han forces should withdraw; if they do not withdraw, we can still fight to the death—it is not too late." The nobles of Dawan all agreed, and together they killed their king Wugu. They took his head and sent a noble envoy to General Ershi with an agreement: "Han must not attack us anymore. We will hand over all the fine horses for you to take as you wish, and provide food supplies for your Han army." If you do not agree, we will kill all the good horses, and help from Kangju is on its way. When they arrive, we will take position inside the city while Kangju's forces take up positions outside to fight against your Han army." The Han army carefully considered their options and asked: "Which should we choose?" At that time, Kangju was watching the Han forces. Seeing that the Han army was still strong, they dared not advance. General Ershi discussed with Zhao Shicheng, Li Duo and others: "We have heard that the defenders of Dawan city recently acquired some Qin people who know how to dig wells, so there is still plenty of food inside the city. The purpose of our coming here was to punish Wugu, the chief villain. Wugu's head has already been delivered to us. If we do not accept this and withdraw our troops, they will hold out resolutely. Meanwhile, Kangju is waiting for the Han forces to grow weary before coming to rescue Dawan; in that case, it would certainly lead to a defeat of our army." The military officers all agreed and accepted the agreement offered by Dawan. Dawan then brought out their finest horses, allowing the Han to choose freely, and also provided a large amount of food supplies for the Han army. The Han forces selected dozens of the best horses. They also took more than 3,00 pieces of male and female horses of medium quality or lower. Then they installed Mei Cai, a noble from Dawan who had previously treated the Han envoys well, as the new king of Dawan. A pact was made with them before withdrawing their troops. The Han forces were never able to enter the inner city. They thus withdrew and returned.

## 33

Initially, General Ershi departed from the west of Dunhuang with a large force. Concerned that the small states along the way could not provide enough food for so many troops, he divided his army into several groups and sent them through both the northern and southern routes. Lieutenant Wang Shengsheng, former Honglu Hu Chongguo, and more than a thousand others took an alternate route to Yucheng. The defenders of Yucheng refused to provide food supplies for their troops. Wang Shengsheng was two hundred li away from the main army, and he underestimated the enemy while conducting a reconnaissance mission. He then confronted Yucheng with contempt. Yucheng still refused to provide food, and they observed that Wang Shengsheng's forces were dwindling daily. Early in the morning, they launched an attack with 3,000 men, killing Wang Shengsheng and others. His army was crushed, and only a few managed to escape and flee back to General Ershi. General Ershi ordered Sosu Duyi Shangguan Jie to go and attack Yucheng, which was then captured. The king of Yucheng fled toward Kangju, and Shangguan Jia pursued him all the way to Kangju. Upon learning that the Han had already captured Dawan, Kangju handed over the king of Yucheng to Shangguan Jie. Jie then ordered four mounted soldiers to bind and guard him before delivering him to the general-in-chief. The four men said to each other, "The king of Yucheng is a man whom the Han state has long wanted dead. Now that he is alive and about to be taken away, we may miss our chance for great achievement." They wanted to kill him but dared not strike first. Zhao Di, a mounted soldier from Shanggui and the youngest among them, drew his sword and struck first, beheading the king of Yucheng. He then carried the head with him. Zhao Di, Shangguan Jie, and others hurried to catch up with the general-in-chief.

## 34

Initially, after General Ershi had departed later than planned, the emperor sent envoys to inform Wusun and ordered them to mobilize a large army to join forces in attacking Dawan. Wusun mobilized 2,000 cavalrymen but remained indecisive and refused to advance. As General Ershi's army moved eastward, the small states they passed through, having heard of Dawan's defeat, all sent their sons and younger relatives to join the Han forces as tribute bearers. They were presented before the emperor and thus became hostages. During General Ershi's campaign against Dawan, Junzheng Zhao Shicheng fought with great valor and earned the most military merit. Shangguan Jie also showed courage by leading a deep penetration into enemy territory, while Li Duo provided strategic planning. More than 10,000 soldiers and over 1,000 horses entered the Yumen Pass. General Ershi departed later than scheduled, and his troops were not short of food. The number of soldiers who died in battle was not large; however, the officers and commanders were greedy and did not care for their men, often exploiting them, which led to many deaths among the soldiers. The emperor, having launched a campaign thousands of miles away against Dawan, chose not to hold any past offenses against them and enfeoffed Guangli as the Marquis of Haixi. He also enfeoffed Zhao Di, the mounted soldier who personally beheaded the king of Yucheng, as the Marquis of Xinzhi. Junzheng Zhao Shicheng was appointed Guanglu Daifu, Shangguan Jie became Shaofu, and Li Duo served as Taishou of Shangdang. Among the military officers, three were appointed as Jiusheng (Nine Ministers), more than a hundred became chancellors of feudal states, governors of commanderies, or officials with salaries equivalent to 2000 dan, and over a thousand held positions below the rank of 1000 dan. Those who showed outstanding initiative were promoted beyond their original expectations, while those whose actions fell short of the standard had their merits reduced accordingly. The soldiers and footmen received rewards amounting to 40,000 gold pieces in total. After two campaigns against Dawan, it took a total of four years before the campaign was finally concluded.

## 35

After defeating Dawan, the Han installed Mei Cai as king of Dawan and then withdrew. More than a year later, the nobles of Dawan considered Mei Cai to be overly flattering and blamed him for leading their country into disaster. They therefore conspired together to kill Mei Cai and installed Chancheng, one of Wugu's brothers, as the new king of Dawang. They then sent his son as a hostage to the Han. The Han therefore dispatched envoys with gifts and bribes to pacify them.

## 36

Meanwhile, the Han sent more than ten diplomatic missions to the foreign states west of Dawan in search of rare treasures and curiosities. These envoys also took the opportunity to publicize the might and virtue of the Han through their travels. Meanwhile, Dunhuang established a Duwei (military commander) in Jiuquan; To the west as far as Yanshui, there were often relay stations. At Lun Tou, several hundred soldiers were stationed to cultivate the land; therefore, officials were also assigned to oversee and store grain for use by envoys sent abroad.

## 37

Grand Historian said: The "Yu Benji" states, "The Yellow River originates from Kunlun." Kunlun is said to be over 2500 li high, where the sun and moon take turns hiding from each other, creating light and darkness. There are also nectar springs and the Jade Pool on its summit." Now, after Zhang Qian's mission to Daxia, people have traced the source of the river. But where exactly is the Kunlun mentioned in the annals? Therefore, when it comes to descriptions of the nine provinces' mountains and rivers, the "Shangshu" (Book of Documents) is the most accurate. As for the strange creatures and monsters described in the "Yu Benji" and the "Shanhaijing," I dare not speak of them.
