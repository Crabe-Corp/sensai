# Huo Zhi Lie Zhuan

## 1

Laozi said: "In the extreme of good governance, neighboring states can see each other, and the sounds of chickens and dogs can be heard from one another. The people find their food delicious, their clothing beautiful, their customs peaceful, and their occupations pleasant; they live out their lives without ever visiting or interacting with others." If one insists on taking this as the priority, and in recent times dazzles the people's ears and eyes, then there will be almost no way to proceed.

## 2

Grand Historian said: Before Shen Nong, I do not know. As for what the Shijing and Shujing describe since Yu and Xia, the ears and eyes desire to indulge in the pleasures of sound and color; the mouth desires to exhaust the taste of fodder and livestock; the body enjoys ease and pleasure, while the heart boasts of honor derived from ability. The customs have been influencing the people for a long time; even if one tries to persuade each household with subtle arguments, ultimately it cannot be changed. Therefore, the best approach is to follow and make use of existing conditions; next is to guide through benefits; then comes instruction and admonition; followed by regulation and standardization; the worst is to compete with them.

## 3

Shanxi abounds in timber, bamboo, grain, hemp, yak hair, and jade stones; Shandong has an abundance of fish, salt, lacquer, silk, and pleasures of sound and color; Jiangnan produces nanmu, zhi wood, ginger, camphor, gold, tin, lian, cinnabar, rhinoceros horn, hawksbill shell, pearls and beads, ivory, and leather; North of Longmen and Jieshi there are many horses, cattle, sheep, felt garments, tendons, and horns; Copper and iron can be found in mountains scattered throughout the land at intervals of a thousand li: this is their general situation. These are all things favored by the people of China for their customs, clothing, food and drink, as well as for the necessities of life and death. Therefore, we rely on farmers to provide our food, on officials in charge of resources to extract them, on artisans to complete their production, and on merchants to circulate them. Could this possibly be achieved through political instruction or the imposition of schedules? Each person follows their ability, exerts their strength, and thus obtains what they desire. Therefore, when goods are cheap, their value becomes high; when they are highly valued, they become cheap. People encourage each other in their occupations and take pleasure in their work, just as water flows downward day and night without rest. They come of their own accord without being summoned, and produce them without needing to be asked. Is this not in accordance with the Dao, a demonstration of nature itself?

## 4

The Zhou Shu says: "If farmers do not produce, food will be lacking; if artisans do not produce, their crafts will fail; if merchants do not trade, the three treasures will be cut off; and if officials in charge of natural resources do not act, wealth will become scarce." When wealth becomes scarce, mountains and marshes remain unexplored. These four are the sources of clothing and food for the people. When the source is abundant, there will be plenty; when it is small, there will be scarcity. At a higher level, this enriches the state; at a lower level, it enriches families. The way of wealth and poverty is not something that can be taken or given by others; the skillful have surplus, while the clumsy lack. Therefore, when Tai Gong Wang was enfeoffed in Yingqiu, the land was saline and barren, with few people. Thus, Tai Gong encouraged women's work, promoted exquisite skills, and facilitated trade in fish and salt; as a result, both goods and people flocked to him, coming like threads converging from all directions. Thus, Qi became the source of hats, sashes, clothing, and shoes for the whole world; between the Hai and Dai regions, people gathered their sleeves in reverence to pay homage. Later, when Qi declined, Guan Zi reformed it by establishing the Nine Treasuries of Light and Heavy Trade; thus Duke Huan of Qi became a hegemon, convened the feudal lords nine times, and unified the realm. And Guan Zi also had three residences for his revenues; though holding a position of an attendant minister, he was wealthier than the rulers of other feudal states. This is why Qi became rich and powerful until the time of Kings Wei and Xuan.

## 5

Therefore it is said: "When granaries are full, people know propriety and restraint; when clothing and food are sufficient, they understand honor and disgrace." Ritual arises from abundance but collapses in scarcity. Therefore, when a gentleman is wealthy, he likes to practice virtue; when a petty person is wealthy, they use it to satisfy their desires. Deep pools give birth to fish; deep mountains attract beasts; when people are wealthy, benevolence and righteousness attach themselves. The rich gain influence and become more prominent; if they lose it, their guests have nowhere to go, and thus they are unhappy. This is even truer for the Yi and Di barbarians. A proverb says: "A son of a man with a thousand gold pieces will not die in the marketplace." This is no empty talk. Therefore it is said: "Under heaven, all bustle about—everyone comes for profit; under heaven, all rush and hustle—everyone goes in pursuit of gain." lineno A king with a thousand chariots, a marquis ruling ten thousand households, or a lord governing a hundred families still worry about poverty; how much more so for commoners and ordinary subjects!

## 6

In the past, King Goujian of Yue was trapped at Kuaiji Mountain; he then employed Fan Li and Ji Ran. Ji Ran said: "Understanding the contest means preparing accordingly; knowing the timing of use allows one to understand goods. When these two are combined, the nature of all commodities can be observed." Therefore, when the year is in Metal, there will be a bountiful harvest; when it is in Water, destruction occurs; in Wood, there will be famine; in Fire, drought. When there is drought, one should prepare boats; when there is flooding, one should prepare carts—this is the principle of things. There are six years of abundance, six years of drought, and a great famine every twelve years. When grain is sold at twenty coins per dan, it harms farmers; when it reaches ninety coins, it harms merchants. If merchants suffer, wealth will not circulate; if farmers suffer, fields will remain uncultivated. If the price does not exceed eighty coins at the top and does not fall below thirty at the bottom, then both farmers and merchants benefit. Equalizing grain prices and goods ensures that customs and markets are never lacking—this is the way to govern a state well. The principle of accumulation lies in ensuring goods remain intact and currency does not stagnate. Exchange goods with each other; do not keep perishable or useless items, nor dare to hoard expensive goods. By assessing what is in surplus and what is lacking, one can know the value of high or low prices. When prices rise to their peak, they will inevitably turn cheap; when they fall to their lowest point, they will rebound and become expensive again. Selling at high prices should be as easy as discarding manure; buying at low prices should be as careful as acquiring pearls and jade. Wealth and currency should flow like water." After ten years of implementing these policies, the state became rich. Generous rewards were given to soldiers, who rushed into battle as if a thirsty man drinks water. Thus they avenged their defeat by the powerful State of Wu, displayed military might in Central China, and earned the title "One of the Five Hegemons."

## 7

After Fan Li had avenged the humiliation at Kuaiji, he sighed and said: "Ji Ran's seven strategies were used by Yue—five of them brought success. Since they have already been applied to the state, I now wish to apply them to my family." Thus he boarded a small boat and floated on the rivers and lakes, changed his name and surname, went to Qi as Chi Yi Zi Pi, then moved to Tao where he became known as Zhu Gong. Zhu Gong considered Tao the center of the world, a place through which feudal lords from all directions passed and where goods were traded. He then began to manage property and accumulate wealth. He followed the times in his pursuits without demanding anything from others. Therefore, those skilled at managing wealth can choose people wisely and act according to the right time. Within nineteen years, he tripled his fortune three times, then distributed it among poor friends and distant relatives. This is what is meant by the wealthy who enjoy practicing virtue. Later, as he grew old, he entrusted his affairs to his descendants; they maintained and expanded the family business, eventually accumulating tens of thousands in wealth. Therefore, whenever people speak of great wealth, they all refer to Zhu Gong of Tao.

## 8

Zi Gan, having studied under Confucius, later served as an official in Wei. He abandoned his position to engage in commerce between Cao and Lu; among the seventy disciples of Confucius, Zi was considered most prosperous and beneficial. Yuan Xian did not disdain scraps and coarse food, living in a remote alleyway. Zi Gan traveled with four horses and carriages, carrying bundles of silk as gifts to visit feudal lords. Wherever he went, the rulers received him in equal courtyards, treating him with full courtesy. It was Zi Gan who ensured that Confucius's name spread throughout the world; he worked tirelessly before and after to promote it. Is this not an example of gaining influence and becoming even more prominent?

## 9

Bai Gui was a native of the Zhou state. During the reign of Duke Wen of Wei, Li Ke focused on maximizing land productivity, while Bai Gui took pleasure in observing changes in timing. Therefore, when others discarded goods, he acquired them; and when others sought to buy, he sold. In years of good harvest, he would take grain and give silk and lacquer in return; when cocoons were produced, he took cloth and gave food. When Taiyin is in Mao, there will be a plentiful harvest; the following year will be one of decline and poor conditions. When it reaches Wu, there will be drought; the following year will be good. When it reaches You, there will be abundance; the following year will decline and become poor. When it reaches Zi, there will be great drought; the following year will be prosperous and bring floods. When it reaches Mao, the accumulation of goods typically doubles in value each year. To increase wealth, buy low-quality grain; to increase in stone and dou measures, acquire the best seeds. He who can be frugal in food and drink, restrain desires, dress simply, share hardships and joys with servants and attendants, and act swiftly as a fierce beast or a hawk strikes— Therefore it is said: "My management of production and wealth is like Yi Yin and Jiang Shang's strategies, Sun Wu's use of military tactics, or Shang Yang's enforcement of laws." Therefore, if one lacks the wisdom to adapt flexibly, the courage to make decisions, the benevolence to give and take appropriately, or the strength to hold firm in principle, even though they wish to learn my methods, I will never reveal them." Thus, throughout the world, those who speak of managing wealth trace their origins back to Bai Gui. Bai Gui had indeed put his theories into practice; he demonstrated effectiveness and possessed real talent, not merely acting out of convenience or chance.

## 10

Yi Dun rose to prominence through the production of salt from brine pits. Meanwhile, Guo Zong of Handan became wealthy through iron smelting and rivaled kings in wealth.

## 11

Wu Shi Lue raised livestock, and as his herds grew large, he sold them off to acquire rare goods and fabrics, occasionally presenting gifts to the chieftains of the Rong tribes. The Rong tribal leaders repaid him tenfold and gave him livestock; his herds became so numerous that they were measured in terms of bushels, like grain. Emperor Qin Shi Huang ordered Lue to be treated on par with a feudal lord, allowing him to attend court sessions and make requests at the same time as high-ranking ministers. And Ba [Shu] Guo Fu Qing, whose ancestors discovered a cinnabar deposit and controlled its profits for several generations, became so wealthy that her family's fortune was immeasurable. Qing was a widow who managed to maintain her business and used her wealth for self-defense, remaining unharmed by any invasions. Emperor Qin Shi Huang regarded her as a virtuous woman and received her as a guest; he even built for her the Nü Huaiqing Tai (the "Nü Huai Qing Terrace"). Lue was a humble herdsman, and Qing was a poor village widow. Yet both were treated with the same courtesy as a king, their names renowned throughout the land—was this not due to their wealth?

## 12

When the Han dynasty rose to power, the realm was unified. Passes and barriers were opened, restrictions on mountains and lakes were lifted; thus, wealthy merchants and great traders traveled throughout the land, trading all kinds of goods without hindrance, obtaining whatever they desired. In this way, powerful aristocrats and influential families from various regions were relocated to Chang'an, the capital.

## 13

The Guanzhong region, from Qian and Yong in the west to the Yellow River and Hua Mountain in the east, is a fertile plain stretching for thousands of li. Since the time of Yu and Xia, this land has been considered the best farmland according to tribute records. Gong Liu moved to Bin, Taizi Wáng and King Ji resided at Qi, King Wen established Feng, and King Wu governed Hao; thus, its people still preserved the traditions of the ancient kings, valuing farming, cultivating five grains, respecting their land, and being reluctant to engage in evil deeds. And during the reigns of King Wen, (Xiao) [De], and Miao who resided in Yong, merchants took advantage of the goods from Long and Shu regions to conduct extensive trade. King Xian (Xiao) Gong moved the capital to Lishi, which was situated to repel the Rong and Di tribes in the north and connect with the three Jin states in the east; thus, there were also many wealthy merchants there. (King Wu) [Xiao], King Zhao governed Xianyang, and later the Han dynasty made it their capital. With the various mausoleums in Chang'an as centers, people from all directions converged there. The area was small but densely populated; therefore, its people became increasingly skillful and engaged in commerce and minor occupations. To the south lay Ba and Shu. Ba and Shu were also fertile lands, rich in resources such as cinnabar, ginger, vermilion sand, stone, copper, iron, bamboo, and timber for tools. To the south they controlled Dian and Bo, where captives were used as slaves. To the west lay Qiong and Zuo, known for their horses and yaks. Yet the region was surrounded by mountains on all sides, with plank roads stretching for thousands of li and connecting everywhere. Only the Baoxia Pass served as a key junction, where abundant goods were exchanged for scarce ones. Tianshui, Longxi, Beidi, Shangjun shared the same customs as Guanzhong. However, to the west lay the fertile lands of Qiangzhong and to the north were the pastures of Rong and Di tribes; thus, animal husbandry in this region was among the most prosperous in the world. Yet the land was also remote and perilous, with only the capital city controlling its key routes. Thus, although Guanzhong accounted for one-third of the land in the empire, its population was no more than one-tenth of that. Yet when it came to wealth, it possessed six-tenths of the total.

## 14

In ancient times, the Tang people dwelled in Hedong, the Yin people resided in Henan (He Nei), and the Zhou people lived in He Nan. The three rivers—Yellow River, He Nei, and He Nan—are located at the center of the empire like the legs of a tripod; they were places where successive kings resided. Kingdoms had been established there for hundreds or even thousands of years. The land was small and narrow, yet densely populated, as it served as the gathering place for capitals and feudal lords. Therefore, its customs became refined, frugal, and practical. Merchants from Yang and Pingyang traded westward with Qin and Di tribes, and northward with Zhongshan and Dai states. Zhongshan and Dai were located to the north of Shishan; their lands bordered the Hu tribes, frequently suffering invasions. The people there were proud and vengeful, valuing personal honor highly; they engaged in chivalric acts of violence and lawlessness, avoiding agriculture and commerce. Yet due to their proximity to the northern Hu tribes, military expeditions frequently passed through, and goods from the central regions were often transported there in abundance. The people were rough and uneven in character. Even since the time of the full Jin state, they had already been regarded as a source of concern for their boldness and ferocity; King Wuling further encouraged this martial spirit, so their customs still bore the style of the State of Zhao. Therefore, officials from Yang and Pingyang stationed there were able to obtain what they desired. Merchants from Wen and Zhi traded westward with Shangdang, and northward with Zhao and Zhongshan. Zhongshan had thin soil but a large population; it still had remnants of the people from Shaqiu, the place where King Zhou indulged in debauchery. The local customs were hasty and impetuous, with the people relying on seizing opportunities for profit to make a living. The men often gathered together, playing and singing sorrowful songs with a sense of melancholy. When they rose up, they followed each other in committing violent robberies; when at rest, they dug graves or engaged in deceitful trades such as metalworking, producing many fine goods to become entertainers and performers. The women played zithers with loud music, wore slippers loosely on their feet, and used flattery to attract the wealthy and noble. They entered royal harems and were found in the courts of feudal lords throughout the land.

## 15

Yet Handan was also a major city located between the Zhang River and the Yellow River. To the north it connected with Yan and Zhuo, to the south with Zheng and Wei. The customs of Zheng and Wei were similar to those of Zhao; yet being close to Liang and Lu, they showed slight refinement and a sense of self-respect. The city of Poshang was moved to Yiwang; the people of Yiwang valued personal honor and chivalry, a characteristic of Wei's customs.

## 16

Yan was also one of the major cities between Bo and Jie. To the south it connected with Qi and Zhao, to the northeast it bordered the Hu tribes. From Shanggu to Liaodong, the land was vast and remote; its population sparse, frequently subjected to invasions. Its customs were largely similar to those of Zhao and Dai, yet its people were bold and fearless with little concern for caution, benefiting from abundant fish, salt, jujubes, and chestnuts. To the north they bordered Wuhuan and Fuyu; to the east they controlled trade with Huihe, Chaoxian, and Zhenfan.

## 17

Luoyang merchants traded eastward with Qi and Lu, southward with Liang and Chu. Thus, the southern side of Mount Tai was occupied by Lu, while its northern side belonged to Qi.

## 18

Qi bordered mountains and seas, with fertile land stretching for thousands of li; it was suitable for growing mulberry trees and hemp. Its people produced much colorful cloth, silk, fish, and salt. Linzi was also one of the great cities between the sea and Mount Dai. Its customs were broad-minded and open, with people full of wisdom who enjoyed debate. The land was stable and difficult to shake; they were timid in mass fights but brave in using weapons for personal defense or attacks, hence there were many robbers—this reflected the style of a great state. Among them lived all five types of people.

## 19

Zou and Lu, bordering the Zhu and Si rivers, still preserved the customs of Duke Zhou; their people favored Confucianism and were well-versed in rituals, so they appeared refined and courteous. They had considerable mulberry and hemp cultivation but lacked the abundance of forests and lakes. The land was small yet densely populated; people were frugal, feared punishment, and stayed far from evil. When the state declined, its people became more eager for commerce and profit than even the people of Zhou.

## 20

From Honggou to the east, and from Mang and Dang to the north down to Juye, this was the region of Liang and Song. Tao and Suixiang were also major cities in this area. In ancient times, Yao once resided at Chengyang, Shun fished by Lake Leizhe, and Tang of the Shang dynasty established his capital in Bo. Their customs still preserved the traditions of ancient kings; they were sincere and had many virtuous men, valuing agriculture. Although lacking in natural resources from mountains or rivers, they could live simply with poor clothing and food while accumulating wealth through careful management.

## 21

Yue and Chu had three distinct customs. From the north of Huai River, Pei, Chen, Ru'nan, to Nanjun, this was known as Xichu. Their customs were bold and impulsive; they easily became angry. The land was poor, with little accumulation of wealth. Jingling, the former capital of Ying, connected to Wucha in the west and had abundant resources from Yunmeng in the east. Chen lay at the crossroads of Chu and Xia, facilitating trade in fish and salt; its people were mostly merchants. Xu, Tong, and Qulv had a clear and strict culture, valuing their word highly and being proud of personal integrity.

## 22

To the east of Pengcheng, including Donghai, Wu, and Guangling, this was known as Dongchu. Their customs were similar to those of Xu and Tong. North of Qu and Zeng, the customs resembled those of Qi. To the south of the Qiantang River was Yue. Wu, since the time of Helu, Chunshen, and Wang Bi, attracted many young men from all over who enjoyed travel. To the east it had abundant sea salt, copper from Zhangshan Mountain, as well as the benefits of the Three Rivers and Five Lakes; thus, it was also a major city in Jiangdong.

## 23

Hengshan, Jiujiang, Jiangnan, Yuzhang, and Changsha formed the southern part of Chu, known as Nanchu. Their customs were largely similar to those in Xichu. After Ying moved its capital to Shouchun, it became another major city. Hefei received tides from both the north and south; it served as a hub for transporting leather goods, preserved fish (bao), and timber. Intermingled with the customs of Minzhong and Gan Yue, Nanchu favored eloquence; its people were skillful in speech but lacked trustworthiness. The Jiangnan region was low-lying and damp, causing men to die young. It had an abundance of bamboo and timber. Yuzhang produced gold, Changsha yielded lead and tin; yet these were only minor resources, insufficient to cover the costs of extraction. From Jiuyi and Cangwu in the south down to Dian'er, their customs were largely similar to Jiangnan, but with even more influence from the Baiyue people. Panyu was also one of their major cities, a hub for pearls, jade, ivory, tortoiseshell, fruits, and textiles.

## 24

Yingchuan and Nanyang were the homelands of the Xia people. The Xia people valued loyalty and simplicity in governance, still preserving the traditions of ancient kings. Yingchuan was known for its sincere and earnest people. In the later years of the Qin dynasty, lawless people were relocated to Nanyang. Nanyang connected westward through Wuguan and Yungan Passes, and received the Han River, Yangtze River, and Huai River from the southeast. Wan was also a major city. Its customs were mixed and people were fond of various pursuits, with many engaged in commerce. Their chivalrous spirit connected them to Yingchuan; thus, even today they are called "Xia people."

## 25

In the world, where resources are scarce or abundant depends on location. The customs and livelihoods of people vary accordingly: in Shandong they consume sea salt; in Shanxi, rock salt. In areas south of the Lingqu Canal and north of Sha, salt is often found naturally. Generally speaking, this is how it has been.

## 26

In summary, the lands of Chu and Yue were vast but sparsely populated. The people ate rice and fish soup; some practiced slash-and-burn agriculture or flooded fields to control weeds. Fruits, mulberries, snails, and clams were abundant, requiring no trade for subsistence. The land was fertile and food plentiful, free from famine. Therefore, the people became lazy and content with a meager existence, lacking accumulation of wealth and often poor. Therefore, south of the Yangtze and Huai rivers, there were no people who froze or starved, but also no families with a fortune of a thousand gold pieces. North of the Yi and Si rivers, the land was suitable for growing five grains, mulberry trees, hemp, and raising six types of livestock. The region had small territory but a dense population, frequently suffering from floods and droughts; thus, the people valued storing resources. For this reason, the states of Qin, Xia, Liang, and Lu favored agriculture and placed great importance on their populace. The regions of Sanhe, Wan, and Chen were similar in this regard, with the added presence of merchants. Qi and Zhao emphasized intelligence and craftsmanship, relying on opportunities for profit. Yan and Dai focused on farming, animal husbandry, and sericulture.

## 27

Viewed in this light, where do the wise men who carefully plan within the halls and temples, debate policies in court, or those virtuous scholars who keep their word, die for principle, or retreat to remote caves seeking fame—where do they ultimately belong? They all return to the pursuit of wealth and abundance. Therefore, upright officials who serve for a long time eventually become wealthy; honest merchants also return with riches. Wealth is part of human nature—an innate desire that requires no learning and is universally shared. Therefore, brave warriors in the army who lead the charge in sieges, break through enemy lines to repel foes, behead generals, and seize banners—those who face arrows and stones, unafraid of fire or boiling water—are motivated by generous rewards. Among young men in the lanes and alleys, those who rob, ambush, kidnap, commit fraud, dig up graves to mint coins, engage in chivalric banditry, form alliances for revenge, pursue hidden targets, and defy laws as they rush into deadly situations—these actions are all ultimately driven by a desire for wealth. Now, the women of Zhao and Zheng beautify themselves, play melodious zithers, drape their long sleeves gracefully, wear elegant shoes, use glances to attract attention, and travel thousands of miles without discrimination for age—these are all done in pursuit of wealth. Leisurely young noblemen adorn their hats and swords, ride in caravans of horses and chariots—these are also displays meant to project wealth and status. Those who hunt, fish, or shoot game rise before dawn and venture out at night, braving frost and snow, rushing through ravines and valleys without fear of fierce beasts—all for the sake of obtaining delicious food. Gambling, racing, cockfighting, and dogfights—those who engage in these activities display pride and always strive to win, because they place great value on avoiding loss. Doctors, herbalists, chefs, and artisans who master their crafts expend great mental effort and skill—this is all for the sake of receiving generous rewards. Officials and scholars who twist legal documents, carve false seals, forge writings, and risk execution by sword or saw—these are all driven by their immersion in bribery and corruption. Farmers, artisans, merchants, and traders who raise livestock and accumulate goods—all seek wealth and increasing profits. This is simply a matter of knowledge and effort; no one will spare their strength to let go of wealth.

## 28

A proverb says: "Firewood is not traded within a hundred li, and rice is not traded beyond a thousand li." Residing in one place for a year, planting grain; After ten years, cultivating trees; After a hundred years, attracting people through virtue. Virtue refers to the character and conduct of individuals. Now there are those who, without official rank or salary, nor income from fiefs, still take pleasure in comparing themselves to them. Such people are called "sufeng." A fief means receiving rent and taxes, with an average of 200 households per year. For a lord governing ten thousand households, the income would be two hundred thousand—expenses for court visits, tribute, and hospitality all come from this. Commoners among farmers, artisans, merchants, and tradesmen generally earn an average annual interest of 2,000. For a family with assets worth one million, the income would be twenty thousand—corvée labor, land taxes, and levies are all drawn from this. The desires for clothing and food can thus be indulged in whatever is beautiful or desirable. Hence it is said: on land, two hundred horse hooves for breeding; a thousand cow hooves and horns; a thousand sheep with sufficient feet; in marshes, a thousand pigs with enough legs; by water, a fish pond yielding a thousand dan of fish; in the mountains, timber from a thousand trees. In Anyi, there are a thousand jujube trees; Yan and Qin have a thousand chestnut trees; Shu, Han, and Jiangling have a thousand tangerine trees; Between the northern reaches of the Huai River and Changshan to the south, as well as between the Yellow River and Ji River, there are a thousand persimmon trees; In Chen and Xia, there are one thousand mu of lacquer cultivation; Qi and Lu have a thousand mu of mulberry and hemp fields; Wei River Valley has a thousand mu of bamboo groves; And in the famous states with cities of ten thousand households, surrounding walls enclosing a thousand mu of fields yielding one dan per mu, or a thousand mu of rhubarb and madder, or a thousand beds of ginger and leeks—such individuals are equal to marquises governing ten thousand households. Indeed, these are the resources that ensure wealth and sufficiency; one need not look to markets or travel to other towns. Sitting idly at home, they await income, maintaining the dignity of a recluse while still securing their needs. If, however, a family is poor and the parents are old, with weak wives and children who cannot afford sacrifices or gatherings during festivals, nor have enough for food, drink, clothing, or adornment to maintain social standing—should such a person feel no shame or disgrace, there would be none more pitiable than them. Therefore, when one has no wealth, he must work hard; with a little fortune, he competes through wit; and once rich, he contends for timing—this is the fundamental principle. Now, if managing one's livelihood does not require risking life to obtain sustenance, then even the virtuous will strive for it. Therefore, wealth gained through agriculture is most esteemed; that from commerce comes next; and wealth obtained through deceit or illegitimate means is the lowest. Without the extraordinary conduct of a recluse, yet remaining poor and lowly while merely talking about benevolence and righteousness is also a source of shame.

## 29

Among ordinary registered households, if one is ten times wealthier than another, the poorer will look down on him; a hundred times richer and he will be feared; a thousand times richer and others will serve him; ten thousand times richer and they will become his servants—this is the natural order of things. To seek wealth from poverty, farming is less effective than craftsmanship; craftsmanship is less effective than commerce. Even the finest embroidery cannot match the profit of standing at a market gate—this saying highlights that commerce and trade are the last resorts for those in poverty. In bustling cities and major towns, one might brew a thousand vats of wine in a year, produce a thousand jars of vinegar and soy sauce, a thousand pots of fermented drinks, tan the hides of a thousand cattle, sheep, and pigs, trade a thousand dan of grain, sell a thousand carts of firewood and straw, own ships stretching a thousand zhang long, possess a thousand trees for timber, ten thousand bamboo poles. They might have a hundred carriages, a thousand ox-drawn carts, a thousand lacquered wooden wares, a thousand jin of copper goods, a thousand dan of plain wood and iron tools or rhubarb and madder dyes, a thousand horse hooves, a thousand cow legs, a thousand pairs of sheep and pigs, a thousand servants with countable fingers, a thousand catties of sinew, horn, vermilion, and cinnabar. They might own a thousand jin of silk, cotton, and fine cloth, a thousand bolts of patterned fabric, a thousand dan of coarse cloth and leather goods, a thousand dou of lacquer, a thousand bundles of malt, yeast, salt, and fermented black beans, a thousand catties each of sea bass, herring, small fish, preserved fish, jujubes, chestnuts, a thousand dan each; three times these amounts for fox and sable furs, a thousand skins of kid or lamb wool, a thousand pieces of felt and mats. Other fruits and vegetables amount to a thousand dan, loans in money totaling ten thousand strings of cash, with expenses for gatherings and travel. Greedy merchants might have three such fortunes; honest ones five—this would be comparable to the wealth of a marquis governing a thousand chariots, representing the general scale. Other miscellaneous businesses that do not yield at least tenfold or twofold returns are not considered true wealth for me.

## 30

Please allow me to briefly describe the ways in which wise men have become wealthy within a thousand li of our time, so that future generations may observe and choose from them.

## 31

The ancestors of Shu's Zhuo family were originally from Zhao; they became wealthy through iron smelting. When the Qin state defeated Zhao, the Zhuo clan was relocated. While others were captured and taken as spoils of war, only a pair from the Zhuo family pulled a cart with their own hands to travel to the place of relocation. Among those relocated, few had remaining wealth; they competed to give money to officials in hopes of being assigned a nearby location, and were settled at Jiamei. Only the Zhuo family said: "This land is narrow and poor. I have heard that beneath Wenshan there are fertile plains, with underground deposits of copper; as long as it lasts, we will never face famine." The people work in the market and can easily become merchants." Thus, they requested to be relocated farther away. They were assigned to Linqiong, and greatly rejoiced. Immediately they began smelting iron in the mountainous region, devised strategies for business operations, attracted the people of Dian and Shu, and became so wealthy that they employed a thousand servants. The pleasures of farming, ponds, hunting, and shooting were comparable to those of a monarch.

## 32

Cheng Zheng was also an immigrant from Shandong, and he too engaged in smelting and casting. He traded with the people who wore topknots, becoming as wealthy as the Zhuo family; both resided in Linqiong.

## 33

The ancestors of the Kong family from Wan were originally from Liang, and they made a living through iron smelting. When Qin attacked Wei, the Kong clan was relocated to Nanyang. They engaged in large-scale iron smelting, constructed irrigation ponds and ditches, maintained caravans of horse-drawn vehicles, traveled among feudal lords, and through commercial profits gained the gifts and recognition usually bestowed upon leisurely young nobles. However, their profits were excessive and surpassed those of stingy merchants. Their family accumulated wealth in the thousands of gold pieces; thus, traveling merchants from Nanyang all emulated Kong's family for their refined and graceful manner.

## 34

The people of Lu were generally frugal, but the Cao Bing family was especially so; they rose to wealth through iron smelting and became fabulously rich. Nevertheless, within the family, from fathers and elder brothers to sons and descendants, they made strict agreements: when in need, one could take from below; when there was surplus, it would be given upward. They borrowed money for trade and conducted commercial activities throughout the commanderies and states. Because of this, many people from Zou and Lu abandoned scholarly pursuits in favor of seeking profit, following the example set by the Cao Bing family.

## 35

In Qi, slaves and captives were generally looked down upon, but Dao Jian alone cherished and valued them highly. Wicked and cunning slaves, a concern for others, were collected by Dao Jian, who employed them to pursue profits in fishing, salt production, and commerce. Some even owned caravans of horse-drawn carts and interacted with magistrates and officials; yet Dao Jian entrusted them more and more. Eventually, he harnessed their abilities and accumulated wealth in the tens of millions. Hence it was said: "Better a noble title than Dao Jian," meaning that he could make even the most unruly slaves self-sufficient and fully utilize their abilities.

## 36

The people of Zhou were already known for being shrewd, but the Shi Shi family was especially so. They operated hundreds of caravans and conducted trade throughout commanderies and states, reaching everywhere without exception. Luoyang, situated between Qi, Qin, Chu, and Zhao, had many poor people who learned from wealthy families. They took pride in long-term commercial ventures, often passing through towns without entering any door. By employing such methods, the Shi Shi family amassed a fortune of seven million gold pieces.

## 37

The ancestors of Ren Shi from Xuanqu served as supervisors in granaries along the Du Road. When Qin fell, heroes and strongmen competed to seize gold and jade, but only the Ren family stored grain in underground cellars. During the Chu-Han confrontation at Xingyang, the people could not farm, and a dan of rice rose to ten thousand gold. Meanwhile, all the gold and jade once held by heroes and strongmen came into the possession of the Ren family, through which they became wealthy. While the rich competed in extravagance, the Rens adopted a humble and frugal lifestyle, focusing their efforts on farming and animal husbandry. When others rushed to buy cheap farmland or livestock, the Ren family alone sought high-quality and valuable assets. Their wealth lasted for generations. Yet the Ren family had strict household rules: they would not wear or eat anything that did not come from their farmland or livestock. If public duties were unfinished, no one could drink alcohol or eat meat. By setting this as an example for the neighborhood, they became wealthy and earned the emperor's respect.

## 38

In the desolate frontier region of Sai, only Qiao Yao had accumulated a thousand horses, twice as many cattle, ten thousand sheep, and grain measured in tens of thousands of dan. During the rebellion of Wu, Chu, and seven other states, marquises and lords in Chang'an who joined military campaigns had to borrow money from lenders. These lenders hesitated because they knew that the fiefs of these nobles were located east of the Guan Mountains, where the outcome of the war was still uncertain. Only the Wu Yan family lent out a thousand gold pieces without hesitation, charging ten times interest. Three months later, Wu and Chu were pacified; within a year, the profits from Wu Yan's loans multiplied tenfold, making them as wealthy as those in Guanzhong.

## 39

Among the rich merchants and traders of Guanzhong, most were descendants of the Tian family—Tian Se and Tian Lan in particular. The Wei Jia Lis, the Anling Dus, and the Du Dus were also fabulously wealthy.

## 40

These are among the most notable examples. None of them became wealthy by holding official ranks, receiving fiefs or salaries, manipulating laws, or committing fraud. Instead, they all relied on bold strategies and timely decisions, adapting to circumstances in order to gain profits. They accumulated wealth through commerce but preserved it with agriculture; they used force when necessary and diplomacy when appropriate. Their methods were flexible yet principled—thus their success was well-earned. As for those who worked hard in farming and animal husbandry, or engaged in craftsmanship, trade, and commerce, using their skills to gain power and profit—some so wealthy they could shake entire commanderies, others counties, and still more the villages and towns—their numbers are too numerous to count.

## 41

Frugality and hard work represent the proper path for managing one's livelihood, yet the wealthy inevitably rely on extraordinary strategies to achieve success. Farming is a humble occupation, but Qin Yang became so rich that his wealth could cover an entire province. Grave-robbing is an illegal act, yet Tian Shu rose to prominence through it. Gambling is a disreputable trade, yet Huan Fa became wealthy from it. Merchants are considered lowly among men, but Yong Le Cheng amassed great wealth through commerce. Selling tallow is a degrading occupation, yet Yong Bo became wealthy with thousands of gold pieces. Selling drinks is a minor trade, yet the Zhang family accumulated tens of millions in wealth. Carving and polishing is a meager skill, yet the Zhi family enjoyed lavish meals from it. Making dried meat is a simple and minor craft, yet the Zhuo family owned multiple horse-drawn carriages through it. Horse medicine is a shallow skill, yet Zhang Li could afford to strike bells in feasts. All of these were the result of their sincerity and single-minded dedication. From this we can see that wealth has no fixed source, and goods have no permanent owners. Those with ability gather fortune like spokes of a wheel converging on the hub; those without talent disintegrate like broken tiles. A family possessing a thousand gold pieces can rival the ruler of an entire commandery, while one with tens of thousands in wealth can share the pleasures of kings. Is this not what is referred to as "seal without rank" (su feng)? No?
