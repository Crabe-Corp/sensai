# Liezhuan of Fu Jin Kuaideng

## 1

Yangling Hou Fu Kuan, as a cavalry general of Wei's Wudaifu rank, followed and became an attendant, rising from Hengyang. He followed in attacking Anyang and Gangli, fought Zhao Ben's army at Kaifeng, and also defeated Yang Xiong's forces at Quyu and Yangwu, beheading twelve enemies. He was granted the title of Qing. He followed to Bashiang. When Pei Gong was established as King of Han, King Wu of Han granted Fu Kuan the title Gongde Jun. He followed into Hanzhong and was promoted to Right Cavalry General. He followed in pacifying the Three Qins, and was granted a fief at Diaoyin. He followed to attack Xiang Ji, waited at Huai, and was awarded the title Tongde Hou. He followed to attack Xiang Guan, Zhou Lan, and Long Ju; the soldiers under his command beheaded one cavalry general at Aoxia, and he was granted additional fiefs.

## 2

Under the command of Huaiyin, he defeated the Qi forces at Lixia and attacked Tian Jie. Under the command of Xiangguo Can, he ravaged Bo and was granted additional fiefs. For pacifying the territory of Qi, he was awarded a covenant seal to be passed down through generations without interruption. He was enfeoffed as Yangling Hou with 2,600 households, replacing his previous fiefs. He served as the Right Chancellor of Qi and prepared for defense in Qi. For five years, he served as Xiangguo (Prime Minister) of Qi.

## 3

In the fourth month, he attacked Chen Xi under the command of Taiwei Bo; as Xiangguo, he replaced Chancellor Pei Kui in attacking Chen Xi. In January, he was transferred to serve as Xiangguo of Dai and commanded garrison troops. After two years, he became Chancellor of Dai and continued commanding the garrison forces.

## 4

He died in the fifth year of Emperor Xiaohui's reign, posthumously honored as Jing Hou. His son Qinghou Jing was enfeoffed; he died in the twenty-fourth year. His son Gonghou Ze was enfeoffed, and died in the twelfth year. His son Hou Yan was enfeoffed. In the thirty-first year, he was executed for conspiring with King Huainan to rebel; his fief was abolished.

## 5

Xinwu Hou Jin Xi, as Zhongjuan, followed and rose from Wanqu. He attacked Jiyang. He defeated Li You's army. He attacked the Qin forces at the south of Bo and northeast of Kaifeng, beheading one cavalry general who commanded a thousand men, killing fifty-seven enemies, capturing seventy-three prisoners, and was awarded a title and fief as Linping Jun. He fought again at the north of Lantian, beheading two Che Sima and one Qi Chang, killing twenty-eight enemies, and capturing fifty-seven prisoners. He arrived at Bashang. When Pei Gong was enfeoffed as King of Han, he granted Jin Xi the title Jianwu Hou and promoted him to Cavalry Commandant.

## 6

He followed in pacifying the three Qins. He was detached to attack Zhang Ping's forces in Longxi from the west, defeating them and pacifying six counties of Longxi. The soldiers under his command beheaded four Che Sima, four Hou, and twelve Qi Chang. He followed eastward to attack Chu, reaching Pengcheng. When the Han army was defeated and retreated, he took up defense at Yongqiu, then went out to attack rebels such as Wang Wu. He raided the territory of Liang, separately leading forces to attack Xing Yue's army at Zinan, defeating it. He personally captured two Duwei from Yue, twelve Sima and Hou, and 4,180 surrendered officers and soldiers. He defeated the Chu forces east of Yingyang. In the third year, he was granted a fief with 4,200 households.

## 7

He was detached to Hedong and attacked Zhao's general Ben Hao at Chaoguo, defeating him. The soldiers under his command captured two cavalry generals and 250 sets of chariots and horses. He followed in attacking Anyang to the east, reaching Jipu, capturing seven counties. He was detached and defeated Zhao's army, capturing two Sima generals, four Hou officers, and 2,400 surrendered officials and soldiers. He followed in capturing Handan. He was detached to capture Pingyang, personally beheading the defending commander; his soldiers killed one Bingshou and one Junshou, and captured Ye. He followed in attacking Chaoguo and Handan, and separately defeated Zhao's forces, pacifying six counties of the Handan commandery. Returning to the military storehouse at Aocang, he defeated Xiang Ji's forces south of Chenggao and cut off Chu's supply routes from Yingyang to Xiangyi. He defeated Xiang Guan's army at Lu Xia. He raided territory east to Zeng, Tan, and Xiapi, south to Qi and Zhuyi. He attacked Xiang Han under Jiyang. Returning, he attacked Xiang Ji at Chen Xia and defeated him. He was detached to pacify Jiangling, where eight officers including the Zhuguo and Daisima surrendered. He personally captured the King of Jiangling and delivered him alive to Luoyang, thereby securing the Nanjun commandery. He followed to Chen, captured King Xiu of Chu, was awarded a covenant seal for generations without interruption, and received a fief of 4,600 households; he was enfeoffed with the title Xinwu Hou.

## 8

As Cavalry Commandant, he followed to attack Dai, attacking Han Xin under Pingcheng, then returned the army to Dongyuan. For his achievements, he was promoted to Chaji General and commanded the cavalry of Liang, Zhao, Qi, Yan, and Chu. He separately attacked Chen Xi's Chancellor Chang, defeated him, and thereby captured Quni. He followed in attacking Qing Bu and achieved success; his fief was increased to 5,300 households. In total, he beheaded ninety enemies and captured one hundred thirty-two prisoners; he separately defeated fourteen armies, surrendered fifty-nine cities, pacified one commandery and one kingdom, and twenty-three counties; captured one king and one Zhuguo, as well as thirty-nine officials from the rank of Erqianshi down to Wubai Shi.

## 9

In the fifth year of Empress Dowager Gao's reign, Jin Xi died and was posthumously honored as Su Hou. His son Ting succeeded him as Marquis. In the twenty-first year, he was found guilty of violating laws concerning his fief's people; in the third year after Empress Dowager Xiaowen, he lost his title and the fief was abolished.

## 10

Kuaideng Hou Xie was a native of Pei; his family name was Zhou. He often served as Emperor Gaozu's chariot companion and, as an attendant, followed in rising from Pei. He reached Bashang, moved west into Shu and Han, then returned to pacify the Three Qins and was granted a fief in Chiyang. Moving eastward, he cut off the supply route and followed to cross Pingyin. He encountered Huaiyin Hou's forces at Xiangguo; his army experienced both victories and defeats but remained loyal without wavering from the emperor's side. He was enfeoffed as Wu Hou, with a fief of 3,300 households.  In the twelfth year of Emperor Gaozu's reign, he was enfeoffed as Kuaideng Hou, replacing his previous fief.

## 11

The emperor wanted to personally attack Chen Xi. Marquis of Kuaideng wept and said, "At the beginning, when Qin attacked and conquered the world, it never did so in person. Now that Your Majesty often goes personally, does this mean there is no one trustworthy enough to send?" The emperor thought it was a sign of affection for him and granted the privilege that he could enter the palace gate without hurrying his steps, and if he killed someone, he would not be put to death.

## 12

In the fifth year of Emperor Xiaowen's reign, Xie died at an old age and was posthumously honored with the title Zhen Hou. His son Chang succeeded him as marquis; however, due to crimes committed, his fief was abolished. In the second year of Emperor Xiaojing's reign, Xie's son Ju was enfeoffed as Marquis of Dai. In the third year of Yuanding, Ju served as Taichang but committed crimes; his fief was abolished as a result.

## 13

Grand Historian said: Yangling Hou Fu Kuan and Xinwu Hou Jin Xi were both granted high ranks. They followed Emperor Gaozu in rising from Shandong, attacked Xiang Ji, killed famous generals, defeated armies and captured cities by the dozen, never experiencing defeat or humiliation; this was also a gift of Heaven. Kuaideng Hou Zhou Xie had a firm and upright character, never suspected by the emperor. Whenever the emperor wished to go somewhere, he would always weep; this kind of heartfelt sorrow shows that he was indeed a loyal and sincere gentleman.
