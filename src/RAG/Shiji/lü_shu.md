# Lü Shu

## 1

A sovereign establishes regulations and laws, sets standards for all things and their rules; everything is based on the Six Laws (liu lü), which are the fundamental basis of all affairs.

## 2

It is especially important for weapons and military equipment, hence the saying "Observing the enemy reveals auspiciousness or misfortune; hearing sounds indicates victory or defeat," which has been an unchanging principle among all past rulers.

## 3

King Wu of Zhou attacked King Zhou, blowing the pitch pipes to listen to sounds, and deduced from Early Spring up to Late Winter. Despite the mingling of killing qi (vital energy), the tone still remained in Gong. Like sounds follow each other; this is the natural order of things, so what is there to find strange?

## 4

Warfare is something that sage rulers use to punish the strong and violent, pacify a chaotic world, level obstacles, and rescue those in peril. Even beasts with teeth or horns that clash when provoked will fight back, so how much more so for human beings who harbor emotions of like and dislike, joy and anger? Joy gives rise to affection; anger brings about harmful actions—this is the nature of human emotion.

## 5

In ancient times, Emperor Huangdi waged the Battle of Zhuolu in order to quell a conflagration; Zhuanxu had the campaign against Gonggong to eliminate water disasters; King Chengtang launched a campaign at Nanchao in order to eradicate chaos under the Xia dynasty. Successive dynasties rise and fall; those who win take control, a mandate bestowed by Heaven.

## 6

After that, distinguished individuals arose in succession: the Jin state employed Jiaofan; Qi employed Prince Wangzi; Wu employed Sun Wu. They clarified military regulations and ensured rewards and punishments were always carried out with integrity. As a result, they became dominant among feudal lords, annexing territories of rival states. Although their achievements did not reach the level of the oaths and proclamations of the Three Dynasties (Xia, Shang, Zhou), yet these individuals gained personal prestige and honored their rulers, achieving great renown in their time—can this not be called glorious? How could this be compared to the shallow scholars of later generations, who are ignorant of major considerations and fail to weigh what is important? They foolishly claim that moral influence alone should prevail and oppose the use of military force. As a result, at best, their rulers suffer humiliation and lose territory; at worst, they face violations and weakening. Yet these scholars stubbornly adhere to such views without change! Whipping and corporal punishment cannot be abolished in the family; punishments cannot be discarded from the state; punitive campaigns cannot be abandoned on a national scale. The difference lies only in whether they are applied skillfully or clumsily, and whether their implementation is appropriate or misguided.

## 7

King Jie of Xia and King Zhou of Yin could wrestle with wolves and jackals, their feet swift enough to match four galloping horses; their bravery was certainly not slight. They won a hundred battles, subduing feudal lords through fear; their authority was by no means weak. The Second Emperor of Qin stationed troops in useless locations and engaged continuous military campaigns on the frontiers; his strength was by no means weak.  He incurred resentment from the Xiongnu and entangled himself in troubles with Yue; his power was by no means scarce.  But when their might was exhausted and power reached its extreme, common people from the lanes and alleys became enemies of the state. The fault lay in their insatiable pursuit of military conquest and unceasing desire for gain.

## 8

Emperor Gaozu gained control over the empire, with three frontiers beyond its borders;  Although kings of major states claimed to be vassal allies, their loyalty as subjects was not yet complete. At that time Emperor Gaozu grew weary of military affairs, and there were also strategies proposed by Xiao He and Zhang Liang; therefore, he ceased warfare for a period of rest, relying on loose control without full preparedness.

## 9

らい Until the reign of Emperor Xiaowen, General Chen Wu and others proposed: "Nanyue and Chaoxian had been subjects since the time of the unified Qin dynasty. Later they amassed troops to block strategic passes, hesitating and watching for opportunities." At Emperor Gaozu's time, the empire had just been pacified, and the people enjoyed a slight sense of peace; it was not yet possible to launch another military campaign. Now, Your Majesty shows benevolence and kindness in governing the people, bestowing grace upon all within the seas; it is appropriate to take advantage of the soldiers' and civilians' willingness to serve, and launch a campaign against rebellious factions to consolidate the borders." Emperor Xiaowen said: "I am capable of wearing official robes and hats, but I have not thought about this. During the turmoil of the Lu clan, meritorious ministers and members of the royal family jointly occupied positions without shame or disgrace; I have always been trembling with fear, worried that affairs might not be brought to a successful conclusion. Moreover, arms are dangerous instruments; even if one achieves what is desired, their use still causes exhaustion and suffering—how much more so for the people in distant regions? Furthermore, my late father emperor knew that overburdening the people was not advisable, and therefore did not consider it a priority. How can I claim to be capable myself? Now the Xiongnu have invaded internally, military officials have achieved no merit, and border civilians—fathers and sons—have borne arms for a long time; I often feel deeply moved with sorrow and pain, never forgetting it on any day. Now we have not yet been able to eliminate the threat; I wish for now to strengthen our borders and establish outposts, foster peace through envoys, and bring tranquility to the northern frontier—this would be a great achievement. And do not discuss military matters." Thus, the common people were free from burdens both within and beyond the borders, able to rest their shoulders on tilling fields. The empire became prosperous, with grain so abundant that it cost only ten or more coins per measure; roosters crowed and dogs barked, while smoke and fire stretched for thousands of miles—can this not be described as a time of harmony and happiness!

## 10

Grand Historian Sima Qian said: During Emperor Wendi's reign, when the empire had just emerged from turmoil and fire, the people were content with their work. Because he followed the people's desires without disturbance or disruption, the common folk thus found peace. Even men in their sixties and seventies had never gone to marketplaces; they roamed about and played like children. This was indeed the kind of virtuous gentleman praised by Confucius!

## 11

The Book [of Documents] says, "Seven Standards," and twenty-eight lodgings. Pitch pipes and the calendar system are how Heaven communicates the qi of the Five Elements and Eight Correct Directions, and through which Heaven brings all things to maturity. The "lodgings" refer to where the sun and moon reside. "Lodging" means the dispersion of qi.

## 12

The Feng of Bu Zhou resides in the northwest, presiding over life and death. Dongbi is located east of the Feng of Bu Zhou, responsible for opening up vital energy and directing it toward the east. Up to Ying Shi. Ying Shi is in charge of nurturing the yang qi within the womb and giving birth to it. To the east up to Wei. Wei means collapse. It refers to the decline of yang qi, hence it is called Wei. This corresponds to the tenth month, when the pitch pipe Yingzhong is in tune. Yingzhong signifies yang qi's response, but it does not yet take action. It corresponds to Hai among the twelve earthly branches. Hai means comprehensive or complete. It indicates that yang qi is stored underground, hence it is comprehensive.

## 13

The Feng of Guangmo resides in the north. Guangmo means that yang qi is below, and yin suppresses yang's expansion; thus it is called Guangmo. To the east up to Xu. Xu means capable of being full or empty. It indicates that yang qi, in winter, curls and hides within emptiness; on the day of Winter Solstice, one yin descends to hide while one yang ascends to expand—thus it is called Xu. To the east up to Xunv. It means that all things are in a state of transformation, where yin and yang qi have not yet separated but still interact closely—thus it is called Xunv. This corresponds to the eleventh month, when the pitch pipe Huangzhong is in tune. Huangzhong signifies yang qi emerging from the yellow springs below. It corresponds to Zi among the twelve earthly branches.  Zi means nourishment; Nourishment refers to the idea that all things grow and develop from below. It corresponds to Ren and Gui among the ten heavenly stems. Ren means "to bear" or "to carry," indicating that yang qi supports and nurtures all things from below. Gui means "measure" or "assess," signifying that all things can be measured, hence it is called Gui. To the east up to Qianniu. Qianniu means that yang qi draws all things outward, bringing them forth. Niu means "emerge" or "break through," indicating that although the earth may be frozen, life can still break through and grow. Niu refers to plowing and planting, cultivating all things. To the east up to Jianxing. Jianxing means establishing all living things. This corresponds to the twelfth month, when the pitch pipe Dali is in tune. Dali means... It corresponds to Chou among the twelve earthly branches.

## 14

Tiaofeng resides in the northeast, presiding over bringing forth all things. "Tiao" means to regulate and bring forth all things orderly; hence it is called Tiaofeng. To the south up to Ji. Ji means that the roots of all things spread out like a chessboard, hence it is called Ji. This corresponds to the first month, when the pitch pipe Taicu is in tune. Taicu means that all things sprout and grow together; hence it is called Taicu. It corresponds to Yin among the twelve earthly branches.  Yin means that all things begin to sprout in a worm-like manner, hence it is called Yin. To the south up to Wei, meaning that all things first grow like tails. To the south up to Xin, meaning that when all things begin to grow, they have a blooming heart or core. To the south up to Fang. Fang means "doorway," indicating that all things have their gateways; when they reach the door, they emerge outward.

## 15

Mingshu Feng resides in the east.  Mingshu means that all things become clearly visible and fully emerge. This corresponds to the second month, when the pitch pipe Jiazhong is in tune.  Jiazhong means that yin and yang intermingle closely. It corresponds to Mao among the twelve earthly branches.  Mao means "luxuriant," indicating that all things flourish and grow abundantly. It corresponds to Jia and Yi among the ten heavenly stems.  Jia means that all things break through their shells or husks to emerge; Yi means that all things grow with a rustling sound. To the south up to Di. Di means that all things arrive or reach their fullness. To the south up to Kang. Kang means that all things become prominent and visible. To the south up to Jiao. Jiao means that all things have branches or structures resembling horns. This corresponds to the third month, when the pitch pipe Guxian is in tune.  Guxian means that all things are cleansed and given new life. It corresponds to Chen among the twelve earthly branches.  Chen means that it is the time when all things emerge and become active.

## 16

The Qingsheng wind resides in the southeast, governing the blowing of winds that carry all things westward. [Up to] Zhen. Zhen means that all things grow increasingly large and appear vigorous and thriving. To the west up to Yi. Yi means that all things possess wings or feathers. This corresponds to the fourth month, when the pitch pipe Zhonglv is in tune.  Zhonglv means that all things complete their journey and move westward. It corresponds to Si among the twelve earthly branches.  Si means that the yang energy has reached its fullness and is now complete. To the west up to Qixing. Qixing means that yang numbers are completed at seven, hence it is called Qixing. To the west up to Zhang. Zhang means that all things expand and stretch outward. To the west up to Zhu. Zhu means that all things begin to wane, and yang energy descends downward; hence it is called Zhu. This corresponds to the fifth month, when the pitch pipe Ruibin is in tune.  Ruibin means that yin energy is young and weak; hence it is called Rui; Yang energy wanes and no longer governs affairs, so it is called Bin.

## 17

The Jingfeng wind resides in the south. Jing means that yang energy reaches its end, hence it is called Jingfeng. It corresponds to Wu among the twelve earthly branches.  Wu means that yin and yang intersect, hence it is called Wu. It corresponds to Bing and Ding among the ten heavenly stems.  Bing means that yang's path becomes prominent and clear, hence it is called Bing; Ding means the time when all things reach their vigorous prime, hence it is called Ding. To the west up to Hu. Hu means that all things begin to wither and approach death. To the west up to Lang. Lang means that all things can be measured and cut, hence it is called Lang.

## 18

The Liangfeng wind resides in the southwest, governing the earth. Earth means to sink and deprive all things of their energy. This corresponds to the sixth month, when the pitch pipe Linzhong is in tune.  Linzhong means that all things approach death and appear in a withering state. It corresponds to Wei among the twelve earthly branches.  Wei means that all things have matured and possess flavor or substance. To the north up to Fa. Fa means that all things' energy is depleted and they can be cut down or punished. To the north up to Shen. Shen means that all things can be compared or measured, hence it is called Shen. This corresponds to the seventh month, when the pitch pipe Yize is in tune.  Yize means that yin energy harms and damages all things. It corresponds to Shen among the twelve earthly branches.  Shen means that yin takes charge, and it harms all things; hence it is called Shen.  To the north up to Zhuo. Zhuo means "contact," indicating that all things come into contact with death; hence it is called Zhuo.  To the north up to Liu. Liu means that yang energy lingers and delays, hence it is called Liu.  This corresponds to the eighth month, when the pitch pipe Nanlv is in tune.  Nanlv means that yang energy retreats and enters storage.  It corresponds to You among the twelve earthly branches.  You means that all things have aged, hence it is called You.

## 19

The Changhe wind resides in the west. Chang means to lead or initiate; He means to store away or conceal. It indicates that yang energy leads all things, and they are concealed in the yellow springs (the underworld). It corresponds to Geng and Xin among the ten heavenly stems.  Geng means that yin energy strengthens all things, hence it is called Geng;  Xin means the time when all things experience a sharp or intense growth phase, hence it is called Xin.  To the north up to Wei. Wei means that yang energy is about to be stored, and all things appear as if they are being contained or gathered; hence it is called Wei.  To the north up to Lou. Lou means calling all things inward, drawing them in.  To the north up to Kui. Kui means it governs poison, stings, and kills all things; it stores them away as Kui does.  This corresponds to the ninth month, when the pitch pipe Wuye is in tune.  Wuye means that yin energy has become dominant and active, while yang energy has no remainder; hence it is called Wuye.  It corresponds to Xu among the twelve earthly branches.  Xu means that all things have perished completely, hence it is called Xu.

## 20

Pitch pipe numbers: Eighty-one (nine times nine) is used as the fundamental tone Gong. One-third of it is subtracted, leaving fifty-four to be used as Zhi. One-third of the remainder is added, making seventy-two for Shang. One-third of it is subtracted again, leaving forty-eight to be used as Yu. One-third of the remainder is added once more, making sixty-four for Jiao. Huangzhong measures eight cun and one-seventh of a fen in length; it corresponds to Gong. Dalu is seven cun, five fen, and two-thirds (one-third) [two] of a fen long. Taicu measures seven cun, ten [seven] fen, and two parts in length; it corresponds to Jiao. Jiazhong is six cun, seven [one] fen, and one-third of a fen long.  Guxian measures six cun, ten [seven]fen, and four parts in length; it corresponds to Yu.  Zhonglv is five cun, nine fen, and two-thirds of a fen long; it corresponds to Zhi.  Ruibin measures five cun, six fen, and two [one]-thirds of a fen in length.  Linzhong is five cun, ten [seven] phen, and four parts long; it corresponds to Jiao.  Yize measures five cun, two-thirds of a fen, [and four fen]; it corresponds to Shang.  Nanlv is four cun, ten [seven]phen, and eight parts long; it corresponds to Zhi.  Wuye measures four cun, four fen, and two-thirds of a phen in length.  Yingzhong is four cun, two fen, and two-thirds of one fen long; it corresponds to Yu.

## 21

Si is one hundred and twenty-eight two-hundred-and-forty-thirds of a fen.  Wu is five hundred and twelve seven-hundred-and-twenty-ninths of a fen.  Wei is one thousand and twenty-four two-thousand-one-hundred-and-eighty-sevenths of a fen.  Shen is four thousand nine hundred and sixteen six-thousand-five-hundred-sixty-firsts of a fen.  You is eight thousand one hundred and ninety-two ten-thousand-nine-thousand-six-hundred-and-eighty-third parts of a fen.  Xu is thirty-two thousand seven hundred and sixty-eight fifty-thousand-nine-thousand-four-nineths of a fen.  Hai is sixty-five thousand five hundred and thirty-six seventeen-ten-thousand-seven-thousand-one-hundred-forty-seventh parts of a fen.

## 22

The method for generating Huangzhong states: For the lower generation, double its actual value and triple its divisor. For the upper generation, quadruple its actual value and triple its rule. Shang is nine, Shang is eight, Yu is seven, Jiao is six, Gong is five, Zhi is nine. Set one and multiply it by three times nine to obtain the divisor. If the actual value equals the divisor, the length is one cun. Whenever nine cun are obtained, it is called "the Gong of Huangzhong." Therefore, it is said that pitch begins with Gong and ends with Jiao; numbers begin at one, end at ten, and are completed in three; the qi begins with the winter solstice, completes a cycle, and is reborn.

## 23

The divine originates from nothingness; form arises from existence. Form follows number, and form gives rise to sound. Therefore it is said: the divine governs qi, and qi conforms to form. When forms are categorized according to their nature, there are things that can be classified together. Some have no form and thus cannot be categorized; some share the same form and belong to the same category. Categories can be arranged, and categories can be recognized. The sage understands the distinctions between heaven and earth and their knowledge; therefore, he proceeds from existence to non-existence, thereby attaining subtleties as fine as qi and as faint as sound. Yet the sage relies on the divine to preserve it; even if subtle, its effects must manifest in reality. Those who examine and understand this principle are truly enlightened. Without the sage's mind to harness wisdom, who could preserve the divine of heaven and earth and complete the essence of form? The divine is something that things receive but cannot know (or perceive) its coming and going; therefore, the sage reveres it and wishes to preserve it. Only by wishing to preserve it does the divine remain present. Because one wishes to preserve it, nothing is more precious than this.

## 24

Tai Shigong said: (Therefore) [In] the rotating armillary sphere with jade balance to regulate the seven officials, that is, the twenty-eight mansions of heaven and earth. Ten mothers, twelve sons; the pitch standards have been regulated since ancient times. Establishing pitch standards, regulating calendars, and creating measures of days are things that can be relied upon for measurement. To align with symbols and seasons, to harmonize the Way and virtue—this is what is meant by this.
