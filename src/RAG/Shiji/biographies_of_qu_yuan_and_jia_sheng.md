# Biographies of Qu Yuan and Jia Sheng

## 1

Qu Yuan was a man named Ping, and he was of the same surname as the State of Chu. He served as Zutu (left minister) to King Huai of Chu. He was widely learned and had a strong memory, clear in matters of governance and disorder, and skilled in rhetoric and diplomacy. Inside the court, he advised the king on state affairs and issued decrees; outside, he received guests and responded to envoys from various feudal lords. The king greatly trusted him.

## 2

Shangguan Dafu was of the same rank as Qu Yuan, and although they both vied for royal favor, Shangguan envied his abilities. King Huai ordered Qu Yuan to draft a constitution; Qu Ping had not yet completed the preliminary draft. Shangguan Dafu saw it and wanted to take credit for it, but Qu Ping did not allow him. Therefore, he slandered him by saying: "The king has ordered Qu Ping to draft the edicts; everyone knows this. Every time an order is issued, Ping boasts of its merit, (saying) that 'no one else could have done it except me.'" The king became angry and gradually distanced himself from Qu Ping.

## 3

Qu Ping was distressed by the king's failure to listen wisely, by the obscuring influence of flatterers and sycophants, by the harm caused by crooked men to public affairs, and by the fact that upright men were not tolerated. Thus, in sorrow and deep thought, he composed "Li Sao." "Li Sao" means "to part with sorrow." Heaven is the origin of humanity; parents are the root of a person. When people find themselves in dire straits, they return to their roots; thus, when weary and exhausted from labor, they have never failed to call upon Heaven; when suffering pain or deep sorrow, they have never ceased to invoke their parents. Qu Ping walked uprightly along the righteous path and devoted his loyalty and wisdom to serve his king, but slanderers sowed discord between them. One could say he had reached the depths of adversity. Being faithful yet suspected, loyal yet slandered—how could one not feel resentment? Qu Ping's composition of "Li Sao" arose from his own feelings of grievance. The Guo Feng section of the Book of Songs portrays love and desire without descending into licentiousness, while Xiao Ya expresses grievances and complaints without falling into disorder. As for "Li Sao," it may be said to embody both of these qualities. It praises the emperors Shun and Yu above, cites Duke Huan of Qi below, and recounts Tang and Wu in between, using these to satirize worldly affairs. It clearly demonstrates the vastness and grandeur of morality, as well as the principles governing order and chaos; nothing is left unexpressed. Its style is concise, its words subtle, its aspirations pure, and its conduct upright. It cites small examples but conveys great meanings; it uses nearby references to reveal distant truths. Because his aspirations were pure, he used fragrant objects as metaphors in his writing. Because of his upright conduct, even after death he was not accepted and remained estranged from the world. He emerged from the mire and filth like a cicada shedding its husk, floating freely beyond the dust of the world. He did not suffer the corruption of worldly impurities; he remained pure, untouched by mud yet unsoiled. With such a spirit, it could be said that his brilliance might rival even the sun and moon.

## 4

After Qu Ping fell out of favor, later the State of Qin wanted to attack Qi. Qi had formed an alliance with Chu; King Huai was troubled by this and therefore ordered Zhang Yi to feign leaving Qin, offering rich gifts and swearing allegiance to Chu, saying: "Qin greatly resents Qi. If Qi and Chu remain allied, then if Chu truly severs ties with Qi, Qin is willing to cede six hundred li of land in Shangyu." King Huai of Chu was greedy and trusted Zhang Yi, so he broke relations with Qi and sent envoys to Qin to receive the land. Zhang Yi deceived them by saying: "I had agreed with Your Majesty for six li, not six hundred li." The envoys of Chu were furious and left. They returned to report this to King Huai. King Huai was enraged, and raised a large army to attack Qin. Qin mobilized its troops and defeated the Chu army at Dan and Xi, killing eight thousand soldiers and capturing Qu Gai, a general of Chu. Thus they seized the Hanzhong region from Chu. King Huai then mobilized all the troops within the country to launch a deep offensive against Qin, engaging in battle at Lan Tian. The State of Wei heard about this and launched an attack on Chu as far as Deng. The Chu army, alarmed by the threat, withdrew from Qin back to their own territory. Qi was ultimately angry and did not come to the aid of Chu; as a result, Chu found itself in dire straits.

## 5

The following year, Qin ceded the Hanzhong territory to Chu to secure peace. King of Chu said: "I do not desire the land; I would be content if only Zhang Yi were in my hands." Zhang Yi heard this and said: "To exchange one person, myself, for the territory of Hanzhong, I shall go to Chu." Upon arriving in Chu, he used generous gifts and relied on the influential minister Jin Shang. He also fabricated devious arguments to sway Zheng Xiu, King Huai's favored concubine. King Huai ultimately listened to Zheng Xiu and released Zhang Yi again. At this time, Qu Ping had already been distanced from court affairs and was no longer in office. He was sent as an envoy to Qi but returned early, advising King Huai: "Why not kill Zhang Yi?" King Huai regretted his decision and tried to pursue Zhang Yi, but it was too late.

## 6

Later, the feudal lords jointly attacked Chu, decisively defeating them and killing their general Tang Mei.

## 7

At that time, King Zhao of Qin had married into the royal family of Chu and wished to meet with King Huai in person. King Huai wanted to go, but Qu Ping said: "Qin is a country of tigers and wolves; it cannot be trusted. It would be better not to go." King Huai's youngest son, Zi Lan, advised the king to proceed: "How can we sever good relations with Qin?" King Huai finally went. Upon entering Wu Guan, Qin's hidden troops cut off his retreat and detained King Huai, demanding territorial concessions in exchange for his release. King Huai was furious and refused to comply. He fled to the State of Zhao, but Zhao did not admit him. Returning to Qin, he eventually died there and was returned for burial in his homeland.

## 8

His eldest son, King Qingxiangwang, ascended the throne and appointed Zi Lan, Qu Ping's younger brother, as Lingyi (Minister of Justice). The people of Chu blamed Zi Lan for having advised King Huai to enter Qin and not return.

## 9

Qu Ping, resentful of this, even though exiled, still looked back with affection toward the State of Chu and remained devoted to King Huai. He never forgot his desire for return, hoping that one day the king might awaken to reason and the customs of society change. His concern for the king and the state, as well as his desire to restore order, is expressed three times in a single work. Yet ultimately there was nothing he could do; thus it was impossible for him to return, and this finally revealed that King Huai never awoke from his delusion. Monarchs, whether foolish or wise, virtuous or unworthy, all desire to seek loyal ministers for themselves and appoint capable men as their assistants. Yet the downfall of states and families continues without end, while enlightened rulers and well-governed nations are rarely seen across generations—this is because those who were called "loyal" proved disloyal, and those deemed "capable" turned out unworthy. King Huai, not understanding the distinction of a loyal minister, was internally deceived by Zheng Xiu and externally tricked by Zhang Yi. He distanced himself from Qu Ping while trusting Shangguan Dafu and Lingyi Zi Lan. His armies were defeated, his territories reduced; he lost six commanderies, died as a foreigner in Qin, and became the laughingstock of all under heaven. This was the calamity of not knowing people's true nature. The "Yi Jing" says: "A well that leaks and cannot provide water causes sorrow in my heart, but it is still possible to draw water from it." "If the king is wise, he will receive its blessings." If a king lacks wisdom, how can he possibly deserve blessings!

## 10

Lingyi Zi Lan heard of this and became greatly enraged. He ultimately had Shangguan Dafu slander Qu Yuan to King Qingxiang, who in anger exiled him.

## 11

Qu Yuan arrived at the riverside and walked along its banks with disheveled hair, reciting poetry as he went. His face was haggard and his appearance emaciated. A fisherman saw him and asked: "Are you not Sanlu Daifu?" Why have you come to this pass?" Qu Yuan said: "The whole world is muddied, yet I alone remain pure; all are drunk, but I alone am sober. That is why I have been exiled." The fisherman said: "A sage does not become fixated on things but can adapt to the changing world. If the whole world is muddied, why not follow its current and ride its waves?" "If all are drunk, why not partake of their lees and sip their thin wine?" "Why hold fast to jade and pearls only to bring about your own exile?" Qu Yuan said: "I have heard that one who has just washed his hair must comb out the tangles, and one who has bathed must shake off the water. Who would allow a pure body to be tainted by filth?" "I would rather throw myself into the river and be buried in the belly of a fish than allow my pure whiteness to be stained by the muddied customs of this world!"

## 12

He then composed the rhapsody "Huasha." Its verses read:

## 13

In the mellow month of early summer, the grass and trees are luxuriant. My sorrow is endless in grief; I am hurriedly driven southward. The scenery flickers and darkens, the silence deep and profound. My grievances are twisted in my heart; I have long suffered from sorrow and injustice. I restrain my emotions and aspirations, bowing low to suppress myself.

## 14

I have rounded the square corners of my principles, yet I still adhere to my standards. To change one's original nature is despised by gentlemen. I remain faithful to the rules and measurements, my former standards unchanged; My inner integrity is of great value—this is what noble men hold in high esteem. If a skillful craftsman does not carve, who will discern the true measure? Deep truths remain hidden; people mistakenly say they are obscure. Even the keen eyes of Lilu cannot see clearly, and the blind man says there is no light. They turn white to black, upside down as if it were right side up. The phoenix is caged while the chickens and pheasants strut about. Jade and stones are mixed together, valued as one. The faction of petty men is envious; they do not know what I treasure in my heart.

## 15

Though heavily burdened and full of purpose, I am stuck and unable to proceed; Holding jade in my heart and pearls in my hand, yet in adversity I cannot show them. The dogs of the village bark together; they bark at what is strange to them; They slander the swift horse and doubt the strong ox—this is the nature of vulgar minds. My refined character and inner depth are not understood by the crowd, who do not recognize my unique brilliance; Though I am rich in talent and virtue, no one knows what I possess. Valuing benevolence and righteousness highly, I regard modesty as richness; Zhonghua cannot be met with, who would know my composure! There are naturally things that do not harmonize; how could they understand the reason? Tang and Yu have long passed away, their time is remote and cannot be emulated. I restrain my anger and resentment, suppressing my heart to strengthen myself; Though suffering sorrow I do not change, I wish for a model of steadfast will. I proceed northward and stop; the day is dimming toward dusk; Bearing sorrow and grief, I am confined by great calamity.

## 16

Chorus: Vast are the Yuan and Xiang rivers, their waters flow in separate streams. The path is remote and veiled; the way is distant and obscure. I have often sighed in sorrow, forever sighing with regret. The world does not know me; human hearts cannot be explained. Holding sincere feelings and noble virtue, I alone have no equal. When Bole is dead, where will the steed find its appraiser? Life is given by fate; each has their own place. Steady my heart and broaden my will, what have I to fear? I was once wounded and grieved; forever sighing in sorrow. The world is confused and does not know me, my heart cannot be explained. Knowing death cannot be avoided, I wish to face it without regret. I will clearly inform the gentleman; I shall take him as my kindred spirit.

## 17

Thus, holding a stone, he threw himself into Miluo River and died.

## 18

After Qu Yuan's death, the State of Chu had men like Song Yu, Tang Le, and Jing Cai; they all loved literary composition and were renowned for their fu (rhapsodies); Yet they all followed in the footsteps of Qu Yuan's graceful rhetoric, but none dared to offer direct remonstrance. After that, the State of Chu gradually declined and was eventually destroyed by the Qin after several decades.

## 19

More than a hundred years after Qu Yuan drowned in Miluo River, the Han dynasty had Jia Sheng, who served as Taifu (grand tutor) to King Changsha. When he passed by Xiang River, he wrote and cast a letter to mourn Qu Yuan.

## 20

Jia Sheng's courtesy name was Yi; he was a native of Luoyang. At the age of eighteen, he became known in his command for being able to recite poetry and compose writings. Wu Tingwei was governor of Henan Commandery; having heard of his talent, he summoned him to be placed under his patronage and treated him with great favor. At the beginning of Emperor Xiaowen's reign, he heard that Governor Wu of Henan Commandery had achieved peace and order ranked first in the empire. Since Wu was from the same native place as Li Si and had often studied under him, the emperor summoned him to serve as Tingwei (Minister of Justice). The Minister of Justice then said that Jia Sheng was young but had a thorough knowledge of the writings of various schools of thought. Emperor Wen summoned him and appointed him as a Bohu (Doctor).

## 21

At that time, Jia Sheng was in his early twenties, the youngest among them. Whenever an imperial decree summoned a discussion and the older scholars could not speak, Jia Sheng would fully respond to them, addressing each point as they wished. The other scholars then regarded him as capable, beyond their reach. Emperor Xiaowen was pleased with him and promoted him rapidly; within a year he rose to the rank of Taizhong Dafu.

## 22

Jia Sheng believed that since the founding of the Han dynasty to Emperor Xiaowen, more than twenty years had passed; the empire was at peace and harmony. Thus, it was time to reform the calendar, change the color of ceremonial robes, revise laws and systems, establish official titles, and revive rites and music. He therefore drafted all these matters in detail, advocating yellow as the imperial color, using five as a key number, establishing new official titles, completely replacing the Qin dynasty's legal system. At first after ascending the throne, Emperor Xiaowen was modest and hesitant, not yet ready to act. All the revised laws and decrees, as well as the order for marquises to return to their fiefs, originated from Jia Sheng's proposals. Thus, the Son of Heaven discussed appointing Jia Sheng to a position among the high ministers. Men like Jiao, Guan, Dongyang Hou, and Feng Jing all resented him. They slandered Jia Sheng by saying: "This man from Luoyang is young and inexperienced; he seeks to monopolize power and create disorder in many affairs." Thus, the Son of Heaven later grew distant from him and did not adopt his proposals. He was then appointed as Taifu (Grand Tutor) to King Changsha.

## 23

After Jia Sheng had taken leave and departed, he heard that Changsha was low-lying and damp. He thought his life would not last long; furthermore, being exiled from the capital made him feel discontented. When he crossed Xiang River, he composed a fu (rhapsody) to mourn Qu Yuan. His words were:

## 24

I have received your grace, yet I await punishment in Changsha. I have heard of Qu Yuan, who drowned himself in Miluo River. I entrust my thoughts to the Xiang River and respectfully mourn the Master. Meeting a world without limits of injustice, he thus lost his life. Alas! What misfortune to meet such an ill-omened time! The phoenix hides and flees, while the owl soars; the vulgar rise to prominence, while slanderers achieve their desires. The virtuous are dragged backward, while integrity is uprooted and overturned. The world calls Boyi greedy, and calls Dazhi upright; Moye swords are blunt while leaden blades are sharp. Alas, how strange! To be born without cause! Discarding the Zhou tripod to treasure a hollow gourd, mounting a tired ox while using lame donkeys as side horses; the steed hangs its ears and pulls a salt cart. A scholar's hat is worn with straw sandals—this cannot last long. Alas, the sorrowful Master! Alone he suffers this misfortune!

## 25

He said: It is over. The country no longer knows me; who shall I speak to about my sorrow? The phoenix drifts high and distant, for it naturally withdraws far away. The dragon of the nine depths hides in deep waters to preserve itself. It conceals itself in quiet obscurity; how could it follow ants and leeches? What is valued is the divine virtue of a sage, who distances himself from a corrupt world to hide. If Qiji (a noble steed) could be tethered and caged, how different would it be from a dog or sheep! To suffer such injustice and leave this blame is indeed the Master's misfortune! Surveying all nine provinces to find a wise ruler, why must one cling to this capital? The phoenix soars above ten thousand feet, looking for virtue and descending. Seeing the peril of petty virtue, it flaps its wings and flies away. 龙头 Those shallow ditches cannot hold a fish large enough to swallow a boat! The great sturgeon that spans the rivers and lakes is bound to be controlled by ants and worms.

## 26

Jia Sheng served as Taifu of King Changsha for three years, when an owl flew into his residence and perched in the corner. The people of Chu called the owl "fu." Since Jia Sheng had been exiled to Changsha, and since Changsha was low-lying and humid, he thought his life would not be long. Grieved by this, he composed a fu to console himself. His words were:

## 27

In the year Dan'e, in the first month of spring, on the Gengzi day at dusk, an owl flew into my house and perched in a corner, appearing very leisurely. 龙头 I was surprised by this strange creature's arrival and investigated its meaning; consulting the divination texts revealed what it foretold. It said, "When a wild bird enters one's home, the master will depart." I asked the owl: "Where shall I go when I leave?" "Tell me if it is auspicious," I said, "but speak of disaster if it is ill-omened." Speak in measured words and tell me the time." The owl sighed, raised its head, flapped its wings, unable to speak but offering a response in meaning.

## 28

All things change and transform; there is no rest or pause. They drift forward, shifting place, sometimes pushed back in return. Form and energy transform continuously; change follows transformation. The profound mystery is boundless; how can it be fully described! Misfortune leans on fortune, and fortune hides misfortune within. Sorrow and joy gather at the same door; good and ill omens share the same domain. The State of Wu was strong, yet King Fuchai failed. łoż Yue took refuge at Kuaiji, and Goujian became the hegemon of his age. This journey was completed, yet he ultimately suffered five punishments; Fu Yue, once a slave, became the prime minister of King Wu Ding. Misfortune and fortune are like twisted ropes—how different can they be? Fate cannot be explained; who knows its limit? Water, when agitated, becomes turbulent; an arrow, when loosed with force, flies far. All things swirl and press upon each other, shaking and turning in cycles. Clouds rise and rain falls; confusion and disorder intermingle. The great Dao governs all things, boundless and formless without limit. Heaven cannot be reasoned with; the Way cannot be schemed against. Fate is determined by numbers and time—how can one know when it will come?

## 29

Indeed, heaven and earth are the furnace, and creation itself the craftsman; Yin and Yang serve as fuel, and all things as copper. Union and dispersion, increase and decrease—how can there be fixed laws? Ten thousand transformations occur; never has there been an end. Suddenly becoming human, how can one grasp or shape it? Transformed into another thing, what cause is there for sorrow! Small knowledge is selfish; it deems others low and values itself highly. The enlightened person sees broadly; nothing among things is unacceptable. Greedy men pursue wealth, and valiant men chase fame; Boasters die for power, while the common people rely on life. Those bound by worldly concerns rush this way and that; The great person does not bend, regarding myriad changes as one. Bound men are shackled by convention, like prisoners in chains; The perfected person transcends things and dwells alone with the Dao. Common people are confused, accumulating likes and dislikes in their minds; The true person is indifferent, resting alone with the Dao. Abandon knowledge and forget form; transcend and lose oneself; Vast and boundless, suddenly free—soar with the Dao. With the current it departs; at a sandbar it rests; Let the body go and entrust fate, not seeking for oneself. Life is like drifting; death is like rest; Calm as the still depths of a deep abyss, free as a boat unbound by moorings. Not cherishing life for its own sake, they dwell in emptiness and drift; The virtuous person is unburdened, knowing fate without sorrow. Trivial matters vanish like mist—what cause have they to doubt!

## 30

After more than a year, Jia Sheng was summoned for an audience. Emperor Xiaowen was just receiving the sacrificial offering and sat in the Xuan Shi Hall. The emperor, moved by matters of spirits, asked about their essence. Jia Sheng thus fully explained the state of affairs as they were. Until midnight, Emperor Wen moved his mat closer to Jia Sheng. After the audience ended, he said: "I have not seen Jia Sheng for a long time and thought I surpassed him; now it seems I fall short." After a short while, the emperor appointed Jia Sheng as Taifu of Prince Liang Huaiwang. Prince Liang Huaiwang was Emperor Wen's youngest son, beloved and fond of books; thus he ordered Jia Sheng to serve as his tutor.

## 31

Emperor Wen again enfeoffed four sons of Prince Liu Li of Huainan as marquises. Jia Sheng advised against this, believing that the source of future troubles lay in this action. Jia Sheng repeatedly submitted memorials, stating that some feudal lords controlled multiple commanderies, which was not in accordance with ancient systems; they could be gradually reduced. Emperor Wen did not listen.

## 32

After several years, Prince Liang Huaiwang fell from his horse while riding and died, leaving no heir. Jia Sheng blamed himself for failing as a tutor; he wept for more than a year and also died. At the time of his death, Jia Sheng was thirty-three years old. After Emperor Xiaowen died and Emperor Xiwu took the throne, two grandsons of Jia Sheng were appointed as commandery administrators. Among them, Jia Jia was most devoted to learning; he inherited his family's legacy and corresponded with me. By the time of Emperor Xiaozhuang, they were ranked among the Nine Ministers.

## 33

Grand Historian said: I read "Li Sao," "Tian Wen," "Zhao Hun," and "Ai Ying," and was moved by his aspirations. When I visited Changsha, gazing at the depths where Qu Yuan drowned himself, I could not help but weep and imagine his character. When I saw Jia Sheng mourning him, I was also puzzled: with such talent, why did Qu Yuan wander among the feudal lords and find no country willing to accept him, leading himself into this fate? Reading "Fu Wu Fu," which equates life and death and treats departure and staying lightly, I was suddenly struck with a sense of confusion about myself.
