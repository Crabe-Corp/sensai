# Preface by Grand Historian Tai

## 1

In ancient times during Zhuanxu's reign, Nanzheng Zhong was appointed to oversee the heavens and Beizheng Li was appointed to oversee the earth. During the time of Tang and Yu, descendants of Zhong and Li were appointed to succeed their ancestors' duties, thus resuming their roles in overseeing these matters. This continued through the Xia and Shang dynasties; therefore, the families of Zhong and Li successively held positions related to the heavens and earth throughout generations. During the Zhou dynasty, Cheng Bouxifu was a descendant of them. During the reign of King Xuan of Zhou, they lost their hereditary posts and became the Sima clan. The Sima family successively held positions overseeing the historical records of the Zhou dynasty. Between King Hui and King Xiang, the Sima clan left the Zhou court and moved to Jin. Jin Zhongjun Suihui fled to Qin, while the Sima family moved into Shaoliang.

## 2

Since the Sima family left Zhou and relocated to Jin, they became dispersed, with some settling in Wei, others in Zhao, and still others in Qin. Those who settled in Wei served as prime ministers of Zhongshan. Those in Zhao gained prominence through their expertise in sword transmission and theory; Kuai Nie was a descendant of theirs. Those who settled in Qin bore the name Cuò, and he debated with Zhang Yi. As a result, King Hui of Qin appointed Cuò as general to attack Shu; he successfully captured it and thus remained its governor. Cuò's grandson Jin served under General Wu'an Jun Baiqi. Meanwhile, Shaoliang was renamed Xiayang. Jin participated with General Wu'an Jun in the massacre of Zhao's forces at Changping, and upon returning, he was executed together with him at Duyou by imperial decree; they were buried at Huachi. Jin's grandson Chang served as the Qin dynasty official in charge of iron administration during the reign of Emperor Qin Shi Huang. Kuai Nie's great-grandson Ang became a general under Wu Xin Jun and led troops in the campaign at Chaoguo. When feudal lords conferred royal titles upon one another, they granted the title of King to Ang at Yin. During Han's campaign against Chu, Ang surrendered to the Han and his territory was established as Hedong Commandery by the Han. Chang gave birth to Wuzé, who served as a market official in the Han dynasty. Wuzé had a son named Xi, who held the rank of Dawaifu (Five Officials). Upon his death, they were all buried at Gaomen. Xi fathered Tan, who became Tai Shigong (Grand Historian).

## 3

Tai Shigong studied the discipline of celestial phenomena with Tang Du, received instruction in the Yijing from Yang He, and learned Daoist theories from Huang Zi. Tai Shigong served during the years between Jianyuan and Yuanfeng. Feeling sorrow for scholars who failed to grasp their true meanings and whose teachers were mistaken, he composed a discussion on the essential points of the six schools of thought, saying:

## 4

The Great Commentary on the Yijing says: "Though all under heaven share one principle and consider a hundred matters, they ultimately converge in the same destination but take different paths." The schools of Yin-Yang, Confucianism, Mohism, Logicians, Legalism, and Dao-De are all concerned with governance; they merely differ in the paths through which they express their ideas, some being more insightful than others. I have often secretly observed the methods of Yin-Yang theory; they are grand in appearance but filled with numerous taboos, making people overly constrained and fearful. Yet their arrangement of the great order of the four seasons cannot be disregarded. Confucian scholars are broad in knowledge but lack essential focus, diligent yet achieve little success; therefore, their teachings cannot be fully followed. Nevertheless, their emphasis on the rites between rulers and subjects, fathers and sons, as well as distinctions among husbands and wives, elders and juniors, cannot be altered. Mohists advocate frugality but find it difficult to follow in practice; therefore, their doctrines cannot be universally adhered to; Yet their emphasis on strengthening fundamentals and practicing thrift should not be abandoned. Legalists are strict yet show little mercy; Yet their establishment of the proper distinctions between rulers and subjects, as well as hierarchical relationships, cannot be changed. The Logicians encourage frugality yet often lose sight of truth; Yet their focus on clarifying names and realities cannot be ignored. Daoists guide people to concentrate the spirit, act in harmony with the formless, and provide for all things abundantly. Their method follows the great order of Yin and Yang, adopts the strengths of Confucianism and Mohism, integrates the essentials of Logicians and Legalists, adapts to changing times, responds flexibly to circumstances, establishes customs and implements affairs with universal suitability. Their principles are concise and easy to apply, requiring little effort yet yielding abundant results. Confucians, however, do not act in this way. They believe the ruler is the model for all under heaven; when the sovereign leads, ministers follow, and when the ruler acts first, subjects comply. In this way, the ruler toils while the ministers remain idle. As for the essentials of the great Dao, one must abandon ambition and excess, restrain wisdom and intelligence, and relinquish these in favor of strategy. When spirit is overused, it becomes exhausted; when the body is excessively labored, it deteriorates. If both form and spirit are constantly agitated, to seek longevity equaling that of heaven and earth is unheard of.

## 5

The Yin-Yang system, the four seasons, eight directions, twelve degrees, and twenty-four solar terms each have their own instructions. Those who follow them prosper; those who oppose them either die or perish—this is not necessarily true. Hence it is said, "they make people constrained and filled with fear." Spring brings growth, summer promotes flourishing, autumn ensures harvest, and winter facilitates storage—this is the fundamental principle of heavenly order. To disregard it would mean losing the framework for governing all under heaven; hence it is said, "the great sequence of the four seasons must not be neglected."

## 6

Confucians take the Six Arts as their guiding principles. The classics and commentaries of the Six Arts number in the tens of thousands; generations cannot fully grasp their teachings, and a lifetime is insufficient to master their rites. Hence it is said: "broad yet lacking essentials, diligent yet yielding little success." As for establishing the rites between rulers and ministers, fathers and sons, and distinguishing husbands and wives, elders from juniors—these are fundamental principles even the hundred schools cannot alter.

## 7

Mohists also uphold the way of Yao and Shun, speaking of their virtues: "The hall's platform is three chi high, with a three-tiered earthen staircase; thatched roofs are left untrimmed, and wooden beams remain unplaned. They eat from earthen bowls, drink water in simple clay cups, consume coarse grains, and sip porridge made of wild vegetables." łoż In summer they wear hemp clothing; in winter, deerskin robes." For funerals, they use a paulownia coffin three cun thick and express mourning with brief lamentations that do not fully convey their sorrow. They teach funeral rites, insisting these must serve as the standard for all the people. If all under heaven were to follow this, distinctions between high and low would vanish entirely. As the world changes with time, endeavors need not remain identical; hence it is said: "advocating frugality yet difficult to follow." The essential point—strengthening fundamentals and practicing thrift—is the path to ensuring every household is well-provided for. This is Mohism's strength; even a hundred other schools cannot discard it.

## 8

Legalists make no distinction between close and distant relatives, nor do they differentiate between noble and humble; all matters are decided solely by law. Thus, the bonds of affection among kin and respect for hierarchy are severed. Such a system may serve as an expedient measure in one era but cannot be perpetually applied; hence it is said: "strict yet lacking mercy." If the ruler remains exalted and ministers remain subordinate, with clear distinctions in roles that cannot be transgressed, even a hundred schools of thought cannot alter this.

## 9

The Logicians are overly meticulous and entangled in technicalities, preventing people from grasping the true intent. By focusing solely on names while neglecting human sentiment, they cause one to lose sight of reality; hence it is said: "they encourage frugality yet easily lose truth." However, if one controls names and demands factual correspondence, ensuring consistency without error, this cannot be overlooked.

## 10

Daoists advocate non-action (wuwei), yet also say there is nothing they do not act upon; in practice, it is easy to follow, but their words are difficult to comprehend. Their method takes emptiness and non-existence as its foundation and employs adaptability and inaction as its application. Without fixed forms or established patterns, they can thus understand the essence of all things. Not acting ahead of things nor lagging behind them, they are therefore able to be the master of all things. Whether there is law or not, they adapt their endeavors according to the times; With or without standards, they align with things as circumstances dictate. Hence it is said: "The sage remains eternal by upholding the principle of adapting to change." "Emptiness is the constant nature of Dao; adaptability is the guiding principle for rulers." When all ministers arrive together, they are allowed to clarify their own positions. If the substance matches its expression, it is called "duan"; if the content does not align with its words, it is termed "kuan." If hollow words are disregarded, then deceit will not arise; the virtuous and the unworthy will naturally distinguish themselves, and right from wrong will become clear. With such a method in use, what task cannot be accomplished? This aligns with the great Dao, merging into an indistinct and formless unity. Its brilliance illuminates all under heaven, yet ultimately returns to namelessness. All that humans possess is spirit; the body serves merely as its vessel. When spirit is overexerted, it becomes exhausted; when form is excessively labored, it decays. When body and spirit separate, death occurs. Death cannot be reversed, nor can separation be undone; therefore, sages value this greatly. From this we see that spirit is the essence of life, while form is merely its vessel. Without first stabilizing one's spirit [and body], yet claiming "I have the means to govern all under heaven"—how could this possibly succeed?

## 11

After assuming his position as Tai Shigong overseeing celestial phenomena, he did not manage civil affairs. He had a son named Qian.

## 12

Qian was born in Longmen, where he farmed and herded livestock on the southern banks of the Yellow River and its surrounding mountains. At ten years old, he could recite classical texts. At twenty, he traveled south to the Yangtze and Huai rivers, ascended Kuaiji Mountain, explored Yu's cave, surveyed Jiuyi Mountain, and floated along the Yuan and Xiang rivers; he crossed northward through Wen and Si rivers, studied in the capitals of Qi and Lu, observed the lingering traditions of Confucius, and practiced archery rites in Zou and Yi; he encountered hardships in Po, Xue, and Pengcheng, passing through Liang and Chu before returning home. At this time, Qian was appointed as Langzhong and dispatched on a western mission to conquer regions south of Ba and Shu, conducting campaigns in Qiong, Zhe, and Kunming before returning with his report.

## 13

In this year, the Son of Heaven initiated the Han dynasty's system of enfeoffment, but Tai Shigong remained stationed in Zhou Nan and could not participate; thus, he became deeply frustrated and eventually died. Meanwhile, his son Qian happened to return from a mission and met his father in the region between He and Luo rivers. Tai Shigong, holding Qian's hand, wept and said: "My ancestors were Grand Historians of the Zhou dynasty. In earlier generations, they achieved fame during Yu and Xia dynasties by overseeing celestial affairs." The family's prestige declined in later generations—will it end with me? If you become Grand Historian again, you will continue our ancestral legacy. Now the Son of Heaven has resumed a lineage spanning a thousand years, enfeoffing Mount Tai, yet I cannot accompany him—this is fate, indeed, this is fate!" When I die, you must become Grand Historian; Once in that role, never forget the writings and discussions I wish to complete. Moreover, filial piety begins with serving one's parents, continues through serving the ruler, and culminates in cultivating personal virtue. To gain renown in later generations and honor one's parents thereby is the greatest expression of filial piety. The world praises Duke of Zhou, saying he could discuss and compose songs extolling the virtue of King Wen and King Wu, promote the customs of the states of Zhou and Shao, convey the thoughts and considerations of Taiwang and Wangji, extending to Gongliu, in order to honor Houji. After the reigns of King You and King Li, royal ways declined and rites and music waned. Confucius restored what had been neglected, compiled the Classic of Poetry and Book of Documents, and composed the Spring and Autumn Annals—since then scholars have followed his example. Since the year the qilin was captured, more than four hundred years have passed, and during this time feudal lords annexed each other's territories while historical records were neglected. Now that the Han dynasty has risen and the realm is unified, with enlightened rulers, virtuous sovereigns, loyal ministers, and men who died for righteousness—yet I, as Grand Historian, do not record these, abandoning historical writings of the world. I am greatly afraid; you must remember this!" Qian bowed his head and wept, saying: "I am unworthy and slow-witted. I shall thoroughly discuss all the accounts of our ancestors' recorded traditions without omission."

## 14

After three years, Qian became Grand Historian and examined historical records stored in the stone chambers and golden cabinets. In five years, it was the first year of Taichu; on the Jiazi day of the eleventh month, the winter solstice coincided with the first day of the lunar month. The celestial calendar began to change, established in the Mingtang Hall, and all deities received their records.

## 15

Grand Historian said: "My ancestors once said: 'Five hundred years after the death of Duke of Zhou came Confucius. After Confucius' death until now, five hundred years have passed—will there be someone who can continue the enlightened ways of our age, correct the transmission of the Yijing, follow the Spring and Autumn Annals, and clarify the junctures between the Classic of Poetry, Book of Documents, rites, and music?' Is this referring to me?!" Does it refer to me?"! A lowly man like I, how could I dare refuse this responsibility."

## 16

Shang Dafu Husui said: "In ancient times, why did Confucius compose the Spring and Autumn Annals?" Grand Historian said: "I heard Dong Sheng say: 'When the Zhou dynasty's ways declined and fell into disrepair, Confucius served as a judicial official in Lu, but feudal lords resented him and high officials obstructed him. Knowing his words would not be heeded and his principles would not be implemented, Confucius judged the events of 242 years to serve as a model for the world, censuring the Son of Heaven, demoting feudal lords, and condemning high officials—this was merely to clarify royal affairs." He said: 'I would rather not convey my ideas through empty words, but instead demonstrate them clearly and profoundly through historical events.'" The Spring and Autumn Annals illuminate the ways of the Three Sovereigns above, distinguish human affairs below, clarify ambiguities, discern right from wrong, resolve uncertainties, commend virtue while condemning vice, honor the worthy while despising the unworthy, preserve fallen states, revive extinguished lineages, mend what is broken and restore what has been abandoned—these are the great principles of royal governance. The Yijing records Heaven, Earth, Yin-Yang, the Four Seasons, and the Five Elements; therefore, it excels in explaining change; the Rites establish human relationships, so they excel in guiding conduct; the Book of Documents records the affairs of former kings, thus excelling in governance; the Classic of Poetry describes mountains, rivers, valleys, birds and beasts, plants, males and females, hens and roosters, thus excelling in capturing local customs; Music establishes harmony, so it excels in creating concord. The Spring and Autumn Annals distinguishes right from wrong; thus, it excels in governing people. Therefore, rites regulate human conduct, music fosters harmony, the Book of Documents instructs in affairs, poetry conveys meaning, the Yijing teaches transformation, and the Spring and Autumn Annals teach righteousness. To correct a chaotic age and return it to order, nothing is closer than the Spring and Autumn Annals. The Spring and Autumn Annals contains tens of thousands of words in its text, yet its principles number only a few thousand. The dispersal and gathering of all things are recorded within the Spring and Autumn Annals." Within the Spring and Autumn period, thirty-six kings were assassinated, fifty-two states perished, and the number of feudal lords who fled in disorder and could not preserve their ancestral lands was too numerous to count. Examining the reasons behind these events, they all stemmed from losing one's fundamental principles. Therefore, the Yijing says: 'A loss of a hair's breadth leads to an error of a thousand li.' Hence it is said: 'Ministers assassinating their sovereigns, sons killing their fathers—these are not events of a single day or night; they result from gradual developments over time.'" Therefore, those who rule a state cannot afford to be ignorant of the Spring and Autumn Annals; if there are sycophants before them they fail to see, or traitors behind them they remain unaware. As for those who serve as ministers, they cannot afford to be ignorant of the Annals; adhering rigidly to established principles without understanding their proper application, or encountering sudden changes and failing to grasp the need for flexibility. A ruler or father who does not understand the principles of the Spring and Autumn Annals will inevitably bear the name of chief villain. A minister or son who does not understand its principles will surely face punishment for usurpation and regicide, bearing the name of a capital offender. In reality, they all believed themselves to be doing good; acting without understanding its principles, they were burdened with empty accusations and dared not protest. Those who fail to grasp the essence of rites and righteousness will descend into a state where rulers are not true rulers, ministers not true ministers, fathers not true fathers, and sons not true sons. When a ruler is not a proper ruler, he invites rebellion; when a minister is not a true minister, he will be punished; when a father fails to act as a father, he becomes inhumane; and when a son does not behave as a son, he is unfilial. These four transgressions constitute the greatest faults of the world. To bestow such a great fault upon someone, they would accept it and dare not refuse. Therefore, the Spring and Autumn Annals is the grand repository of rites and righteousness. Rites prevent wrongdoing before it occurs; laws are applied after an act has already taken place; The effects of law are easy to see, but the prohibitions established by rites are difficult to perceive."

## 17

Husui said: "In Confucius's time, there was no enlightened ruler above and he could not be entrusted with office below; thus he composed the Spring and Autumn Annals to establish a model of rites and righteousness through written words, as if it were the law of one sovereign. Now you, Master, are above served by an enlightened Son of Heaven and below entrusted with your duties; all affairs are well arranged, each in its proper order. What is it that you wish to clarify through your discussions?"

## 18

Grand Historian said: "Yes yes, no no, not quite so. łoż I have heard from my ancestors say: 'Fu Xi was most sincere and thick, he created the Yijing's Eight Trigrams. During the flourishing reigns of Yao and Shun, the Book of Documents recorded them, and rites and music were established. The prosperity of Tang and Wu was sung by poets. "The Spring and Autumn Annals selects virtue while censuring vice, promoting the virtues of the Three Dynasties, upholding the Zhou dynasty—it is not merely a work of criticism." Since the rise of the Han dynasty, under this enlightened Son of Heaven, auspicious signs have been received; Mount Tai has been enfeoffed and sacrifices made to heaven; the calendar system has changed; new colors for ceremonial robes have been adopted; the mandate from the Supreme Pure Heaven has been accepted. The grace extended is boundless, reaching even distant lands with unique customs, where people through multiple interpreters come in submission, requesting permission to present tribute and appear before the court—these cannot be fully described. Ministers and officials strive to recite the Son of Heaven's virtuous deeds, yet still cannot fully convey their meaning. Moreover, it is a shame for those who rule a state to neglect capable and virtuous men; If the Son of Heaven is wise and virtuous yet his virtue remains unknown, it is the fault of those in charge. Moreover, I once held this office; to neglect recording the illustrious virtue of a wise and saintly ruler, or to fail in recounting the achievements of meritorious ministers, noble families, and virtuous officials, would be to betray what my ancestors said—no greater crime could there be. What I have done is merely to narrate past events and organize their genealogies; it is not what one would call composing a work. To compare it with the Spring and Autumn Annals would be a mistake."

## 19

Thus, he arranged and compiled his writings. After seven years, Grand Historian suffered the calamity of Li Ling's affair and was imprisoned in shackles. He sighed deeply and said: "This is my fault indeed! This is truly my fault!" My body has been ruined and I am no longer of use." Retreating to reflect deeply, he said: "The Classic of Poetry and the Book of Documents contain allusions and ambiguities because they aim to express the aspirations of their authors. In ancient times, King Xi Bo was imprisoned at Youli and composed the Yijing; Confucius suffered hardships in Chen and Cai and composed the Spring and Autumn Ann als; Qu Yuan was exiled and wrote Li Sao; Zuo Qiuming, after losing his sight, composed the Guoyu (Discourses of the States); Sunzi had his knees broken and yet discussed military strategies; Lü Buwei was exiled to Shu, yet the world passed down the Lüshi Chunqiu (Spring and Autumn Annals of Master Lu). Han Fei was imprisoned in Qin and composed Shuo Nan and Gu Fen; The three hundred poems of the Classic of Poetry were mostly written by sages and virtuous men expressing their frustrations. These individuals all had unresolved grievances, unable to express their principles; thus they recounted past events and contemplated those who would come after." Thus he completed his account beginning from Taotang and ending with the year of the qilin's appearance, starting from Huangdi.

## 20

In ancient times, Emperor Huangdi followed Heaven and modeled Earth; four sages followed in order, each establishing their own laws and systems; Tang Yao abdicated his throne, while Yu Shun did not ascend to the platform; Their virtues and imperial achievements were recorded for ten thousand generations. The First Annals: The Five Emperors.

## 21

Emperor Yu's achievements unified the Nine Provinces, shining during the time of Tang and Shun; his virtue flowed to future generations. King Jie of Xia was licentious and arrogant, so he was deposed at Mingtiao. The Second Annals: The Xia Dynasty.

## 22

Qi founded the Shang dynasty, passing it down to King Chengtang; Taijia resided at Tong, and his virtue surpassed that of E Heng; Wuding discovered Yuese and thus was honored as Gaozong; Emperor Xin indulged in wine, and feudal lords no longer paid homage. The Third Annals: The Yin Dynasty.

## 23

Qi cultivated millet, and his virtue was great in King Xi Bo; King Wu of Zhou subdued the world at Muye; Kings You and Li were corrupt and disorderly, losing Feng and Hao; The decline continued until King Dan. Sacrifices ceased in Luoyi. The Fourth Annals: The Zhou Dynasty.

## 24

The ancestors of Qin, Boyi assisted Yu; King Mu considered righteousness and mourned the fallen soldiers. They sacrificed people as attendants, inspiring poems about the yellow birds; Emperor Zhaoxiang established imperial achievements. The Fifth Annals: The Qin Dynasty.

## 25

After the First Emperor ascended to power, he unified the six states, melted weapons into bells, and ceased warfare. He adopted the imperial title, prided himself on military might, and relied on force; The Second Emperor inherited the mandate, but Ziying surrendered to captivity. The Sixth Annals: The First Emperor of Qin.

## 26

When the Qin lost their way, heroes and chieftains rose in turmoil; Xiang Liang initiated it, and Xiang Yu continued it; He killed Qing to rescue Zhao, and feudal lords declared him ruler; After killing Ying and betraying his trust, the whole world condemned him. The Seventh Annals: The Chronicle of Xiang Yu.

## 27

Xiang Yu was cruel, while the Han dynasty practiced virtue and merit; From Shu and Han they rose in anger, returning to pacify the Three Qins; After killing Yu Ji and establishing imperial achievements, peace returned to the world, and institutions and customs were reformed. The Eighth Annals: The Chronicle of Emperor Gaozu.

## 28

Emperor Hui died young, and the Lu clan did not hold power long; They honored powerful ministers Lu and Chan, but feudal lords plotted against them; After killing the hidden friends of You, senior officials were deeply suspicious, leading to disaster for the imperial clan. The Ninth Annals: The Chronicle of Empress Dowager Lü.

## 29

After the Han dynasty first rose, the succession was unclear; they welcomed King Wang to the throne and won the hearts of all under heaven; They abolished corporal punishment, opened roads and bridges, widely bestowed kindness and generosity, thus earning the title Taizong. The Tenth Annals: The Chronicle of Emperor Xiaowen.

## 30

Feudal lords grew arrogant and indulgent, with Wu leading the rebellion. The capital executed them, seven states were punished, and all under heaven united in peace, enjoying great stability and prosperity. The Eleventh Annals: The Chronicle of Emperor Xiaojing.

## 31

After five generations since the Han dynasty's rise, prosperity reached the Jianyuan era. The emperor repelled foreign tribes externally and reformed laws internally; he performed sacrifices at Mount Tai, changed the calendar system, and altered ceremonial colors. The Twelfth Annals: The Chronicle of the Current Emperor.

## 32

The Three Dynasties are long past, their chronology no longer verifiable. Their records were likely derived from genealogical documents and old accounts. Based on these sources, I have briefly compiled the following: The First Table of Generations of the Three Dynasties.

## 33

After Kings You and Li, the Zhou dynasty declined in power, feudal lords seized authority, and some events of the Spring and Autumn period were not recorded; However, genealogies and historical records show the rise and fall of the Five Hegemons. To understand the succession of Zhou dynasty rulers, I compiled the Second Table: The Annals of the Twelve Feudal Lords.

## 34

After the Spring and Autumn period, subordinate ministers held power, and powerful states vied for royal titles; This continued until the Qin dynasty, which ultimately unified all Chinese states, abolished feudal territories, and claimed imperial authority exclusively. The Third Table: The Annals of the Six States.

## 35

After the Qin became tyrannical, the Chu people rose in rebellion; the Xiang clan then caused chaos, and the Han supported righteousness to wage war; Within eight years, the world changed hands three times. With many events and transformations, I have therefore carefully recorded the Fourth Monthly Table: The Period Between Qin and Chu.

## 36

Since the Han dynasty's founding until Taichu, a span of one hundred years, feudal lords were deposed and reinstated, territories divided or reduced. Genealogical records are unclear; officials could not keep up with these changes, and the rise and fall of power passed from generation to generation. The Fifth Table: The Annals of Feudal Lords Since the Han Dynasty's Founding.

## 37

The First Emperor's meritorious ministers, loyal pillars of the state, were enfeoffed with titles and their descendants benefited. Some forgot their ancestral order, while others died for their country or brought about its downfall. The Sixth Table: The Annals of Marquises Who Were Meritorious Ministers of Emperor Gaozu.

## 38

Between Emperors Hui and Jing, the descendants and relatives of meritorious ministers were granted titles and fiefs. The Seventh Table: The Annals of Marquises Between Emperors Hui and Jing.

## 39

Campaigns northward against the powerful Hu, southward to punish the fierce Yue, and military expeditions against the Yi and Man tribes; martial achievements are thus recorded. The Eighth Table: The Annals of Marquises Since the Jianyuan Era.

## 40

After feudal lords grew powerful, the Seven States formed an alliance. With many sons and brothers lacking titles or fiefs, benevolence and righteousness were extended, weakening their power until virtue returned to the capital. The Ninth Table: The Annals of Princes' Descendants Granted Marquis Titles.

## 41

A state with virtuous chancellors and capable generals has role models for its people. I have compiled a table of famous ministers, chancellors, and generals since the Han dynasty's founding. Virtuous ones are recorded for their governance; those lacking virtue are noted for their deeds. The Tenth Table: The Annals of Famous Ministers, Chancellors and Generals Since the Han Dynasty's Founding

## 42

The rites of the Three Dynasties each had different modifications according to their respective needs, yet they all aimed at aligning with human nature and morality. Therefore, rituals were established in accordance with people's dispositions, briefly harmonizing changes from ancient times to the present. The First Treatise: On Rites.

## 43

Music is what transforms customs and changes social mores. Since the rise of elegant and eulogistic music, people have already favored the melodies of Zheng and Wei. The origins of Zheng and Wei music are long-standing. What stirs human feelings is a longing for distant customs. Comparing musical traditions to recount the past, I compose the Second Treatise: On Music.

## 44

Without military strength, a state cannot be powerful; without virtue, it cannot flourish. Huangdi, Tang, and Wu rose to greatness, while Jie, Zhou, and the Second Emperor of Qin fell into ruin. How can one not exercise caution? The origins of the "Sima Fa" (Military Methods) are ancient. Tai Gong, Sun Tzu, Wu Qi, and Wang Zi were able to inherit and clarify its principles, closely relating them to their times and reaching the extremes of human transformation. The Third Treatise: On Military Law.

## 45

Laws govern the Yin while managing the Yang, and calendars govern the Yang while managing the Yin. Laws and calendars regulate each other in succession, with no moment of pause or hesitation between them. The writings of five schools differ greatly; only the fundamental theory from the Taichu era remains correct. The Fourth Treatise: On Calendars.

## 46

Books on stellar omens and atmospheric signs are often filled with superstitious auguries, lacking authenticity; Examining their texts and verifying their predictions reveals no significant differences. By comparing and compiling discussions of their actions, verified in order according to celestial orbits and measurements, I compose the Fifth Treatise: On Heavenly Officials.

## 47

Receiving the mandate to rule, the symbols of Fengshan sacrifices were rarely used; yet when they were employed, all spirits and deities without exception received offerings. Tracing back to the rites of sacred mountains, great rivers, and various gods, I compose the Sixth Treatise: On Fengshan Sacrifices.

## 48

Yu dredged the rivers, bringing peace to the Nine Provinces; Later, during Xuangfang's time, dikes were breached and canals connected. The Seventh Treatise: On River Channels.

## 49

The circulation of currency facilitates agriculture and commerce; When taken to extremes, it leads to cunning tricks and the accumulation of wealth through mergers. People compete for profit from machinery and commerce, abandoning fundamentals for trivial gains. I compose the Eighth Treatise: On Price Stabilization to observe changes in affairs.

## 50

Taibo avoided succession, and went to live among the Jiang and Man peoples; Wen and Wu flourished, establishing King Gugong's legacy. Helu killed Liao, subduing Jing and Chu as vassals; Fuchai conquered Qi, but Sunzi was drowned in a leather bag. Trusting Tian Pí and befriending Yue, the state of Wu was eventually destroyed. Praising Taibo's abdication, I compose the First Hereditary Chronicle: The House of Wu.

## 51

Shen and Lü have perished; Shangfu was obscure, but eventually joined King Xi Bo, serving as a teacher to Kings Wen and Wu; His achievements surpassed those of all other ministers, and he wielded great power during the reigns of You and Li. With his long white hair, he was granted fiefs at Yingqiu to enjoy sacrifices. Not breaking the oath of Ke, Duke Huan rose to prosperity, uniting feudal lords nine times and achieving clear hegemonic success. Tian and Kan vied for favor, leading to the downfall of the Jiang clan. Praising Shangfu's strategy, I compose the Second Hereditary Chronicle: The House of Tai Gong of Qi.

## 52

Whether followed or defied, Duke of Zhou pacified them; Inspired by moral virtue, the whole world harmonized with it; Assisting King Cheng, feudal lords revered the Zhou dynasty. During the reigns of Yin and Huan, what was unique about this period? The Three Huans vied for power, and Lu thus did not flourish. Praising the golden chest of Dan, I compose the Third Hereditary Chronicle: The House of Duke of Zhou.

## 53

King Wu defeated Zhou, but before the world was pacified he died. As King Cheng was still young, Guan and Cai doubted him, the Huai Yi rebelled against him.
