# Biography of Sima Xiangru

## 1

Sima Xiangru was a native of Chengdu, Shu Jun. His courtesy name was Zhangqing. When he was young, he liked to read books and studied swordsmanship; thus his parents named him Quanzi. After studying, Xiangru admired Lin Xiangru's character, and so he changed his name to Xiangru. He became an officer by means of a monetary contribution and served Emperor Jing, holding the post of Wuqi Changshi; however, this was not to his liking. At that time, Emperor Jing did not favor rhapsodies. When Prince Xiao Wang of Liang came to court, he was accompanied by eloquent men such as Zou Yang from Qi, Mei Cheng from Huaiyin, and Master Zhuang Ji from Wu. Xiangru saw them and admired their talents; thus, he feigned illness to resign his post and traveled to Liang as a guest. Prince Xiao Wang of Liang ordered him to reside with the scholars, and Xiangru lived among these learned men for several years before composing his rhapsody "Zixu."

## 2

When Prince Xiao Wang of Liang died, Xiangru returned home but found himself poor and unable to support himself. He had long been on good terms with Wang Ji, the magistrate of Linqiong. Wang Ji said, "Master Zhangqing has not achieved success in his official career for a long time; now he has come to visit me." Thus Xiangru went there and stayed at the Du Ting inn. The magistrate of Linqiong pretended to be respectful, visiting Xiangru every day. At first, Xiangru still received him, but later claimed to be ill and sent his attendants to thank Wang Ji. As a result, Wang Ji became even more respectful and courteous. Linqiong had many wealthy people. Zhuo Wangsun employed eight hundred servants, and Cheng Zheng also had several hundred. The two men said to each other, "The magistrate has a distinguished guest; we should prepare an invitation for him." They invited the magistrate as well. When the magistrate arrived, hundreds of guests from the Zhuo family were present. By midday, they paid homage to Master Sima Zhangqing, but he declined on the grounds of illness and could not attend. The magistrate of Linqiong did not dare to taste his food and personally went to welcome Xiangru. Xiangru had no choice but to go, and when he arrived, the entire gathering was completely captivated by him. When the wine had flowed freely, the magistrate of Linqiong stepped forward and played the qin, saying, "I have secretly heard that Master Zhangqing enjoys it; I wish to entertain myself with it." Xiangru politely declined but eventually played a couple of pieces. At that time, Zhuo Wangsun had a daughter named Wenjun who was newly widowed and fond of music. Therefore, Xiangru pretended to be on good terms with the magistrate in order to gain his favor and used the qin to express affection for her. When Xiangru came to Linqiong, he was followed by a retinue of horsemen and chariots; his demeanor was graceful, composed, and very refined. When he attended the banquet at Zhuo's house and played the qin, Wenjun secretly peered through the door from her room. She was delighted by him and developed affection for him but feared she would not be worthy of his attention. After the banquet, Xiangru sent someone to give generous gifts to Wenjun's attendant in order to convey his earnest feelings. That night, Wenjun fled and ran away to Xiangru, who then rode with her back to Chengdu in haste. Their home was so poor that there were only four bare walls standing. Zhuo Wangsun became very angry and said, "My daughter has behaved most unworthily; I cannot bear to kill her, but I will not give her a single coin." Some people advised Wangsun, but he never listened. After a long time, Wenjun became unhappy and said, "Master Zhangqing, let us both go back to Linqiong; we can still manage to live by borrowing from my brothers. Why must we suffer so much!" Xiangru went with her to Linqiong and sold all his carriages and horses, using the money to buy a wine shop where he brewed liquor, while ordering Wenjun to serve at the stove. Xiangru himself wore a short apron and worked alongside laborers, washing utensils in the market. Zhuo Wangsun heard about this and was ashamed; he closed his doors and refused to leave the house. His brothers and relatives repeatedly told Wangsun, "You have one son and two daughters; what you lack is not wealth. Now Wenjun has already pledged herself to Master Sima Zhangqing. Zhangqing is a man who has long been weary of traveling for official posts; although poor, he is talented enough to rely on. Moreover, he is also a guest in this region. How can you humiliate him like this!" Zhuo Wangsun had no choice but to give Wenjun a hundred servants, one million coins, and all the clothes and belongings she had brought with her at the time of her marriage. Wenjun then returned to Chengdu with Xiangru, bought farmland and a house, and became wealthy.

## 3

After living there for some time, Yang Deyi of Shu served as the dog supervisor and attended upon the emperor. The emperor read "Zixu Fu" and praised it, saying, "Alas! Why was I not born in the same age as this man!" Deyi said, "My fellow townsman Sima Xiangru claimed to have written this rhapsody." The emperor was surprised and summoned Xiangru for questioning. Xiangru said, "That is true." However, this rhapsody deals with matters of feudal lords and is not worth much attention. Please allow me to compose a rhapsody on imperial hunting for the Son of Heaven; I will present it once completed." The emperor agreed and ordered the Shangshu officials to provide him with brushes and paper. Xiangru used "Zixu," meaning a fictitious name, to refer to the state of Chu; "Mr. Wu You," meaning something that does not exist, was used to represent Qi in a debate; "And 'Wushi Gong,' meaning no such person exists, was employed to illustrate the righteousness of the Son of Heaven." Therefore, he used these three fictional figures as a basis for his argument in order to extol the hunting grounds and pleasure gardens of the Son of Heaven and feudal lords. In the final chapter, he returned to the theme of frugality, using it as a means of indirect advice. When presented to the Son of Heaven, the emperor was greatly pleased. The rhapsody reads:

## 4

Chu sent Zixu as an envoy to Qi. The King of Qi mobilized all the soldiers within his territory, prepared a large number of chariots and horsemen, and went hunting with the envoys. After the hunt ended, Zixu boasted to Mr. Wu You, who was present along with Wushi Gong. When they were seated, Mr. Wu You asked, "Did you enjoy the hunt today?" Zixu said, "Yes, it was enjoyable." "Did you catch many animals?" He replied, "Few." "If so, then what was there to enjoy?" He said, "I found it enjoyable that the King of Qi wished to impress me with his numerous chariots and horsemen. I responded by mentioning the Yumeng hunting grounds." Mr. Wu You said, "May I hear about it?"

## 5

Zixu said, "Yes." The king drove a thousand chariots and selected ten thousand horsemen to hunt by the seaside. His soldiers filled the marshes, his nets covered the mountains; they drove out hares and deer, shooting antelopes and fish with their feet. They galloped toward the salt marshes, cutting fresh meat and staining their wheels; they shot and captured many animals. Turning to me, he said, "Does Chu also have such vast plains and fertile wetlands for hunting as enjoyable as this?" "How does the King of Chu's hunt compare with mine?"' I stepped down from my carriage and replied, "I am a humble man of Chu. I have been fortunate enough to serve as an attendant for more than ten years, occasionally accompanying the king on outings. I once roamed in the royal garden and saw both what was present and absent there; yet still I did not see everything. How could I possibly speak about hunting grounds beyond that!" The King of Qi said, "Even so, please briefly describe what you have heard and seen." "

## 6

"I replied, 'Yes, yes. I have heard that Chu has seven marshes; I once saw one of them but have not seen the others." What I saw was merely a small part of them, named Yunmeng. Yunmeng is an area of nine hundred square li, and there are mountains within it. Its mountains wind and twist in dense clusters, rising high and rugged; Their peaks rise unevenly, so that the sun and moon are partially obscured. They intertwine in confusion, reaching up to touch the blue clouds; The valleys and slopes below descend into rivers and streams. Its soil contains cinnabar, blue stone, ochre, white earth, tin, green jade, gold and silver; a multitude of colors dazzle the eye, shining like scales on a dragon's back. Its stones include red jade and rose quartz, linmin kunwu, jianle xuanli, and longshi wufu. To the east are fields of hui, huanglan, zhi, ruo, shegan, qiongqiong chongyang, jiangli, miwu, zhusha, and boqie. To the south are vast plains and fertile marshes; hills rise and fall gently, stretching out in wide expanses. The area is bordered by a great river to its north and bounded by Wushan Mountain to its south. On the higher, drier ground grow zhen, li, bao li, xue sha, and qing fan. In the low, moist areas grow zanglang, jianjia, dongqiang, diaohu, lotus roots, gulu, yuxuan yu; many creatures dwell there, too numerous to be fully depicted. To the west are surging springs and clear pools, where water flows forcefully and shifts constantly; Externally, lotus flowers bloom in abundance, while internally, large stones and white sand lie hidden. Within them live divine turtles, crocodiles, maolie, turtles, and yinyu. To the north are dense forests of towering trees, such as piannan yuzhang, gui jiao mulan, bei li zhu yang, lanshi yingli, and ju you fenfang. Above are red monkeys, tianyuan, yanchu kongluan, and shetuan. Below are white tigers, black leopards, xianyan chuxian, si, xiang, yexi, qiongqi, and minyi.

## 7

"Therefore, the king then sent men like Zhuanzhu to personally subdue these beasts. The King of Chu then rode in a team of four tamed horses,乘坐 a chariot adorned with carved jade. He carried a banner fastened with fish-hair tassels and trailed by flags decorated with bright moon-like pearl ornaments. He raised the mighty halberd of Ganjiang, held on his left an elaborately carved bow called "Wuhao," and in his right hand had strong arrows from Xiafu; Yangzi rode as a side horse, while Xian E served as the charioteer. Before the chariot's pace had fully relaxed, they already overcame cunning beasts, drove out qiongqiong and ju xu, surpassed wild horses and yuetao, and rode on the lingering wind to shoot at running qiji; The hunt was swift and fierce, like thunder striking suddenly; arrows flew as if stars were falling or lightning flashing. Every bow shot hit its mark, piercing the eyes and penetrating through to the chest and armpits, severing the heart's ligaments. The fallen beasts rained down like rain, covering the ground so thickly that they obscured the grass. At this point, the King of Chu reined in his chariot and moved slowly, flying freely as he roamed. He gazed into the dense forest, observing the fierce rage of warriors and the terror of ferocious beasts, experiencing intense excitement and tension, fully witnessing all the transformations of creatures.

## 8

"Then came Zheng's daughter Manji, dressed in a robe made of Axi silk, wearing yuzhu gao and xianluo, her skirts trailing like misty hu fabric; Her garments were pleated and gathered, flowing gracefully as she moved slowly along the winding valleys. Her robes fluttered gently, her wide sleeves billowed and narrowed, while delicate strands of silk trailed behind like swaying feathers. She moved with grace and elegance, brushing past clusters of fragrant orchids below and touching the feathered canopy above. Her ornaments sparkled like emerald leaves, entwined in intricate patterns around jade tassels; She appeared light and ethereal, as if a goddess or fairy were faintly visible.

## 9

"They then went together to hunt in the hui garden, moving lightly and quietly up the golden embankment. They covered fuchui, shot juyi, cast fine arrows from hidden positions, used delicate lines for falconry, shot white cranes, captured geese in pairs, and brought down black cranes." After resting briefly, they went on to hunt in the clear pools; They floated on boats decorated with ying patterns, held oars carved from cinnamon wood, spread green silk canopies, raised feathered canopies above their heads. They cast nets to catch maolie and fished for purple shells; They struck bronze drums and blew flutes, while the boatmen sang songs. The sound echoed across the water, startling aquatic creatures and causing waves to surge like boiling tides. Fountains of water rose as streams converged, and stones clashed against each other with a loud rumbling noise, like thunder, audible for hundreds of miles beyond.

## 10

"They then prepared the hunters, beating sacred drums and lighting beacon fires. The chariots advanced in formation, the cavalry assembled into ranks, moving slowly with flowing banners, spreading out in orderly lines." At this point, King Chu ascended the Yangyun Terrace, calm and effortless in demeanor, composed and self-controlled. He prepared a decoction of shao yao (peony root) and other ingredients before partaking in it. It was not like the great king, who drove his chariot all day without dismounting, cutting into the wheels with his own hands and quenching them in water, taking this as amusement for himself. I secretly observed it, and I think Qi is likely not comparable." At this, the king fell silent and had no words to respond to me."

## 11

Wu You Xiansheng said: "What an exaggerated statement that is!" You have not hesitated to travel thousands of miles, coming to visit the State of Qi. The king mobilized all the men within his territory and prepared a large number of chariots and cavalry for an outing in the fields, aiming only to make great efforts to capture game and entertain those around him—how can this be called boasting! Those who inquire about the existence or non-existence of things in Chu wish to hear about the great state's noble traditions and your esteemed remarks, Master. Now you do not praise the virtue and benevolence of King Chu, but instead greatly extol Yunmeng as lofty, indulging in extravagant talk about excessive pleasures to display luxury and wastefulness. I secretly think this is inappropriate for you to do. If it must be as you say, then certainly it does not represent the virtue of the State of Chu. To speak of something that exists is to highlight your ruler's faults; to speak of something that does not exist is to damage your own credibility. Highlighting the ruler's faults and harming personal righteousness—neither of these is acceptable. Yet you, Master, have done so; this will surely make Qi look down on you and bring disgrace to Chu. Moreover, Qi lies east of the vast sea and south of Langya. It gazes upon Chengshan Mountain, shoots at Zhi'fu, floats on Boxiao Sea, roams in Mengzhu Marsh, borders Yaxia and Su Shen to the north, and has Tanggu as its western boundary. In autumn it hunts in Qingqiu, wandering beyond the seas, encompassing eight or nine regions comparable to Yunmeng; yet such vastness is no more than a mere trifle in the mind of Qi's ruler. As for the magnificent and wondrous sights, the exotic creatures from distant lands, the rare and strange birds and beasts—countless in variety, their scales and forms gathered together within it. The number is too great to record; even Yu could not name them all, nor Qi calculate their quantity. Yet as a ruler among the feudal lords, he dares not speak of the pleasures of hunting or the vastness of his parks and enclosures; Master also saw a guest, so the king made an excuse to avoid responding. Why then did you say there was no response?"!

## 12

Wushi Gong listened and laughed, saying: "Chu has indeed erred, but Qi is not without fault either. The reason feudal lords present tribute to the Son of Heaven is not for wealth or currency, but in order to report their duties; Marking borders and setting boundaries is not merely for defense, but to prevent excess and disorder. Now Qi is listed as an eastern vassal state, yet it secretly cultivates relations with Su Shen beyond its borders, abandoning the nation's limits and crossing the sea to hunt—this is not in accordance with righteousness. Moreover, the arguments of these two gentlemen do not strive to clarify the principles of ruler and minister or rectify the rites among feudal lords. They merely focus on competing in pleasures of hunting and boasting about vast parks and enclosures, seeking to outdo each other in extravagance and indulgence. This cannot bring fame or reputation; it is precisely sufficient only to disgrace their rulers and harm themselves. And as for the matter of Qi and Chu, how could that even be worth mentioning! Have you not seen something truly magnificent? Haven't you heard of the Shanglin 苑 of the Son of Heaven?

## 13

"To its east lies Cangwu, to its west is Xiji. The Dan River flows around it to the south, and the Zi Yuan passes through to the north; It begins at Ba and ends at Sheng, with its boundaries crossing the Jing and Wei Rivers; Feng, Hao, Liao, and Yu rivers wind and twist through it, meandering within its expanse. Vast and boundless are the eight great rivers flowing in different directions, each displaying a unique form as they diverge from one another. They rush to and fro in all directions—east, west, south, north. They emerge from the gates of Jiaoqiu Hill, flow through the marshes at Zhouyu, pass through the middle of Guilin, and cross the vast wilderness of Yangmang. They rush in a mighty current, following gentle slopes downward to converge at the narrow straits. They struck towering rocks and surged against clustered banks, boiling with fury. The waters swelled and crashed violently, swirling in chaotic eddies, colliding and twisting unpredictably. They surged sideways, rushing forward through waves to the quiet pools, cascading down rapids, crashing into cliffs and surging past obstructions. The torrents dashed wildly, splashing and leaping as they rushed with great speed. Far away, the waters flowed in deep contemplation, silent and still, finally spreading out endlessly toward their eternal destination. Then the waters spread wide and calm, gliding slowly in gentle circles, shimmering with a smooth surface. They flowed eastward into the great lake, spreading out to fill the ponds and reservoirs. At this time, dragons and red chis swam in the waters, along with giant eels, leaping fish, yuyu, shusha, and other creatures. They raised their fins and tails, flapped their scales and wings, and dwelled hidden within deep grottoes; Fish and turtles made lively sounds, while countless creatures thrived. Pearls as bright as the moon sparkled in the river, along with Sichuan stones, yellow jade, water jade, and large pebbles—shining brightly, their colors dazzling and radiant, piling up abundantly within it. Cranes, honghu, suoda, gege, yuyu, jingjiao, huanmu, fanwu, zuqu, juju, and xielv flocked together on its surface. They floated freely, drifting with the wind and waves, swaying gently. They brushed past grassy islets, pecking at water plants and algae, nibbling on lotus roots and stems.

## 14

"Here, towering mountains rise majestically—looming and imposing. Deep forests and massive trees stand on steep cliffs and rugged peaks. The Nine Zong Mountains, the Jieye Peaks, and the southern mountain range stretch high into the sky. Jagged rocks and uneven terrain form winding streams that cut through valleys, while narrow gullies and deep ravines crisscross the land. Vast hills and isolated islets rise and fall in irregular shapes; mounds and rocky outcrops jut out haphazardly. Hills and ridges undulate gently across the landscape, with marshes and ponds scattered throughout. The terrain flows smoothly into flatlands, stretching for thousands of li—everywhere is covered by human construction." They were shaded with green hui, draped in Jiangli, mixed with miwu, and interspersed with liuyi. They were entwined with zhuansuan, clustered with lisha, decorated with jieche and henglan, accompanied by gaoben and shegan. Purple ginger, yanhua, zhenzhi, chengzi, rousun, xianzhi, huangli, jiangxu, qingfan—these plants spread across the vast wetlands, extending into Ta yuan, sprawling over wide plains. They swayed gently in the wind, releasing fragrant blossoms and strong scents, flourishing luxuriantly with a profusion of colors. The mingling aromas filled the air, spreading far and wide, creating an atmosphere thick with fragrance and vitality.

## 15

"At this point, one gazes around in all directions, eyes wide and alert. The view is vast and hazy; looking at it, there seems to be no beginning or end, and examining it closely reveals no boundaries. The sun rises over the eastern marsh and sets behind the western pond. To its south, even in the depths of winter, life flourishes; waters surge and waves leap. Among the beasts are yongmao, maoli, shenniu, chumi, creatures with red heads and round foreheads, qiongqi, xiangxi. To its north, even in the height of summer, ice remains frozen enough to crack the earth; one treads on ice and crosses rivers. Among the beasts there are qilin, jueduan, tatu, tuotuo, qiongqiong, duoli, jiaoti, luyao.

## 16

"Thus, there are palaces and pavilions scattered throughout the land, spanning mountains and crossing valleys. High corridors extend in all four directions; multi-tiered halls and winding towers rise up. Ornate eaves and jade chandeliers adorn them, with paved roads connecting one to another. Walkways encircle the grounds, allowing for leisurely strolls that can last through the night." On the flat hills, halls are built; terraces rise in layers. Rock caves and underground chambers open into hidden rooms, so deep that one cannot see the bottom when looking down, yet reaching up to touch the sky by climbing the beams. Stars rush past the windows like meteors, while rainbows arch gracefully over the eaves. Green qiu twist and coil in the eastern chamber, while elephant-drawn carriages gracefully pass by the western pavilion. Divine steeds rest in the leisurely halls, and Wuquan and his kind bask in the southern eaves. Sweet springs gush forth from the clear chambers, flowing through the central courtyard like open rivers. Massive rocks and towering cliffs rise steeply, leaning precariously. Jagged peaks and sharp ridges stand like carved sculptures. Rose quartz and green lapis lazuli shimmer among the coral clusters growing in abundance. Jade stones lie scattered beside the paths, their patterns as varied as the scales of a fish. Red veins cut through the rocks in bold streaks, mingling with other minerals within. Hanging from the cliffs are smooth, translucent wanyan jade, and He Shi jade is found here.

## 17

"At this time, the luju oranges ripen in summer; yellow gan oranges and chengzuo fruit hang from the trees. Loquats, persimmons, longkui, nai fruits with thick rinds, yingzao, yangmei, cherries, grapes, yinfu, yudi plums, lada lychees—these are arranged in rows behind the palace and planted in the northern garden. They climb the hills and stretch across the plains, their green leaves shining, purple stems standing tall. They bloom with red flowers and display crimson glory, glowing brilliantly as they illuminate the vast fields. Shatang, lili, huafan, shulan, liuluo, xuyu, renpin, binglv, zhan tan, mulan, yuzhang, and nüzhen trees grow tall—some reaching a thousand ren in height, their trunks so thick they require two people to embrace. Their branches stretch straight and unbroken; their fruits and leaves are abundant and lush. They stand clustered together, leaning on one another, entwined like waves of greenery. Some rise high and fall low, others lean across the uneven terrain. Their hanging branches sway gently in the breeze, petals drifting down like banners fluttering in the air. The scene is a riot of color and fragrance, with soft breezes carrying scents that blend into a harmony as rich as the sound of jade and stone, or the music from flutes and reed pipes. Chai chi, cishi—these plants wind around the rear palace in endless loops. They gather and spread across hillsides and valleys, following slopes down to low-lying wetlands. Looking at them, one sees no beginning; examining further, there seems no end.

## 18

"Here, black monkeys and white females, weizhuo, feidu, zhitiao, chongya, hulu, huangzhi—these creatures dwell among the trees; They emit long howls and mournful cries, flapping their wings as they dart between branches. Some cling to the crooked limbs of trees, while others rest lazily at the tips of high boughs. At this point, they leap across broken beams and dash through dense thickets, swiftly grabbing at hanging branches or leaping over sparse gaps. They move freely in a scattered yet graceful manner, drifting far away with ease.

## 19

"Such scenes exist in thousands of places like this. They wander and play freely, moving from palace to pavilion without needing to relocate the kitchens or move the inner quarters. All government offices remain fully staffed and prepared.

## 20

"At this time, as autumn turns into winter, the Son of Heaven conducts a grand hunting expedition. He rides in an ornate elephant-drawn carriage, pulled by six jade-colored qiu. Rainbow banners trail behind, and clouds of flags flutter ahead; the front is led by puxuan, while Dao you follows at the rear; Sun Shu holds the reins, and Wei Gong rides as a side attendant. A retinue of attendants spreads out in all directions, emerging from among the four hunting formations. Drums beat in unison, and the hunters are released. Rivers and lakes become enclosures; Mount Tai serves as a rallying point. The chariots and cavalry surge forward like thunder, darkening the sky and shaking the earth. They scatter and regroup, pursuing their prey in dazzling formations—flowing across hills and valleys like clouds spreading or rain descending."

## 21

"They capture pibao, fight jackals and wolves, wrestle bears with their bare hands, chase wild sheep on foot. They wear he su masks, tights made from white tiger fur, cloaks embroidered with Bin patterns, and ride untamed horses." They ascend the perilous heights of Sanzong Mountain and descend to the flat expanse of Qili Ridge; They rush toward dangerous cliffs, crossing deep ravines and braving rushing streams. They drive away feilian, play with jiezhi, confront xiaha, challenge mengshi, entangle yaozhao, and shoot fengshi. Arrows are not shot without purpose; they pierce the neck or embed deep into the skull; Bows do not fire in vain—each arrow strikes and brings down its target at once. At this point, the royal carriage moves slowly and circles around as it soars back and forth. The Son of Heaven observes the advance and retreat of his troops and examines the changing dispositions of their commanders. Then, moving swiftly and suddenly far away, they pursue swift birds and outwit cunning beasts. They corner white deer, catch quick-footed hares, overtake red lightning, leave behind glowing trails, chase strange creatures beyond the limits of space and time. Bows are bent tightly; arrows are fully drawn. They shoot at flying jiao, strike at fleeing ji, choosing their targets with precision before releasing—hitting exactly where intended. The strings snap, arrows fly, and one by one, the prey falls.

## 22

"Then, raising their banners and ascending upward, they ride the startled winds and pass through terrifying gales. They travel on empty air, accompanied by divine beings, colliding with black cranes and scattering Kunji birds. They chase kongluan, urge juyi forward, brush past yiniao, and lightly touch the phoenix. They capture young mandarin ducks and obscure Jiaoming.

## 23

"When all paths are exhausted and no more can be done, they turn their chariots back in return. They wander aimlessly through Xiangyang, descending and gathering at the northern horizon. They proceed directly forward, then suddenly turn back in confusion toward their homeland. They cross the Guan Pass, pass through Fengluan Mountain, go past Zhi Que, gaze at Luhan Hill, descend from Tangli, rest in Yichun, ride westward to Xuanqu, bathe their boats at Niushou, ascend Longtai, and pass by Xiliu. They observe the diligence of officials and scholars, as well as the trophies captured by the hunters. The tracks of the chariots, the trampling by horses, and the footsteps of people all leave their mark. Amidst exhaustion and terror, many fall in fear or collapse without even being wounded—so numerous are they that they fill ditches and valleys, covering plains and wetlands alike with a dense sprawl.

## 24

"At this point, in leisure and relaxation, they set up wine on the Haotian Terrace and hold music performances under the Jiaozhou Hall; They strike great bells weighing a thousand dan and raise massive drums of ten thousand dan in height; They raise emerald banners and set up drums carved with the image of divine turtles. They perform the dances of Taotang and listen to the songs of Getian. A thousand people sing, ten thousand harmonize—the mountains tremble at their sound, and rivers surge with waves in response. The dances of Ba, Yu, Song, and Cai, the music of Huainan and Yuzhe, the songs of Wencheng Diange—these performances are presented in succession. Gongs and drums rise one after another, ringing with a deafening clangor that strikes to the heart and startles the ears. The melodies of Jing, Wu, Zheng, and Wei, the music of Shaohuo and Wuxiang, the soft and lingering tones of Yin Yin Anyan—these blend together in a riotous display from Yanying. The passionate rhythms of Jichu and Jiefeng echo through the air. Dancers, jesters, midgets, and foreign musicians perform to please the eyes and ears, delighting the heart. Before them lies a dazzling array of beauty; behind follows a procession of graceful and attractive figures.

## 25

"As for beauties like Qingqin and Mifei, they are extraordinary beings who transcend the ordinary world. Graceful and refined in appearance, they dress with elegance and precision. Their movements are smooth and supple, their postures light and elegant; they exude charm and gentle grace; They wear flowing robes of fine silk, their wide sleeves trailing behind like shadows. Their figures are slender and refined, dressed in garments unlike those of the common world; Their fragrance is rich and intoxicating, a blend of strong and delicate scents; Their white teeth gleam brilliantly, their smiles radiant and dazzling; Their long eyebrows are gracefully arched, their eyes gazing gently with a distant look; Their beauty captivates the soul and brings joy to those who gaze upon them from afar.

## 26

"At this moment, as the wine flows and merriment reaches its height, the Son of Heaven suddenly feels a pang of thoughtfulness, as if something were lost. He said: 'Alas! This is excessive luxury!' "I have enjoyed leisure and idleness, wasting days without purpose. I followed the will of Heaven to wage war, yet found rest here in pleasure. I fear that future generations may become addicted to luxury, proceeding ever forward without return; this is not how one should establish a legacy or pass on a dynasty." At this, he ceased drinking and hunting, and ordered the officials: "All land that can be cultivated should be tilled to become farmland for the people, so as to support the common folk; demolish walls and fill in ditches, allowing those who live by mountains and lakes to reach it." Fill the ponds and reservoirs without restriction; leave the palaces and pavilions empty and do not rebuild them. Open granaries to relieve the poor, make up for their shortages, show compassion to widows, and care for orphans and the lonely. Issue decrees of virtue, reduce punishments, reform institutions, change dress codes, alter the calendar, and begin anew with all under heaven. "

## 27

"On this occasion, he selected an auspicious day and observed rites of purification. He donned ceremonial robes, rode in a grand procession, raised the ornate banner, and sounded the jade 鸾 bells. He roamed through the domain of the Six Arts, strode along the path of benevolence and righteousness, surveyed the forest of Spring and Autumn annals, shot at the Li Shou target while also practicing with the You Yu, hunted black cranes with arrows, raised weapons for ritual dance, displayed the cloud-patterned banners, recited the refined verses from the Odes, lamented the Ballad of Fada Tan, rejoiced in the music of Lexu, practiced proper conduct within the Courtyard of Rites, and soared through the Garden of Writings. He expounded on the principles of the Yi Jing (Book of Changes), displayed tamed beasts, ascended the Mingtang Hall, sat in the Qingmiao Temple, allowed his ministers to speak freely about gains and losses, and within all four seas, no one failed to benefit." At this time, the people throughout the realm rejoiced greatly. They turned toward virtue and listened attentively, following its current to be transformed. With heartfelt sincerity, they embraced righteousness and shifted their ways; punishments were set aside and never used. His virtue surpassed that of the Three Sovereigns, his achievements exceeded those of the Five Emperors. Thus, it is only then that hunting can be considered a joyous affair.

## 28

"If, however, one spends the entire day exposed to the elements and galloping about, exhausting both mind and body, wearing out carriages and horses, draining the strength of soldiers, depleting the wealth of state treasuries, yet offering no virtue or benevolence, seeking only personal pleasure without regard for the common people, neglecting national governance, and greedily pursuing game such as pheasants and hares—then a truly virtuous person would not follow this path. Viewed in this light, the affairs of Qi and Chu are indeed pitiable! The land area did not exceed a thousand li, yet the hunting grounds occupied nine hundred of them. Thus, grass and trees could not be cultivated, and the people had nothing to eat. For a mere vassal lord to indulge in luxuries befitting a Son of Heaven, I fear the common people will suffer greatly as a result."

## 29

At this, the two men turned pale and changed their expressions, appearing lost in thought. They hesitated, stepped back from their seats, and said: "We are humble and ignorant; we did not know what to avoid or refrain from saying. We have only now received your instruction, and we humbly accept your words."

## 30

After the rhapsody was presented, the Son of Heaven appointed him as a Lang official. Wushi Gong remarked that the Son of Heaven's Shanglin 苑 was vast, containing mountains, valleys, springs, and myriad creatures. However, Zi Xu exaggerated the abundance of Wu Yu Meng in Chu, describing it with excessive luxury beyond reality, which is not valued by principles of righteousness. Therefore, he edited out the excess, extracted its essentials, and discussed it according to the proper path.

## 31

Sima Xiangru served as a Lang official for several years. At this time, Tang Meng was sent to open up communication with the western regions of Yelang and Shibo. He mobilized one thousand officials and soldiers from Ba and Shu, while the commandery also raised more than ten thousand people to transport supplies. Using military law, he executed their chieftains, causing great alarm among the people of Ba and Shu. The Son of Heaven heard about this and sent Sima Xiangru to reprimand Tang Meng, using the opportunity to inform the people of Ba and Shu that these actions were not his own intentions. The edict read:

## 32

To the Governors of Ba and Shu: For a long time, the barbarian tribes have acted independently without being punished. At times they have invaded our borders, causing hardship for officials and soldiers. The Son of Heaven has ascended the throne, preserving peace throughout the land and uniting stability within China. Only then did he raise an army to campaign northward against the Xiongnu, causing the Chanyu to be terrified and surrender by clasping his arms in submission, kneeling humbly to request peace. Kangju and the Western Regions, through multiple interpreters, requested audience and paid homage with clasped hands and bowed heads. Shifting his forces eastward, he led campaigns against Minyue, where conflicts arose among their leaders. To the south, he pacified Panyu; the crown prince entered court in submission. The chieftains of the southern tribes and the leaders of western Shibo have long fulfilled their duties as vassals, never daring to be negligent. Stretching their necks and raising their heels in yearning, they eagerly sought righteousness, desiring to become subjects or servants. However, due to the vast distances and deep mountainous barriers, they were unable to reach the court on their own. Those who have not submitted have already been punished, yet those who do good remain unrewarded. Therefore, the Son of Heaven sent a Zhonglangjiang to welcome them in friendship, mobilizing 500 each from the soldiers and civilians of Ba and Shu to carry tribute goods and escort the envoy. There will be no need for weapons or warfare, nor any danger of battle. Now we hear that military mobilization and preparations have been made, alarming the young and causing anxiety among the elders. Moreover, the commandery has taken it upon itself to organize grain transport—these are all actions contrary to the Son of Heaven's intentions. Those who were supposed to go may have fled or even resorted to self-harm, which is also unbecoming of loyal subjects.

## 33

The soldiers of the border commanderies, upon hearing the beacon fires and signal beacons lit, all grasp their bows and gallop forward, bearing weapons and rushing ahead. Sweat pours from them in succession, for they fear being left behind. They face white blades and flying arrows without hesitation or backward glance, not even turning back a step. Each man harbors wrath within his heart, as if avenging a personal grudge. Are they not fond of life and afraid of death? Are these not ordinary people under the same administration, sharing the same sovereign as those in Ba and Shu? They think deeply and plan far ahead, regarding the nation's difficulties as urgent, and take joy in fulfilling their duties as loyal subjects. Therefore, they receive fiefs with engraved jade seals and are granted ranks of nobility. They hold the title of Tong Hou, reside in noble mansions on the eastern side of the capital, and their illustrious names endure through posterity, passing lands to descendants. Their actions were deeply loyal and respectful; their positions brought peace and ease. Their fame spreads endlessly, and their meritorious deeds remain recorded for all time. Thus, the virtuous men and gentlemen do not hesitate to spill their blood upon the central plains or let their life essence nourish the wild grass. Now, in carrying tribute and service to the southern tribes, some have committed self-harm or fled only to be punished. Their deaths bring no honor—posthumously labeled as extreme folly, their shame extends even to their parents, becoming a laughingstock for all under heaven. How vastly different are the measures of human conduct! Yet this is not solely the fault of those who acted thus. It lies in the failure of fathers and elder brothers to teach first, and in the lack of careful example set for younger generations; They have little sense of integrity or shame, and their customs do not foster sincerity and thickness. Thus, to suffer punishment is only fitting!

## 34

The Son of Heaven is troubled by the conduct of envoys and officials as previously described, and grieves over the foolishness of the people who acted in such a manner. Therefore, he has sent trustworthy messengers to explain to the common folk about conscription for military service, holding them accountable for their disloyalty and deaths, and admonishing the village elders and filial sons for their failure to teach and guide properly. At present, it is the season for farming, and we do not wish to trouble the people further. We have already personally visited nearby counties; however, we fear that those living in distant valleys and mountainous regions may not have heard of this. Upon receipt of this edict, you must immediately deliver it to all county offices so that everyone may understand the Son of Heaven's intent. Do not neglect this matter.

## 35

Sima Xiangru returned and reported back. Tang Meng had already begun opening communication with Yelang, thus initiating the road to the southwestern tribes. He mobilized soldiers from Ba, Shu, and Guanghan, employing tens of thousands in construction efforts. After two years of road construction, the route remained unfinished; many soldiers died or were lost, and expenses amounted to tens of thousands. Many people from Shu and officials in Han court spoke against this project, saying it was inconvenient. At that time, the chieftains of Qiong and Zhuo heard that the southern tribes had established communication with Han China and received many rewards and gifts. Many of them also wished to become vassals or subjects of Han, requesting officials be sent to govern them, just like the southern tribes. The Son of Heaven asked Sima Xiangru, who replied: "Qiong, Zhuo, Ran, and Mang are close to Shu; the roads are easy to open. During the Qin dynasty, they were once connected as commanderies and counties, but this was abandoned after the Han dynasty rose to power. If we now truly reestablish communication and establish commanderies and counties there, it would be even more beneficial than with the southern tribes." The Son of Heaven agreed. He then appointed Sima Xiangru as Zhonglangjiang, bestowed him with a ceremonial staff, and sent him on the mission. As his deputy envoys, Wang Ranyu, Hu Chongguo, and Lü Yue Ren traveled in four-horse carriages, using gifts provided by Ba and Shu officials to bribe the western tribes. When they arrived in Shu, the governor and his subordinates met them at the outskirts; county magistrates carried bows and arrows ahead as vanguard. The people of Shu regarded this reception as a great honor. At that time, Zhuo Wangsun and the prominent gentry of Linqiong all sent oxen and wine through their subordinates to celebrate a cordial relationship. Zhuo Wangsun sighed and said, feeling that it was late for his daughter to have married Sima Xiangru. He generously gave her a large share of wealth, equaling what would be given to a son. Sima Xiangru swiftly and skillfully pacified the western tribes; the chieftains of Qong, Zhuo, Ran, Mang, and Siyu all requested to become Han vassals. Frontier passes were removed, and the borders expanded further west to Mei and Ruoshui, south to Zangke as a boundary. The Lingguan Road was opened; bridges were built across Sun Shui River to connect with Qiongdu. Returning to report to the Son of Heaven, he received great praise from the emperor.

## 36

While Sima Xiangru was on his mission, many elders in Shu claimed that opening communication with the southwestern tribes would not be beneficial; only high-ranking officials agreed with this view. Sima Xiangru wished to advise against it, but since the project had already been initiated, he dared not. Instead, he wrote a treatise using the words of Shu elders as an argument and refuted them himself, subtly advising the Son of Heaven while also explaining his mission's purpose so that the people might understand the emperor's intent. The text reads:

## 37

Since the founding of Han, seventy-eight years have passed. Virtue flourishes through six generations; might and power are widespread. Profound grace flows abundantly, nourishing all living beings, spreading far beyond the borders of the realm. Thus, he dispatched envoys westward to campaign and expand territory, following rivers as they advanced. Wherever the winds of Han influence reached, all submitted without exception. They pacified Ran and Zongran, stabilized Zhuo and Qiong, subdued Siyu, captured Bao Man, crossed Yihuan, turned their chariots eastward to return with reports, arriving at the capital of Shu.

## 38

Twenty-seven venerable elders and learned gentlemen in official robes solemnly gathered there. After finishing their words, they added: "We have heard that the Son of Heaven treats the barbarians and nomads with a policy of nominal subjugation—maintaining loose ties without severing them. Now, having mobilized the forces of three commanderies to open a road through Yelang for three years without completing the task, soldiers are weary and exhausted, and the common people lack sufficient resources. Now we must extend this effort further westward toward the western tribes; with their strength already strained, I fear they will be unable to complete the work. This is also a burden on the envoys. I secretly worry for Your Majesty's sake." Moreover, Qiong, Zhuo, and the western Shibo tribes have coexisted with China for many years—so long that it is impossible to record. Virtuous men do not bring them in through benevolence alone, and the strong cannot subdue them by force. Perhaps it is simply unattainable! Now, to take the common people and bind them to barbarians, using our resources for purposes of no value—this is what a humble and ignorant man like me cannot understand."

## 39

The envoy replied: "How can you say such things? If what you say is true, then it means the people of Shu have not changed their customs and those of Ba remain unrefined. I still find your words distasteful. Yet this matter is of great importance, and thus not something ordinary onlookers can fully comprehend. My journey was urgent, so the details cannot be heard now. Please allow me to briefly outline its essentials for you, esteemed sirs.

## 40

"Indeed, there must first be an extraordinary person in this world before there can be an extraordinary achievement; There must first be an extraordinary achievement before one can accomplish something truly remarkable. What is extraordinary, by nature, differs from what ordinary people consider normal. Hence it is said: The origins of the extraordinary cause common folk to fear; But once such achievements are realized, peace and tranquility return to all under heaven.

## 41

"In ancient times, the great flood surged forth, overflowing and spreading widely; people ascended and descended, migrating from place to place, living in unstable and perilous regions. The Xia Hou Shi dynasty was deeply concerned and thus filled in the great flood, dredged the Yangtze River, channeled the Yellow River, drained stagnant waters to fertile lands, directing them eastward into the sea, thereby bringing lasting peace to all under heaven. At that time, such arduous efforts were not for the people alone. Their minds were burdened with worry and their bodies bore the toil; they worked so hard that calluses formed on their hands, leaving no smooth skin, and even their skin became hairless from overexertion. Thus, their great achievements shine eternally, and their fame spreads throughout the world.

## 42

"Moreover, when a virtuous ruler ascends to power, Would he merely be content with petty matters and trivial pursuits, bound by convention and tradition, reciting old teachings for the sake of popularity in his time? He must surely raise lofty discussions and grand deliberations, establish a foundation and pass on a legacy to serve as a model for all future generations. Therefore, he strives toward inclusiveness and comprehensiveness, diligently contemplating matters that reach to the heavens and touch the earth. And is it not said in the "Shi Jing": "All under heaven are the king's land; all who dwell on earth are the king's subjects." Therefore, within the four directions and beyond the eight quarters, if there are any living beings that remain unbenefited by the king's grace and have not been nourished by his virtue, a virtuous ruler would regard this as a shame. Now within the borders of our realm, all those adorned with hats and sashes have received blessings; none are left out or neglected. Yet in the distant lands of foreign tribes with different customs, and remote regions far from our realm where boats and carriages cannot reach and human footprints are rarely seen, governance and moral instruction have not yet been extended, and the influence of virtuous conduct remains faint. Internally, they violate righteousness and breach propriety at the borders; externally, they commit wicked acts recklessly, even killing their superiors. Rulers and subjects have exchanged positions, the hierarchy of respect has been overturned; fathers and elder brothers suffer unjustly while young orphans become slaves. Chained together in sorrowful weeping, they look inward with resentment, saying: 'We have heard that there is supreme benevolence within China, where virtue is vast and grace universal, ensuring all things find their proper place—why then are we alone left forsaken?' They raise their feet in longing, yearning for it as a drought-stricken land longs for rain. Even an ordinary person would weep at this sight; how much more so for the supreme sage, who could not possibly remain indifferent? Therefore, armies were sent northward to punish the powerful Hu tribes and envoys dispatched southward to admonish the formidable Yue people. Virtue spread like wind in all directions, and chieftains from both north and south gathered in droves, looking up with reverence, wishing to receive titles—countless by the millions. Thus, they passed through Guanmei and Ruo, patrolled Zangke, carved inscriptions on Lingshan Mountain, and crossed Liangsun Yuan. They pioneered the path of morality and established a legacy of benevolence and righteousness. They sought to bestow widespread grace and extend benevolence far and wide, ensuring that distant lands were not isolated and even the most secluded and obscure regions could bask in the light of civilization. Thus, they would lay down arms here and cease warfare there. Distant and near lands united as one, both within and beyond our borders sharing blessings—would this not bring peace and well-being? To rescue the people from drowning, to uphold the noble virtue of the Son of Heaven, to reverse the decline and decadence of a waning age, and to continue the interrupted legacy of the Zhou dynasty—this is indeed the most urgent duty of an emperor. Though the people may labor arduously, how could such efforts possibly be abandoned?

## 43

"Moreover, matters of state have never begun with ease and comfort but always with diligence and toil, only ending in leisure and pleasure. Thus, the sign of receiving Heaven's mandate must lie precisely here. Now they will increase the sacrifices at Mount Tai, perform rituals on Mount Liangfu, sound the harmonious 鸾 (luan) horns, and extol music and songs, ascending to match the Five Emperors above and descending to rival the Three Sovereigns below. Those who watch have not yet seen the meaning; those who listen have not yet heard the sound. It is like a small bird already soaring in the vast expanse, while netters still look toward marshes and wetlands. How sorrowful!

## 44

At this, the various ministers were suddenly disoriented and lost their original aspirations and reasons for advancement. Sighing together, they said: "Indeed, is not the virtue of Han admirable? This is what we humble men have longed to hear! Though the people may be sluggish, we beg to lead them by example." They were bewildered and hesitant, delaying and evading with excuses.

## 45

Later, someone submitted a memorial accusing Sima Xiangru of accepting bribes during his mission, resulting in the loss of his official position. After more than a year, he was again summoned to serve as an Attendant-in-Ordinary (lang).

## 46

Sima Xiangru had a stammer but was skilled in writing. He often suffered from diabetes. After marrying the Zhuo family, he became wealthy. When it came to official matters, he never willingly engaged in discussions with high-ranking officials or state affairs; instead, he claimed illness and lived a reclusive life, showing no desire for official rank. He often accompanied the emperor on hunting expeditions to Changyang. At that time, the Son of Heaven was fond of personally killing bears and boars, chasing wild beasts; Sima Xiangru submitted a memorial advising against this practice. His words were as follows:

## 47

I have heard that things may belong to the same category but possess different abilities. Hence, strength is measured by Wu Huo, speed by Qing Ji, and bravery by Bi Yu. In my humble opinion, if humans indeed possess such qualities, beasts should be no different. Now, Your Majesty delights in ascending perilous heights and hunting fierce beasts. Should You suddenly encounter a dangerous animal or be startled by an unexpected situation, the royal procession would be endangered; carriages could not turn back quickly enough, nor would people have time to react skillfully. Even if one possessed the strength of Wu Huo or the archery skills of Feng Meng, such abilities would be useless—decaying trees and rotten stumps could all become sources of harm. This is akin to the Hu and Yue tribes rising from beneath the wheels, or Qiang and Yi people approaching the rear of the procession. How dangerous this would be! Even if there were a hundred percent safety with no danger, such activities are fundamentally unsuitable for the Son of Heaven to engage in personally.

## 48

Moreover, even when the road is cleared and one proceeds cautiously, or travels along a central path at moderate speed, accidents such as a horse biting its bit can still occur. How much more perilous it would be to ride through fields of weeds and over hills and mounds, driven by the joy of hunting fierce beasts while having no awareness of potential dangers—would not this invite disaster? To treat the immense weight of a thousand chariots as if it were light, not regarding it as a matter of safety, yet to find pleasure in traveling along paths where there is even a one-in-ten-thousand chance of danger—this, I secretly believe, is unwise for Your Majesty.

## 49

Indeed, the wise foresee dangers before they arise, and the prudent avoid peril even when it is invisible. Misfortune often lies hidden in subtle details and erupts precisely where people least expect it. Hence, the common saying goes: "A family with a fortune of a thousand gold pieces does not sit beneath the eaves." Though this statement may seem trivial, it can be used to illustrate greater truths. I humbly request Your Majesty's attention and consideration.

## 50

The emperor approved of it. On returning, passing through Yichun Palace, Sima Xiangru presented a rhapsody lamenting the misdeeds of the Second Emperor. His words were as follows:

## 51

Ascending the long slope of Bichi, I entered in confusion the towering and jagged halls of Ceng Gong. Standing by the curved banks of Qujiang, I gazed at the uneven peaks of Nanshan Mountain. The deep mountains stood tall and imposing, their hollows vast; the valleys were wide and expansive, echoing with emptiness. Rushing torrents flowed ceaselessly onward, pouring into the broad plains of Pinggao. Observing the dense and shadowy groves of trees, I surveyed the thick bamboo forests. Rushing eastward past Tushan Hill, I turned north to ascend Shilai Rock. With measured steps and lingering pace, I passed by the site where the Second Emperor was mourned. Failing to conduct oneself with care led to the loss of state and power. Believing slander without awakening brought about the extinction of ancestral temples. Alas! How sorrowful! Woe to the conduct that led to downfall; tombs overgrown with weeds and left unattended, spirits homeless and without offerings. The distance is vast and uneven, growing ever more desolate as time passes. Spirits drift aimlessly through the void, ascending to the nine heavens before vanishing forever. Alas! How sorrowful! 🙏

## 52

Sima Xiangru was appointed as the Commandant of Xiaowen Yuan. The Son of Heaven, having praised the "Zixu Fu," noticed Sima Xiangru's interest in immortality and Daoist practices. Taking advantage of this, he said: "The 'Shanglin Fu' is not yet sufficient to be considered beautiful; there are still more splendid things. I once composed a rhapsody titled "Da Ren Fu," but it was not yet completed. I humbly request to finish and present it." Sima Xiangru believed that the accounts of immortals in the annals described them living among mountains and marshes, their forms emaciated; this was not the kind of immortalism befitting an emperor. Thus he proceeded to complete "Da Ren Fu." His words were as follows:

## 53

In this world there exists a great man, residing in the central lands. His domain spans ten thousand li, yet it is insufficient for even a brief stay. Lamenting the narrow-mindedness of the world, he departs lightly and travels far away. He trails a crimson banner with white streamers, borne aloft on clouds as he ascends. He raises a long pole of Geze, adorned with colorful flags glowing in brilliance. He drapes the Xunshi as a canopy and lifts the comet's tail to form his flowing hair ornament. With graceful gestures, he moves in a stately manner, swaying elegantly as if beckoning and displaying grandeur. He grasps the Chanchang to serve as his banner, unfurling the curved rainbow into flowing silk. Red and hazy, dazzling and bewildering, sudden winds surge while clouds drift upward. He rides a dragon-drawn carriage of the Yingtong, its undulating form graceful and flowing; his side horses are crimson chis and green qiu, their sinuous shapes dark and winding. They bow and rise in a playful manner, proud and unrestrained; they twist and curl like the folds of silk, entwining together. They surge forward with vigor and strength, appearing both dignified and playful; they scatter along the banks, their movements bold yet graceful. They move in a slow, undulating manner, displaying beauty and grace; they intertwine and lean against each other with cautious yet elegant postures. They twist and leap, their movements swift and forceful as they traverse the path; they rise and bound with reckless abandon. Suddenly, like a gust of wind through grass or lightning flashing past, the scene brightens as mist clears and clouds vanish.

## 54

He turns away from Shaoyang to ascend into Taiyin, seeking communion with the True Person (zhenren). They twist and turn in elegant curves toward the right, crossing flying springs to head directly east. He summons all divine attendants for selection, arranging a host of deities at Yao Guang. He sends the Five Emperors to lead the way, returning with Taiyi and following Lingyang. To his left is Xueming, to his right Hanlei; ahead is Luli, behind him Yuhuang. He commands Bo Qiao and Xianmen as attendants, entrusting Qi Bo to oversee Shangfang. Zhurong is startled into clearing the way; only after purifying the atmosphere does he proceed. I gather my ten thousand chariots, adorned with clouds as canopies and planted with splendid banners. I send Goumang to lead the way; I wish to go south for a joyful excursion.

## 55

He passes through Chongshan where Tang Yao once ruled, and crosses Jiuyi where Yu Shun was honored. In confusion and disorder they intermingle, entangled yet moving forward in all directions. They stir up the central plain in chaotic tangles, surging and spreading like a forest set ablaze. They gather thickly in dense clusters, sprawling and flowing in dazzling splendor across the land. He enters directly into the thunderous chamber of Penglin Yulv, emerging through the eerie ravines where towering cliffs and broken rocks stand. He surveys all eight directions to behold the four wildernesses, crossing nine great rivers and surpassing five major streams. He traverses fiery flames and floats across the weak waters, braving isolated islets and crossing shifting sands. At last he rests at the ultimate limit where surging waters play, commanding Lingwei to strike the zither and dance with Fengyi. At that time, when darkness and muddiness were about to prevail, he summoned Pingyi, executed Fengbo, and punished Yushi. Looking west toward Kunlun, where the landscape is vast and hazy, he dashes straight ahead to Sanwei. He pushes open Changhe's gates to enter the imperial palace, carrying the Jade Maid and returning with her. He spreads his wings in 阆风, gathering strength; a black bird soars upward and then halts momentarily. Descending slowly around Yanshan in winding flight, I now behold with my own eyes the White-Haired Queen Mother of the West, her hair as white as snow. She resides in a cave and carries triumph; happily, she has a three-legged crow to serve as her messenger. If one must attain eternal life like this without death, even benefiting ten thousand generations would not be enough cause for joy.

## 56

He turns his chariot and returns, cutting off the road at Bu Zhou, meeting to feast in You Du. He breathes in the mist of hengxie and feasts on morning glow; he searches for lingzhi flowers and gathers qionghua blossoms. They rise high in bold ascent, surging upward like a flock of wild geese. They pass through the inverted light of Lieque and cross the surging torrents of Fenglong. They gallop along the path, descending gracefully; they dash through lingering mists and vanish into the distance. Pressing against the narrow pass of Quzhong, he extends his steps beyond the northern frontier. He leaves behind a contingent at Xuanque, surpassing the vanguard at Hanmen. Below lies a precipice with no ground in sight; above is boundless emptiness without sky. Looking around, all is dizziness and darkness, nothing visible; listening intently, only confusion and silence are heard. He rides on emptiness to ascend beyond the physical world, transcending all companions to exist alone in solitude.

## 57

After Sima Xiangru presented his ode to the Great One, the Son of Heaven was greatly pleased. He seemed to be filled with a soaring spirit that reached the clouds, as if he were wandering freely between heaven and earth.

## 58

After Sima Xiangru fell ill and was dismissed from office, he lived at home in Maoling. The Son of Heaven said: "Sima Xiangru is very ill; one may go to him and collect all his writings." Otherwise, they will be lost after his death." They sent Suozhong to go, but Sima Xiangru had already died, and there were no writings in his home. When they asked his wife, she replied: "Zhangqing never actually wrote anything down." He often wrote things down, but others took them away; thus, the house is empty. Before Zhangqing died, he had written one scroll of text and said: "If an envoy comes to request writings, present this." "There are no other writings." His surviving letter discussed the matter of fengshan, which was presented to Suozhong. Suozhong submitted the writing to the Son of Heaven, who found it remarkable. The letter read:

## 59

In the beginning of ancient times, from the boundless sky came the people. Through successive generations and sovereigns, it continued until the Qin dynasty. Those nearby followed in their footsteps; those far away heard only rumors. Numerous and flourishing, yet unnamed and forgotten—there were more than could be counted. Continuing from the Xia dynasty, honoring titles and posthumous names, roughly seventy-two rulers are worth mentioning. Who was virtuous yet failed to flourish? Who defied order but still managed to survive?

## 60

Before Xuanyuan, how distant and remote it all was! The details are no longer known. Only through the records of the Five Classics and Six Canons can we glimpse something remarkable. The Book says, "How wise is the head, how capable are the arms and legs." Based on this discussion, no ruler was greater than Tang Yao; no minister wiser than Hou Ji. Hou Ji founded a cause in Tang, Gong Liu rose to prominence among the western Rong people. King Wen reformed institutions, and thus Zhou reached its height; great achievements were made and passed on, but later declined into obscurity, remaining silent for a thousand years—was this not a case of both an excellent beginning and a poor ending? Yet there were no heretical deviations; it was simply that caution in the past and careful instruction for future generations had been neglected. Therefore, their path was smooth and easy to follow. Their deep grace flowed abundantly, easily yielding abundance; Their laws and standards were clearly defined, easy to emulate. The system they established was orderly and logical, easily continued. Therefore, their achievements flourished from the cradle and reached a height surpassing two empresses. Examining their origins, they ultimately ended in peace; there is no extraordinary or unique legacy that can be verified today. Yet still, they ascended Liangfu Mountain and Mount Tai, establishing a grand title and bestowing an honored name. The virtue of the great Han dynasty surged forth like a spring, spreading widely and filling all directions. Clouds dispersed and mists cleared; it reached upward to the nine heavens and downward through the eight regions. All living things were nourished and moistened by its grace; harmonious energy flowed widely. Martial virtue vanished like drifting clouds, while near and far lands basked in peace. Vast plains stretched out with flowing foam; evildoers were buried and forgotten, darkness gave way to clarity, and even insects rejoiced in the blessings, turning their faces inward toward the Han dynasty. Then, within the enclosures were rare herds of zhouyu and strange beasts like deer. In the kitchen grew a single stalk with six ears of grain; sacrificial animals had two horns but shared one head. Precious turtles from Zhou were captured at Qi, while green cranes rode dragons in the marshes. Ghosts and spirits connected with divine attendants, receiving guests in quiet halls. Strange objects appeared in bewildering forms, ever-changing and unpredictable. How admirable! Omens of divine favor have gathered here, yet they still considered it insufficient to warrant a fengshan ceremony. When the Zhou dynasty leaped at the sight of a fish falling from the sky, they offered sacrifices with fire to appease it. Without such signs as these to serve as omens, how could one ascend Mount Jie and not feel ashamed! The way of advancing through humility—what could be more appropriate than this?

## 61

At this, the Grand Marshal stepped forward and said: "Your Majesty has nurtured all living beings with benevolence and subdued the wicked through righteousness. The Huaxia peoples joyfully offer tribute, while the hundred barbarian tribes present their gifts. Your virtue matches that of ancient sages, your achievements unparalleled. Glorious deeds have permeated the land; auspicious signs abound in many forms, appearing continuously as if foretold by prophecy—not a singular occurrence but an inevitable culmination. It seems that Mount Tai and Liangfu have prepared altars, eagerly awaiting Your Majesty's visit. A grand title is ready to bestow honor; Heaven has already shown its grace by granting blessings, intending to complete this ceremony. Yet the Emperor remains humble and refuses to proceed." To withhold from uniting with Heaven's joy and neglect the rites of the royal way would leave all ministers ashamed. Some say that if Heaven itself is dim, such precious omens cannot be refused; If so, to refuse them would mean Mount Tai has no record and Liangfu holds no promise. They all flourished in their time, yet ultimately perished with the age—what would future generations say of them? And why mention seventy-two rulers at all? To cultivate virtue and receive omens, then act in accordance with those signs—this is not overstepping. Therefore, the sage kings never neglected this; they observed rites to honor Earth deities and paid homage to Heaven's gods. They inscribed their achievements on central mountains to demonstrate supreme authority, spreading great virtue, issuing honored titles, receiving abundant blessings, and sharing them with the common people. How magnificent is this undertaking! It is the grand spectacle of the world, the great achievement of a sovereign—unworthy to be diminished. I pray Your Majesty will complete this endeavor. And then, by incorporating the concise strategies of learned gentlemen and scholars, one may capture even the faintest glow of the sun and moon's last light, to display brilliance in governance. This also involves upholding righteousness, refining their writings, composing a single art form as part of the Spring and Autumn Annals, adding it to the six classical canons to make seven, thus opening an endless source for future generations, allowing them to inspire pure streams, raise gentle waves, spread noble reputations, and achieve flourishing achievements. This is why the sages of old were able to preserve their great names forever and remain at the forefront of praise. Your Majesty should command the Keeper of Records to fully present its meaning for your review."

## 62

At this, the Son of Heaven's expression changed with deep emotion and he said: "How delightful! I shall try it!" He then reflected deeply, reconsidered his thoughts, gathered the discussions of high officials, inquired about the fengshan ceremony, recited the grand verses on great lakes and marshes, and expanded upon the abundance of auspicious signs. He then composed an ode:

## 63

From the heavens above, I am covered in grace; clouds drift gently and smoothly. Sweet dew and timely rain—its soil is fit for wandering. Moisture seeps through the earth; what life does it not nurture? Wondrous grains with six ears—what harvest could be greater than this?

## 64

Not only does rain fall, but it also nourishes and moistens. tolist It not only moistens them, but widely spreads its warmth and grace. All things flourish in harmony, filled with longing and reverence. Famous mountains stand in their honored places, eagerly awaiting the arrival of the Son of Heaven. O Sovereign! O Sovereign! Why do you not proceed forthwith?

## 65

Graceful beasts, rejoicing in the Son of Heaven's park; With white bodies and black markings, their appearance is admirable (joyous); Gentle and harmonious—this is the virtue of a true gentleman. I have long heard their sound; now I behold them coming. Their path leaves no trace, a sign of heavenly auspiciousness. Thus it was with Shun; the Yu clan rose to prosperity.

## 66

The pure and noble qilin, roams in that sacred shrine. In the first month of winter, the Son of Heaven offers sacrifices at the suburban altar. I urge my sovereign's chariot forward; may the Emperor receive blessings and happiness. Before the Three Dynasties, such things had never been known.

## 67

The graceful yellow dragon rises with virtue; Its colors shine brilliantly, dazzling and resplendent. The true yang energy is revealed, awakening the multitude of people. As recorded in the classics, it is said to be what one rides upon receiving the Mandate of Heaven.

## 68

Its signs are clear; there is no need for constant reminders. By analogy and allegory, I advise the Son of Heaven to perform the fengshan ceremony.

## 69

Examining the records, it is clear that heaven and humanity have already connected; the responses from above and below are harmonious and reciprocal. The virtue of a sage ruler is marked by constant vigilance and careful conduct. Hence it is said, "When prosperity arises, one must consider decline; when peace prevails, one must think of danger." Therefore, Kings Tang and Wu, in their highest authority, never failed to maintain reverence and solemnity. Shun observed the ancient rites, reviewing what had been left behind: this is precisely what it means.

## 70

Five years after Sima Xiangru's death, the Son of Heaven began to offer sacrifices to Hou Tu. Eight years later, he proceeded with the rites for Zhongyue Mountain, performed the fengshan ceremony on Mount Tai, and reached Liangfu for the shan ceremony at Susuan.

## 71

Other works by Sima Xiangru, such as the letter to Lord Pingling Hou, his debates with the Five Marquises, and passages from the "Grasses and Trees" chapter that were not selected, are omitted here; only those particularly notable among the officials and ministers have been included.

## 72

Grand Historian said: The Spring and Autumn Annals reveal the profound through subtle indications; the Yijing reveals the hidden by making it evident. The Da Ya speaks of kings, dukes, and great men, yet its virtue reaches even to common people. The Xiao Ya censures minor officials' gains and losses, but its influence extends upward. Thus, although their expressions may differ outwardly, in the end they all converge on virtue. Although Sima Xiangru often used exaggerated and excessive rhetoric, his essential purpose was to guide toward frugality and restraint. In this respect, how is he different from the satirical style of the Shijing? Yang Xiong believed that luxuriant and gorgeous fu compositions, though they encourage a hundred virtues with one satire, are like galloping to the music of Zheng and Wei, ending in a performance of ya—was this not flawed? I have selected his remarks worthy of discussion and recorded them here.
