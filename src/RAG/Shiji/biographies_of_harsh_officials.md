# Biographies of Harsh Officials

## 1

Confucius said: "If you lead the people with administrative policies and regulate them through punishments, they will avoid punishment but have no sense of shame." "Lead them by virtue and regulate them with propriety, and they will develop a sense of shame and become upright." Laozi said: "The highest virtue does not dwell on virtue; therefore it possesses virtue;" "Low virtue never loses its sense of virtue, and thus has no true virtue." "The more the laws and decrees multiply, the more thieves and bandits there will be." Grand Historian said: How true these words are! Laws and decrees are instruments of governance, but they are not the source that determines whether a state is well governed or corrupt. In ancient times, when the net of laws was once very dense, yet deceit and fraud began to arise. In its extreme form, this led to mutual evasion between rulers and subjects, resulting in a state of decline. At that time, officials governing the people were like trying to put out a fire by stirring up boiling water; without strength and severity, how could they have fulfilled their duties with satisfaction! Those who talk about morality neglect their official duties. Therefore it is said, "Hearing lawsuits, I am like others; but what I must do is to make sure there are no lawsuits at all." "The low-minded person hears the Dao and laughs loudly at it." This is not an empty statement. When the Han dynasty rose to power, it broke the square corners and made them round, chiseled away elaborate decorations to return to simplicity. The law was so lenient that even a big fish could escape through its loopholes; yet official administration flourished, corruption did not arise, and the common people lived in peace and security. Viewed from this perspective, the key lies there rather than here.

## 2

During Empress Dowager Gao's reign, the only harsh official was Hou Feng, who cruelly oppressed the imperial clan and insulted meritorious ministers. After the Lu family had been defeated, they exterminated the family of Hou Feng. During Emperor Xiaojing's reign, Chao Cuo used severity and cunning to support his position. However, the Rebellion of the Seven States arose in anger against him, and ultimately he was executed. After that came officials like Zhi Du and Ning Cheng.

## 3

Zhi Du was a native of Yang. He served as an Attendant Officer under Emperor Xiaowen. During the reign of Emperor Xiaojing, Du was a Central Attendant General who dared to give direct advice and openly rebuked ministers in court. Once when he accompanied the emperor into Shanglin Park, Lady Jia went to the latrine, and a wild boar suddenly rushed into it. The emperor looked at Du, but Du did not move. The emperor wanted to personally take up arms to rescue Lady Jia, but Du knelt forward and said: "Losing one concubine means another will come in her place. Are there not many women in the world who are as good as Lady Jia?" "Although Your Majesty may take your own life lightly, what about the ancestral temples and Empress Dowager!" The emperor returned, and the boar also left. Empress Dowager heard about this and bestowed a hundred catties of gold upon Du; from then on, she held Zhi Du in high regard.

## 4

The Qie clan of Jinan had more than three hundred families, who were arrogant and lawless; no governor could control them. Thus Emperor Jing appointed Du as the governor of Jinan. Upon his arrival, he exterminated the most wicked members of the Qie clan; the rest were seized with fear. After more than a year, there was not a single case of lost property being left unclaimed in the entire commandery. The magistrates of more than ten neighboring commanderies feared Du as one would fear the central government.

## 5

Du was a courageous man, strong and upright; he was public-spirited and honest. He did not open private letters, accepted no gifts or inquiries, and paid no attention to requests or messages of recommendation. He often said: "Having abandoned my family for an official career, I should naturally serve with loyalty and die in the line of duty. I will never consider my wife and children again."

## 6

Zhi Du was transferred to become Minister of Justice. The Prime Minister, Marquis Tiao Hou, was extremely arrogant, yet Du merely bowed to him. At that time, the people were simple-minded and feared punishment enough to respect themselves. Yet Du alone emphasized severity; he enforced laws without showing favor even toward nobles or imperial relatives. The marquises of the clan looked at him with sidelong glances in fear, calling him "The Blue Hawk."

## 7

Prince Linjiang was summoned to the Office of the Minister of Justice for interrogation. Prince Linjiang wanted a knife and writing brush to write an apology letter to the emperor, but Du forbade his officials from providing them. Marquis Weiqi sent someone secretly to communicate with Prince Linjiang. After Prince Linjiang had written the letter of apology, he committed suicide. Empress Dowager Dou heard about this and became furious; she used severe legal charges to target Du, who was then dismissed from office and sent back home. Emperor Xiaojing then dispatched an envoy with a token to appoint Du as governor of Yanmen, allowing him to take the most convenient route to his post and exercise authority at his discretion. The Xiongnu had long heard of Zhi Du's integrity; when he was stationed at the frontier, they withdrew their troops. They never approached Yanmen again until after Du's death. The Xiongnu even made a dummy in the image of Zhi Du, and ordered their cavalry to ride by and shoot at it, but none could hit it; they feared him so greatly. The Xiongnu were troubled by this. Empress Dowager Dou eventually used Han law to bring charges against Du. Emperor Jing said: "Du was a loyal minister." He wanted to release him. Empress Dowager Dou said: "Was Prince Linjiang not a loyal minister?" Thus, Zhi Du was executed.

## 8

Ning Cheng was a native of Rang. He served as an official attendant under Emperor Jing. He was hot-tempered; even when he held a minor post, he would always bully his superiors. When placed in a higher position, he treated subordinates like bundles of wet firewood—handled with force and cruelty. He was cunning, lawless, and relied on his authority to dominate others. Gradually he rose in rank until he became the Commandant of Jinan, while Zhi Du was the governor there. Previously, all commandants had to enter the governor's office on foot and were received by the governor through officials like a county magistrate; they feared Zhi Du so greatly. When Ning Cheng arrived, he openly overstepped his rank and placed himself above Du. Du had long heard of Cheng's reputation; therefore, he treated him kindly and formed a friendly relationship with him. After some time, when Zhi Du died, many members of the imperial clan in and around Chang'an committed violent crimes. Thus, the emperor summoned Ning Cheng to serve as Minister of Justice. His administration emulated Zhi Du's methods, but his integrity did not match that of Zhi Du; however, the powerful members of the imperial clan were all filled with fear.

## 9

When Emperor Wu ascended the throne, he transferred Cheng to serve as an Interior Magistrate. Many of his relatives by marriage criticized Cheng's shortcomings, and he was punished with hair shaving and iron collar. At that time, high-ranking officials who were sentenced to death or executed did not usually suffer corporal punishment beforehand; but Cheng received the harshest penalty. Thinking he would never be reinstated, he removed his restraints and forged a travel permit to leave the pass and return home. He said: "If one's official rank does not reach that of a two-thousand-dan magistrate, or if one's wealth as a merchant is less than ten thousand coins, how can such a person be compared to others!" He borrowed money to buy more than one thousand mu of marshland, leased it out to poor peasants, and controlled thousands of households through this. After several years, a general amnesty was declared. He accumulated wealth amounting to thousands of gold coins and became a patron of ruffians, holding officials in check by their secrets. He traveled with dozens of mounted attendants at his side. His authority over the people was greater than that of the commandery governor.

## 10

Zhouyang You's father, Zhao Jian, was the maternal uncle of Prince Huainan and thus ennobled as Marquis of Zhouyang. Therefore, he took the surname Zhouyang. You held office through his noble lineage as an Attendant Officer and served under Emperors Xiaowen and Jing. During Emperor Jing's reign, You was a commandery governor. When Emperor Wu ascended the throne and official administration emphasized careful adherence to law, You was among those holding two-thousand-dan posts, yet he was by far the most brutal, cruel, arrogant, and unrestrained. For those he favored, he twisted the law to save their lives; for those he hated, he perverted justice to have them executed or exterminated. In whatever commandery he governed, he was sure to eliminate its powerful and lawless families. As a governor, he treated the Commandant as if he were merely an ordinary official. When serving as a commandant, he would inevitably dominate and usurp authority from the governor. Both You and Ji An were known for their vengeful natures; the writings of Si Ma An were evil. Though both held two-thousand-dan ranks, when traveling together in a carriage they never dared to sit on equal cushions or recline equally.

## 11

Later You became the Commandant of Hedong, and at that time he quarreled for power with his governor, Tu Gong. They accused each other of crimes. Tu Gong was to be executed and refused to accept corporal punishment before death; he committed suicide, while You was publicly hanged in the marketplace.

## 12

After Ning Cheng and Zhouyang You, such incidents became even more frequent; the people became cunning in evading laws. In general, official administration tended to resemble that of Cheng and You.

## 13

Zhao Yu was a native of Li. He began his career as an assistant official and filled positions in the central government, rising to become a clerk due to his integrity, and served under Grand General Zhou Yafu. When Zhou Yafu became Prime Minister, Yu became a clerk in the prime minister's office; all within the office praised his integrity and fairness. However, Zhou Yafu did not trust him, saying: "I know fully well that Yu is harmless, but he is overly meticulous in legal matters; therefore, he cannot be entrusted with a high office." During the current emperor's reign, Yu gradually rose to become an imperial censor due to his long years of service as a legal clerk. The emperor regarded him as capable and promoted him all the way to Grand Secretary. He worked with Zhang Tang to finalize various laws and regulations, establishing the "See-and-Know" system, which allowed officials to supervise each other through official transmissions. The use of law became increasingly severe; this trend began from here.

## 14

Zhang Tang was a native of Du. His father served as the Magistrate of Chang'an; when his father went out, young Tang stayed behind to guard their home. When he returned and found that a rat had stolen some meat, his father was furious and beat Tang with a stick. Tang dug up the burrow and caught the rat that had stolen the meat, along with the remaining meat. He then formally accused the rat of theft, conducted an investigation as if it were a criminal case, compiled the official record, interrogated "the suspect," and submitted his judgment report. He even displayed the rat and the meat in court below the hall for public display. His father saw this, read his written report, which was as skillfully composed as that of an experienced legal official, and was greatly astonished. From then on, he had Tang study law and serve in the court. After his father's death, Tang served as a local official in Chang'an for many years.

## 15

When Marquis Zhouyang was first serving among the high-ranking officials, he once was imprisoned in Chang'an, and Tang devoted himself to helping him. After being released and ennobled as a marquis, Zhouyang formed a close friendship with Tang and introduced him widely to influential people of high rank. Tang served as an official in the Interior Office and was a clerk under Ning Cheng, who considered Tang harmless. He recommended him to the central government; thus, Tang was transferred to serve as Magistrate of Maoling, where he governed during the construction period of the imperial tomb.

## 16

When Marquis Wu'an served as Prime Minister, he summoned Tang to serve in his office and recommended him to the Son of Heaven. The emperor appointed him as an imperial censor, sending him on official investigations. While handling the case involving Empress Chen's witchcraft conspiracy, Tang thoroughly investigated and exposed all accomplices involved in the plot. The emperor then regarded him as capable and gradually promoted him to the position of Taizhong Dafu (Grand Councillor). Together with Zhao Yu, he helped establish various legal codes and regulations, emphasizing strict interpretation of the law to bind officials in their duties. Later, Zhao Yu was promoted to Zhongwei (Commander of the Guards) and then transferred to Shaofu. Meanwhile, Zhang Tang became Tingwei (Minister of Justice). The two maintained a warm relationship, with Tang treating Yu as an elder brother. Zhao Yu was a man who was upright yet arrogant in manner. Since he began his career as an official, he never entertained guests at home. When high-ranking officials visited and invited him, Yu never responded or expressed gratitude; he was determined to cut off all requests from friends, guests, and acquaintances, maintaining a stance of isolation and single-mindedness. Whenever he encountered legal documents or cases, he would immediately take action without reviewing them again, seeking out hidden faults among officials and subordinates. Tang was a man of many deceptions, using his cunning intelligence to manage people. When he first became a minor official, he embezzled public funds and maintained private relationships with wealthy merchants in Chang'an such as Tian Jia and Yu Wongshe. When he rose to the rank of one of the Nine Ministers, he sought out and cultivated connections with famous scholars and officials throughout the empire. Although in his heart he might not have agreed with them, he outwardly showed admiration and respect.

## 17

At that time, the emperor was particularly interested in literary and scholarly pursuits. In handling major cases, Tang wished to apply classical principles, so he requested that students of the Doctorate who studied the Book of Documents and the Spring and Autumn Annals be assigned as assistants to the Minister of Justice, to help interpret ambiguous legal matters. When submitting judgments on doubtful cases, he always first presented to the emperor a detailed analysis of their origins. When the emperor approved his recommendations, Tang would record these decisions as legal precedents under the authority of the Minister of Justice and publicize them in order to highlight the emperor's wisdom. When submitting reports and being reprimanded, Tang would respond with apologies, aligning his actions with what the emperor found acceptable. He always cited upright officials such as magistrates or clerks who were known for their virtue, saying: "These officials advised me to act this way; if Your Majesty holds me responsible, I did not follow their wise counsel and thus bear the blame." As a result, he was often pardoned of his faults. Whenever there was an opportunity, he would submit reports to the emperor. The emperor praised him and said: "I did not know this report came from you; it must have been prepared by Magistrate or Clerk So-and-so." In this way, he promoted officials by highlighting their virtues and concealing their faults. When handling cases that aligned with the emperor's desire to punish someone, he would assign magistrates and clerks known for being severe in their judgments. If the emperor wished to pardon someone, he would instead appoint magistrates and clerks who were lenient and fair. When dealing with powerful or influential individuals, he was sure to twist the law skillfully in order to accuse them. But when it came to poor and weak commoners, he would sometimes speak out on their behalf; even if the law was applied in a written manner, the emperor would carefully examine the case. Thus, the emperor often followed Tang's recommendations and released those he had spoken for. Tang rose to a high-ranking official position; internally, he cultivated personal virtue. He maintained connections with guests and entertained them with food and drink. For the sons of his old friends who became officials, as well as poor brothers, he provided especially generous support and protection. When visiting high-ranking officials to make requests or inquiries, he never avoided the cold of winter or the heat of summer. Therefore, although Tang was known for his strict legal interpretations and suspicious nature, which made him not entirely impartial, he still gained this reputation. Many of the harsh and severe officials who served as enforcers relied on scholars well-versed in literary and classical studies. Chancellor Gong Hong often praised his virtues. When handling the cases of rebellion involving the kingdoms of Huainan, Hengshan, and Jiangdu, he thoroughly investigated their root causes. Yan Zhu and Wu Bei were among those involved; the emperor wanted to pardon them. Tang argued: "Wu Bei originally devised the plot of rebellion, and Yan Zhu was a favored official who had close private dealings with the feudal lords in this manner. If they are not executed, it will be impossible to govern effectively in the future." Thus, the emperor approved their punishment. In handling cases, he often took credit for himself by displacing or undermining high-ranking officials; there were many instances like this. As a result, Tang gained even greater respect and trust from the emperor, and was promoted to Minister of Censors (Yushi Daifu).

## 18

At this time, Hunye and others surrendered to the Han dynasty. The empire mobilized a large army to attack the Xiongnu, while floods and droughts struck Shandong, causing poor people to become displaced; they all relied on government support, which left the imperial treasury exhausted. Therefore, following the emperor's intent, he proposed to mint white metal coins and five-zhu coins, to monopolize salt and iron production nationwide, to suppress wealthy merchants and large traders, to issue a decree for reporting monetary crimes, to uproot powerful families who had accumulated wealth through land consolidation or monopolies, and to use legalistic interpretations and skillful accusations as tools to support the law. Whenever Tang presented reports in court and discussed matters of state finances, he spoke so long that it was often late into the day, to the point where the emperor forgot to eat. The prime minister merely held office in name; all major affairs of state were decided by Tang. The common people became unsettled in their livelihoods, causing unrest. The policies initiated by the government had not yet brought benefits to them, but corrupt officials took advantage and exploited them; thus, Tang severely punished those guilty of such crimes. As a result, from the high-ranking ministers down to commoners, all pointed fingers at Tang. Once when Tang was ill, the emperor personally visited him to inquire about his condition; such was the honor and prestige he enjoyed.

## 19

When the Xiongnu sent envoys requesting a marriage alliance for peace, court officials debated the matter before the emperor. Doctor Di Shan said: "A peace through marriage is beneficial." The emperor asked him to explain why this was advantageous, and Di Shan replied: "Military force is a weapon of violence; it should not be used lightly or frequently." Emperor Gaozu once wished to attack the Xiongnu but was heavily defeated at Pingcheng, and so he ultimately concluded a peace through marriage. During the reigns of Emperor Xiaohui and Empress Dowager Gao, the empire enjoyed peace and prosperity. But when Emperor Xiaowen wished to take military action against the Xiongnu, the northern frontier was left in a state of desolation and hardship due to warfare. During Emperor Xiaojing's reign, the Seven States Rebellion broke out between Wu, Chu and others; for several months, Emperor Jing moved back and forth between two palaces in great anxiety. After the suppression of Wu and Chu, Emperor Jing never again spoke of war; as a result, the empire became wealthy and prosperous. Now that Your Majesty has launched military campaigns against the Xiongnu, China's treasury is exhausted and frontier civilians are in great poverty and hardship. From this we can see, it would be better to pursue a peace through marriage." The emperor then asked Tang for his opinion. Tang replied: "This is an ignorant Confucian scholar, who knows nothing." Di Shan said: "I am indeed a foolish loyalist; as for the Minister of Censors Zhang Tang, he is a deceitful loyalist. When Tang handled the cases of Huainan and Jiangdu, he used strict legal interpretations to harshly accuse feudal lords, creating divisions among family members and causing vassal officials to feel insecure. I have long known that Tang is a deceitful loyalist." At this, the emperor turned angry and said: "If I were to appoint you as governor of a single commandery, could you prevent the Xiongnu from raiding it?" He replied: "No, I cannot." The emperor asked: "Then how about being in charge of a single county?" He replied: "No, I still could not." The emperor continued: "Then how about being responsible for a single frontier outpost?" Di Shan, realizing that he was running out of arguments and would soon be handed over to the officials for punishment, replied: "Yes, I can." Thus, the emperor sent Di Shan to take charge of the outpost. After more than a month, the Xiongnu beheaded him and left. From then on, all court officials were deeply frightened and cowed.

## 20

Tang's associate Tian Jia, although a merchant by trade, was known for his virtuous character. When Tang first became a minor official, they had shared financial dealings; but when Tang rose to high office, Tian Jia would hold him accountable for any misconduct or failure in virtue, displaying the spirit of a righteous man.

## 21

Tang served as Minister of Censors for seven years before his downfall.

## 22

A native of Hedong named Li Wen had once been at odds with Tang. Later, when he became Vice Minister of Censors, he was filled with resentment and repeatedly searched official documents for any matter that could be used to harm Tang, but found nothing incriminating. Tang had a favored clerk named Lu Yiju. Learning of Tang's injustice, Li Wen sent someone to report false accusations against Tang for corruption. The case was then referred back to Tang himself, who investigated and sentenced Li Wen to death, though he knew in his heart that it was actually Lu Yiju who had instigated the false charges. The emperor asked: "Regarding these allegations of misconduct, where exactly did they originate?" Tang feigned surprise and said: "This must be due to old grudges from Li Wen's former associates." Lu Yiju was lying ill in the home of a host family in his neighborhood, and Tang personally visited him to check on his condition, even massaging his feet. The State of Zhao was known for its iron smelting and casting industries; the king frequently petitioned about matters concerning the state's iron officials, and Tang often opposed King Zhao. King Zhao sought out any hidden misconduct of Tang's. Lu Yiju had once investigated King Zhao, and the king bore a grudge. He submitted a report to the emperor accusing: "Tang is a high-ranking minister; his clerk Lu Yiju was ill, yet Tang personally went to massage his feet—this suggests they are engaged in some serious misconduct." The case was referred to the Minister of Justice. Lu Yiju died while still ill, and the matter involved his younger brother, who was detained in the Office of Judicial Administration. Tang also had other prisoners under investigation at the Office of Judicial Administration, and when he saw Lu Yiju's younger brother among them, he secretly intended to help him but pretended not to recognize him. Lu Yiju's younger brother was unaware of this and resented Tang, so he sent someone to submit a report accusing Tang of conspiring with Lu Yiju to fabricate the charges against Li Wen. The case was then referred to Jiansheng. Jiansheng had once been at odds with Zhang Tang, and upon receiving this case, he thoroughly investigated the matter but had not yet submitted his report. At this time, someone was caught stealing buried money from the tomb of Emperor Xiaowen. Chancellor Qingzhi met with Tang in court and they had agreed to jointly apologize for the incident. When they appeared before the emperor, however, Tang thought that only the prime minister, who was responsible for seasonal rituals at the imperial tombs, should offer an apology; since he himself had no involvement, he did not apologize. The chancellor apologized, and the emperor ordered the censors to investigate the matter. Tang wanted to use legal wording to hold the prime minister responsible for having known about the incident, and this troubled the chancellor. The three senior secretaries of the prime minister all resented Tang and wished to see him fall into disgrace.

## 23

Originally, the chief secretary Zhu Maichen was a native of Kuaiji. He studied the Spring and Autumn Annals. Zhuang Zhu sent someone to recommend Maichen, who was then favored by the emperor along with Zhuang Zhu and served as an Attendant-in-Ordinary, becoming a Taizhong Dafu and holding power in affairs of state. Meanwhile, Tang was still a minor official, bowing and kneeling before Maichen and others. Later, when Tang became the Tingwei (Minister of Justice) and handled the case against the Kingdom of Huainan, he pushed aside Zhuang Zhu. Maichen had long been resentful in his heart. When Tang became the Grand Censor, Maichen served as governor of Kuaiji and was appointed Duke Commandant-in-Chief, ranking among the Nine Ministers. After several years, Maichen was dismissed from office for breaking the law and reduced to a minor official. When he met Tang, Tang sat on his chair, and the clerks treated Maichen without courtesy. Maichen, being a native of Chu, bore deep resentment toward this treatment and often wished for an opportunity to take revenge on Tang. lords Wang Chao was a native of Qi. He attained the position of You Neishi through his skills and strategies. Bian Tong studied 纵横 (Zongheng, or Diplomacy), was a strong-willed and fierce man, and twice held the position of Chancellor of Jinan. Therefore, they all ranked above Tang in official status. Later, when they lost their posts and were reduced to minor officials, they had to humble themselves before Tang. Tang often carried out the duties of the prime minister and knew that these three senior secretaries had long been men of high status, so he frequently treated them with arrogance and condescension. Because of this, the three senior secretaries conspired together, saying: "Initially, Tang made an agreement with you to apologize together, but later he betrayed you; now he wants to accuse you of matters concerning the ancestral temple, which is merely his attempt to replace you." "We know about Tang's secret dealings." They sent officials to arrest and investigate Tang's associate Tuo Xin, claiming that whenever Tang was about to submit a report or request, Xin always learned of it first. He had grown wealthy through illicit dealings in property, sharing the profits with Tang, as well as engaging in other corrupt activities. The details of these accusations became somewhat known. The emperor asked Tang, "Whenever I take action, merchants always learn of it in advance and grow richer by acquiring the goods involved. There must be someone who is leaking my plans to them." Tang did not apologize. Tang then pretended to be surprised and said, "Of course there should be someone." Jiansheng also submitted a report on the matter of Ju Deng. The emperor indeed believed that Tang had been harboring deceit and had lied to his face, so he sent eight successive envoys to interrogate and press charges against him. Tang fully explained himself, denying any wrongdoing and refusing to admit guilt. At this point, the emperor sent Zhao Yu to hold Tang accountable. Yu arrived and rebuked Tang, saying: "How could you not know your proper place? How many people have you destroyed through the cases you handled?" Now, there are accusations against you with detailed evidence. The emperor is sending you to prison and wants you to consider your own situation carefully; why do you insist on defending yourself in court?" Tang then wrote a letter of apology, saying: "I have no achievements however small, and I rose from the position of an official who handled legal documents. It is by Your Majesty's grace that I was elevated to the rank of one of the Three Dukes; yet I am unable to fulfill my responsibilities adequately. Yet those who plotted to frame me with crimes were the three senior secretaries." He then committed suicide.

## 24

After Tang's death, the total value of his family's assets did not exceed 500 jin (a unit of weight), all of which were gifts and salaries he had received from the emperor; there was no other source of wealth. Tang's brothers and sons wished to give him a lavish funeral, but Tang's mother said: "Tang was a high minister of the Son of Heaven, yet he died under false accusations. Why should we give him an extravagant burial?" They placed his body in a cart drawn by oxen and provided only a coffin without a casket. The emperor heard of this and said: "Without such a mother, it would not have been possible to raise such a son." He then had the three senior secretaries investigated and executed. Chancellor Qingzhi committed suicide. Tuo Xin was released from prison. The emperor regretted the loss of Tang. He gradually promoted Tang's son, Anshi.

## 25

Zhao Yu was once dismissed from office but later became the Tingwei (Ministry of Justice). Initially, Marquis Tiao considered Zhao Yu to be too ruthless and did not trust him. When Yu became the Shaofu (Minister of Works), he ranked among the Nine Ministers. Yu was harsh and strict, but in his later years, as more affairs accumulated, officials became increasingly severe; however, Yu's administration grew somewhat lenient, and he gained a reputation for fairness. Later figures such as Wang Wenshu emerged who were even more ruthless in their governance than Zhao Yu. Because of his old age, Yu was transferred to serve as Chancellor of Yan. After several years, he committed crimes and became corrupt; thus, he was dismissed from office and returned home. More than ten years after Tang's death, Zhao Yu passed away at home of old age.

## 26

Yi Zong was a native of Hedong. When he was young, Yi Zong once joined Zhang Cigong in forming a band of robbers and engaging in raids together. Zong had an elder sister named Xu, who gained favor with Empress Dowager Wang through her medical skills. Empress Dowager Wang asked: "Do you have any brothers or sons who hold official positions?" His sister replied, "I have a younger brother, but he is of poor character and cannot be recommended." The empress dowager then informed the emperor, who appointed Yi Zong, Xu's younger brother, as a Zhonglang (attendant) and later assigned him to serve as the commander of Shangdang Commandery. He governed with boldness, was not reserved or courteous, and his county had no unresolved cases; he was therefore recommended as the top official. He was promoted to serve as magistrates of Changling and Chang'an, where he enforced the law impartially without showing favoritism even toward powerful aristocrats or relatives of the imperial family. He was transferred to serve as a military commissioner in Hedong after successfully arresting and investigating Ju Zhong, the maternal grandson of Empress Dowager Wang. The emperor regarded him as capable and promoted him to be the Duwei (military commissioner) of Hedong. Upon his arrival, he exterminated the powerful Rang clan and its associates; as a result, the roads in Hedong became so safe that no one dared to pick up lost property. Meanwhile, Zhang Cigong also served as a Lang (attendant), and for his bravery in military campaigns, he was allowed to lead troops into battle. He earned merit through his courage and was enfeoffed as the Marquis of Antou.

## 27

Ning Cheng, while living in retirement at home, was considered by the emperor for appointment as a commandery governor. The Grand Censor Hong said: "When I was a minor official in Shandong, Ning Cheng served as the Duwei of Jinan. His administration was like that of a wolf tending sheep." "He is not fit to govern the people." The emperor then appointed Ning Cheng as the Duwei of Guan. After more than a year, officials from eastern Guan who passed through the customs and checkpoints would say: "It is better to encounter a tigress with cubs than to face Ning Cheng's anger." Yi Zong, having been transferred from Hedong to serve as governor of Nanyang, heard that Ning Cheng was living in retirement in Nanyang. When Zong arrived at the customs checkpoint, Ning Cheng approached him sideways to greet and escort him, but Zong remained arrogant and did not return the courtesy. Upon arriving in his commandery, he immediately investigated the Ning family and completely destroyed their household. Ning Cheng was found guilty of crimes, and members of his clan such as Kong and Bao fled in fear; the officials and people of Nanyang walked with great caution, afraid to tread on each other's footprints. Meanwhile, Zhu Qiang, Du Yan, and Du Zhou from Ping Shi served as Zong's subordinates. They were entrusted with important roles and later promoted to become officials of the Tingwei (Ministry). The military frequently campaigned in Dingxiang, causing chaos and disorder among its officials and people. As a result, Zong was transferred to serve as the governor of Dingxiang. When Zong arrived, he secretly arrested more than 200 prisoners in the Dingxiang jail who were being held for serious crimes but treated lightly. He also detained over 200 visitors, including guests and relatives of these prisoners, who had come to see them privately. Zong conducted a single investigation and arrest, claiming that "these were cases where capital punishment should be applied." On that day alone, over 400 people were reported executed. After this, the entire commandery became so terrified that even in warm weather, people trembled with fear; lawless civilians then assisted officials in maintaining order.

## 28

At that time, Zhao Yu and Zhang Tang had already become members of the Nine Ministers due to their strict enforcement of laws. However, their governance was still relatively lenient, applying legal codes with some flexibility; in contrast, Zong governed by striking down even minor offenses like an eagle preying on weak prey. Later, when the circulation of Wuzhu coins and white silver led to widespread corruption among the people—especially in Chang'an—the emperor appointed Yi Zong as You Neishi (Right Minister of Justice) and Wang Wenshu as Zhongwei (Commander of the Capital Guards). Wang Wenshu was extremely cruel, and whenever he carried out actions without first informing Zong, Zong would inevitably use his authority to dominate him, undermining his achievements. His administration resulted in the execution of many people; however, this only led to minor improvements, and corruption became even more rampant. It was then that the system of direct imperial envoys (Zhi Zhi) began to be deployed. Officials governed by focusing on executions, killings, and restraints, and thus Yan Feng became notorious for his cruelty. Zong was upright in character, and his administration resembled that of Zhi Du. The emperor once visited Dinghu but fell ill for a long time; later, he recovered and traveled to Ganquan, where the roads were poorly maintained. The emperor was furious and said: "Does Zong think I will never travel this road again?" He bore a grudge against him. By winter, Yang Ke was in charge of collecting fines for coin-related accusations. Zong considered this to be causing disorder among the people and ordered his subordinates to arrest those acting on behalf of Yang Ke. The emperor heard about it, sent Du Shi to investigate, and found Zong guilty of obstructing official business; he was executed in the marketplace. One year later, Zhang Tang also died.

## 29

Wang Wenshu was a native of Yangling. When he was young, he engaged in violent and criminal activities. Later, he was temporarily appointed as a county post official but was repeatedly dismissed from office. As an official, he rose through the ranks by handling legal cases and eventually became an officer in the Tingwei (Ministry of Punishment). He served under Zhang Tang and was promoted to be a censor. 략 He supervised the suppression of bandits, killing or wounding many, and gradually rose to become Duwei (military commissioner) in Guangping. He selected more than ten officials from the commandery who were powerful and daring, appointing them as his enforcers. He secretly held onto evidence of their serious crimes, allowing them to pursue bandits in order to satisfy their personal ambitions. These men, though guilty of a hundred offenses, would not be punished; If they attempted to evade punishment, he would use their past crimes as grounds for execution and even exterminate their entire clan. Because of this, bandits in the outskirts of Qi and Zhao dared not approach Guangping, and Guangping became renowned for its reputation that "lost items were never picked up on the roads." The emperor heard about this and promoted him to be governor of Hedong.

## 30

While he had previously lived in Guangping, he already knew the powerful and corrupt families in Hedong; upon his arrival there, within nine months he had dealt with them. He ordered the commandery to prepare fifty private horses for relay stations from Hedong to Chang'an, and arranged his subordinates according to the strategies he had used in Guangping. He arrested powerful and corrupt local elites in the commandery; as a result, more than a thousand families of these influential troublemakers were implicated and punished. He submitted a memorial requesting that the most serious offenders be exterminated by clan, while lesser ones be executed, with all their property confiscated to compensate for losses. The emperor approved his proposals within two or three days after they were submitted. After the verdicts were carried out, blood flowed for more than ten li. The people of Hedong all marveled at how quickly his proposals were implemented, considering it miraculous and swift. By the end of the twelfth month, there was complete silence throughout the commandery; no one dared to travel at night, and there were no bandits in the fields even daring to make a dog bark. Those who managed to escape were driven into neighboring commanderies and states, but when they returned in spring, Wenshu stamped his foot and sighed, saying, "Alas! If only the winter had lasted one more month, I would have finished my task!" He was fond of killing and using violence to establish authority, showing no mercy or care for people in this way. The emperor heard about it and considered him capable, promoting him to be Zhongwei (commander of the imperial guards). His administration followed the same methods as in Hedong, transferring notorious corrupt officials and associates. In Hedong they were Yang Jie and Ma Wu, while in Guanzhong they were Yang Gan and Cheng Xin, among others. Yi Zong was the Neishi (commandery administrator), but he feared Wenshu and dared not act freely in governance. After Yi Zong died, following Zhang Tang's downfall, Wenshu was transferred to be an officer of the Tingwei (Ministry of Justice), while Yin Qi became Zhongwei.

## 31

Yin Qi was a native of Chiping, Dongjun. He gradually rose to the position of censor through his skills in writing and legal work. Serving under Zhang Tang, Zhang Tang often praised Yin Qi for his integrity and bravery, assigning him to suppress bandits. He executed criminals without showing favoritism even toward the powerful or imperial relatives. He was promoted to Duwei (military commissioner) of Guannei, and his reputation surpassed that of Ning Cheng. The emperor considered him capable and promoted him to Zhongwei; as a result, officials and commoners alike became increasingly impoverished and weary. Yin Qi was strong-willed but lacked refinement; corrupt officials hid in fear while upright ones could not govern effectively, so many affairs were neglected and led to punishment. The emperor then transferred Wenshu back to the position of Zhongwei, while Yang Pu served as Duwei (commander) of Zhuju, known for his severity and harshness.

## 32

Yang Pu was a native of Yiyang. He began his official career as an officer among the thousand-man units. The governor of Henan recommended him for his abilities, promoted him to censor, and assigned him to suppress bandits in Guandong. He governed in the same manner as Yin Qi, believing that bold and decisive action was necessary. Gradually he rose to become Duwei of Zhuju (Minister of Titles), ranking among the Nine Ministers. The emperor regarded him as capable. When the Nanyue rebels rose up, he was appointed as General of the Louchuan Fleet and achieved success in battle; he was then enfeoffed as Marquis of Jiangliang. He was captured by Xun Zhi. After a long time, he died of illness.

## 33

Wenshu once again became Zhongwei. He was a man of little learning, and in court he appeared drowsy and uncommunicative; but when it came to the duties of Zhongwei, his mind became sharp and decisive. In suppressing bandits, he was well-versed in the customs of Guanzhong and knew the corrupt officials; these corrupt officials were once again put to use by him for strategic purposes. He imposed strict scrutiny on officials, encouraging young criminals and evildoers to report crimes by placing reward boxes. He also appointed local supervisors to oversee the suppression of thieves and bandits. Wenshu was a man who flattered and skillfully served those in power; If someone had no influence, he treated them like a slave. Families with power and connections, even if their crimes were as numerous as mountains, would not be touched by him; But those without influence, even imperial relatives, would inevitably suffer his harassment and humiliation. He manipulated legal documents and skillfully framed minor offenders to eliminate influential local elites. This was how he governed as Zhongwei. Corrupt and cunning individuals were thoroughly punished, most of them perishing in prison without ever being released after sentencing. His enforcers behaved like tigers wearing human hats. At that time, all the cunning and corrupt individuals in the Zhongwei's jurisdiction submitted to his authority; those with influence spread favorable rumors about him, praising his governance. After governing for several years, many of his officials became wealthy through abuse of power.

## 34

When Wenshu returned from a campaign against the Dongyue, there were discussions that did not meet his approval; he was later punished and dismissed for minor legal infractions. At that time, the emperor wanted to build a Tiantai (Heaven-Connecting Pavilion) but had not yet found suitable laborers. Wenshu volunteered to recruit and release convicts from the Zhongwei office, obtaining tens of thousands of workers for the project. The emperor was pleased and appointed him as Shaofu (Minister in charge of imperial workshops). He was transferred to be Neishi of You, governing just as before; corrupt and wicked individuals were rarely restrained. He lost his position due to legal violations. He was later reinstated as Youfu (Right Assistant) and resumed the duties of Zhongwei. He continued to act in the same manner.

## 35

After more than a year, when the army at Wan was mobilized, an imperial edict summoned powerful officials. Wenshu concealed his subordinate Hua Cheng; later, someone reported that he had accepted money for granting cavalry positions and engaged in other corrupt dealings. His crimes were severe enough to warrant clan extermination, so he committed suicide. At that time, his two brothers and the families of his two marriages were also executed for other crimes. Guanglu Xu Zwei said: "Alas! In ancient times, there were only three clans punished for crimes; could Wang Wenshu's offenses be so severe that five entire clans were exterminated at the same time?"

## 36

After Wenshu died, his family's assets amounted to thousands of gold pieces. Several years later, Yin Qi also died of illness while serving as Duwei of Huaiyang; his family's assets were less than fifty gold pieces. He had executed many people in Huaiyang, and upon his death, his enemies wanted to burn his corpse; however, the body disappeared and was secretly taken back for burial.

## 37

Since the time of Wenshu and others who governed through cruelty, local governors, Duwei (military commissioners), and officials at the level of 2000-dan salaries who wished to govern effectively generally followed Wenshu's methods. As a result, commoners and officials alike became increasingly disrespectful toward laws, and banditry grew rampant. In Nanyang there were Mei Mian and Bai Zheng; in Chu, Yin Zhong and Du Shao; in Qi, Xu Bo; between Yan and Zhao, there were Jian Lu, Fan Sheng, and others. Large groups numbering up to several thousand declared their own titles, attacked cities and towns, seized weapons from government arsenals, released criminals sentenced to death, bound and humiliated commandery governors and Duwei officials, killed high-ranking officers of the 2000-dan rank, and issued proclamations ordering counties to prepare food supplies quickly; Smaller groups numbering in the hundreds robbed and looted villages and towns, too numerous to count. At this time, the emperor began sending Zhongcheng of the Censorate and Changshi (chief clerk) of the Chancellor's office to oversee suppression efforts. Even this was still not enough to suppress the unrest, so the emperor sent Guanglu 大夫 Fan Kun, various Duwei (military commanders), and former Nine Ministers such as Zhang De. They were dressed in embroidered uniforms, carried imperial edicts, and wielded tiger-shaped seals to mobilize troops for suppression campaigns. In major operations, they beheaded more than 10,000 rebels at a time; those who provided food or drink to the rebels were also punished by law, with entire commanderies implicated. Some cases involved thousands of people being executed. After several years, they finally captured many of the rebel leaders. Some scattered soldiers and deserters regrouped in mountainous areas, forming gangs that lived together in clusters; the authorities could do nothing about them. At this time, a law called the "Shenming Law" was enacted. It stated that if bandit groups arose and were not discovered, or if those discovered were not captured in sufficient numbers, all officials from the 2000-dann rank down to minor local officers responsible would be executed. Afterward, low-ranking officials feared punishment and, even when bandits appeared, dared not report them, fearing they could not capture enough to meet the quota. This caused accumulated blame on their superiors in the commandery, so local governments also encouraged silence. Thus, bandits gradually increased in number; officials at all levels concealed crimes from each other and used legalistic language to evade responsibility.

## 38

Jian Xuan was a native of Yang. He served as an assistant official in the governor's office of Hedong, known for being upright and incorruptible. General Wei Qing was sent to Hedong to purchase horses; he saw that Xuan was incorruptible and recommended him to the emperor, who summoned him to serve as Cheng of Dajiu (Stable Office). He performed his official duties competently and gradually rose in rank to become a censor and later Zhongcheng. He was assigned to investigate the cases of Zhu Fu Yan and the rebellion in Huainan. Using subtle legal interpretations, he harshly condemned many people to death; he became known for his boldness in resolving difficult cases. He was repeatedly dismissed and reinstated, serving as a censor or Zhongcheng for nearly twenty years. When Wang Wenshu lost his post as Zhongwei, Xuan became Neishi of Zuo. In governing, he paid attention to even the smallest details. Matters of any size were handled by his own hand; he personally arranged county officials and managed supplies. Local officers and magistrates could not act arbitrarily, as he strictly enforced severe punishments against them. After several years in office, the entire commandery was well governed with minor affairs handled efficiently. Yet it was only Xuan who could elevate small matters to great achievements by applying his efforts effectively; however, this approach proved difficult to sustain as a long-term system. He was eventually dismissed from office. When he became Youfufeng (Right Assistant of Fengtai), he was involved in a case against Chen Xin, who had fled and hidden in the Shanglin 苑. Xuan ordered Mei Ling to kill Xin by force. When the officers attacked Xin, they shot an arrow that hit the gate of Shanglin 苑. As a result, Xuan was investigated for legal violations; he was accused of committing great treason and sentenced to clan extermination. He committed suicide. At this time, Du Zhou came into favor and was appointed to office.

## 39

Du Zhou was a native of Duyan, Nanyang. Yi Zong served as governor of Nanyang and regarded Du Zhou as his enforcer; he recommended him to become an officer in the Tingwei. Serving under Zhang Tang, who often praised his integrity and competence, Du Zhou was promoted to censor. He was sent to investigate losses along the frontier; he condemned and executed many people in court cases. His reports pleased the emperor, so he was entrusted with important duties. He worked alongside Jian Xuan and alternated as Zhongcheng for more than ten years.

## 40

His governance style was similar to Xuan's, but he acted with greater caution and deliberation; outwardly lenient, yet deeply calculating and severe at the core. When Xuan became Zonian Shi (Left Assistant of Nanshi) and Zhou became Tingwei, their governance followed the style of Zhang Tang but was even more adept at anticipating and responding to imperial will. For those the emperor wished to eliminate, they would find ways to entrap them; for those the emperor wanted to pardon, they kept them in prolonged detention and subtly revealed their innocence. A guest once rebuked Du Zhou, saying: "You are the emperor's judge who should uphold justice. Yet you do not follow the written laws but instead decide cases solely based on the emperor's personal inclinations." "Is it truly like this for those in judicial office?" Du Zhou replied, "Where do these three feet of law come from originally? The previous emperor's decrees became laws, and the current emperor's approvals were compiled as edicts. What is considered just in one era may not be so in another—how can there be any ancient law that remains valid?"

## 41

By the time Du Zhou became Tingwei, imperial orders for special investigations increased even further. Officials of the second thousand-dan rank who were detained in custody overlapped continuously between new and old cases, never dropping below more than a hundred people. Local officials from the commanderies and major government offices submitted these cases to the Tingwei; in one year, over a thousand memorials were received. In major cases, hundreds of people were arrested and interrogated as witnesses, while even minor cases involved dozens of individuals; some from thousands of miles away, others only a few hundred li distant. When the cases were consolidated for trial, officials would press the accused as if they had already been formally charged and impeached; those who did not confess were forced to do so through corporal punishment. As a result, whenever people heard of an arrest warrant being issued, they would flee or hide. Some cases dragged on for so long that they endured multiple amnesties and lasted over ten years, yet accusations continued to be made against the accused. Most of these charges were ultimately classified as crimes of great treason or worse. The Tingwei and central government offices handling imperial orders for arrests detained up to 60,000 or 70,000 people, while local officials added over 100,000 more.

## 42

Du Zhou was later dismissed from office, but eventually became Zhijinshi (a high-ranking official in charge of palace security). He pursued bandits and severely punished the nephews and grandsons of Sang Hongyang and Empress Wei. The emperor regarded him as diligent and impartial, and promoted him to Yushi Daifu (Minister of Justice). He had two sons who both served as commandery governors on opposite sides of the Yellow River. Their governance was even more brutal and cruel than that of Wang Wenshu and others. When Du Zhou was first summoned to serve as a Tingwei official, he had only one horse, which was not in good condition; After holding office for many years and rising to the rank of Three Excellencies, his descendants were granted high official positions, and his family's wealth accumulated into tens of thousands.

## 43

Grand Historian said: From Zhi Du to Du Zhou, these ten men all gained a reputation for their harsh and ruthless methods. Yet Zhi Du was upright and bold; he upheld justice and debated matters of great national importance. Zhang Tang, with his understanding of the emperor's will, rose and fell in power along with imperial favor. He frequently advised on whether matters were appropriate or not, and the state benefited from his convenience and effectiveness. Zhao Yu at times adhered to the law and upheld righteousness. Du Zhou flattered those in power, gaining influence through his reticence and silence. After Zhang Tang's death, the legal system became more tightly woven with increased accusations of severity; official affairs gradually deteriorated and were neglected. The Nine Ministers merely fulfilled their duties in a perfunctory manner, barely managing to correct their own mistakes, let alone have time to consider matters beyond the strict letter of the law! Yet among these ten men, the upright ones were sufficient to serve as exemplary models, while the corrupt could serve as warnings. In terms of strategy and instruction for preventing corruption and stopping wrongdoing, all of them displayed a certain level of refinement, possessing both literary and martial qualities. Though harsh and cruel, they were still worthy of their positions. As for those like Feng Dang, the governor of Shu who brutally suppressed people; Li Zhen of Guanghan who arbitrarily used crucifixion; Mi Pu of Dongjun who cut off someone's neck with a saw; Luo Bi of Tianshui who unjustly executed by pressing salt on wounds; Chu Guang of Hedong who killed recklessly; Wuji and Yin Zhou of Jingzhao, whose cruelty was as venomous as that of vipers; Yan Feng of Shuiheng, who sold judicial favors through brutal beatings—what need is there to count them all! What need is there even to mention them?
