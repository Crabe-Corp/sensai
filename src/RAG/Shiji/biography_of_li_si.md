# Biography of Li Si

## 1

Li Si was a native of Shangcai, Chu. When he was young, he served as a minor official in the commandery and saw rats in the latrine of the government office feeding on unclean food. These rats were near people and dogs and often frightened him. Li Si entered the granary and observed the rats there, which fed on stored grain and lived under spacious eaves without fear of people or dogs. At this point, Li Si sighed and said: "The virtue or lack thereof in a person is like that of rats; it depends entirely on the environment one places oneself in!"

## 2

He then studied the art of kingship under Xun Qing. After completing his studies, he judged that King of Chu was not worth serving and that all six states were weak, with none offering an opportunity to achieve great accomplishments. He therefore wished to go west into Qin. He bid farewell to Xun Qing, saying: "I have heard that one must not be idle when the opportunity arises. Now, with ten-thousand-chariot states vying for dominance, it is those who travel and seek their fortune who will hold positions of authority." "Now, King Qin wishes to swallow the entire world and rule as emperor; this is the time for commoners to strive and the season for itinerant persuaders." "To remain in a lowly position without making plans is like an animal gazing at meat—it may have a human face but behaves no better than a beast." "Therefore, no disgrace is greater than being lowly and despised, and no sorrow is deeper than poverty and hardship." "To remain for a long time in a lowly position, in a place of hardship, to reject the world and despise profit, and to entrust oneself to non-action is not the nature of a true scholar." "Therefore, I am going west to persuade King Qin.'"

## 3

When he arrived in Qin, he coincided with the death of Zhuangxiang Wang. Li Si then sought to become a retainer for Lord Wenxin Hou Lü Buwei, the prime minister of Qin; Lü Buwei regarded him as talented and appointed him as an attendant-official. Li Si thus gained the opportunity to advise, persuading King Qin by saying: "Those who wish to achieve something must eliminate their doubts. "To accomplish great feats lies in taking advantage of weaknesses and enduring patiently." "In the past, King Mu of Qin became a hegemon but ultimately did not unify the six eastern states to the east. Why was that?" "The feudal lords were still numerous, and the virtue of the Zhou dynasty had not yet declined; therefore, five hegemons arose in succession, each vying to honor the Zhou royal house." "Since King Xiao of Qin, the Zhou royal house has become humble and weak, while feudal lords have annexed one another. The lands east of the Guan Pass became six states; now, for six generations, Qin has taken advantage of its victories to subdue these feudal lords." "Now, the feudal lords submit to Qin as if they were commanderies and counties." "With Qin's strength and Your Majesty's virtue, it would be as easy as sweeping the ashes from a stove to eliminate the feudal lords, accomplish imperial achievements, unify all under heaven, and this is an opportunity that comes once in ten thousand generations." "If now we delay and do not act urgently, the feudal lords will grow strong again, unite in an alliance, and even with Your Majesty's virtue equaling that of Huangdi, you would be unable to conquer them.'" King Qin then appointed Li Si as Chief Clerk and heeded his strategy, secretly sending strategists with gold and jade to travel and persuade the feudal lords. "For renowned men of the feudal states who could be won over by wealth, generous gifts were given to secure their allegiance; " "those who refused were assassinated with sharp swords." "They sowed discord between the rulers and their ministers of these states, and King Qin then dispatched his capable generals to follow up behind them. " King Qin appointed Li Si as a guest official.

## 4

At this time, the Korean Zheng Guo came to spy on Qin by proposing to build an irrigation canal; soon after, he was discovered. The royal clan and high-ranking ministers of Qin all said to King Qin: "Those from the feudal states who come to serve Qin mostly do so as spies for their lords. We request that all guests be expelled." Li Si's name was also included in the list of those to be expelled. Li Si then submitted a memorial, saying:

## 5

"I have heard the officials' proposal to expel guests. I secretly believe this is a mistake." "In the past, Duke Mu sought talented men; he obtained You Yu from Rong to the west and Bailing Xi from Wan to the east. He welcomed Jian Shu from Song and brought Pei Bao and Gongsun Zhi from Jin." "These five men were not born in Qin, yet Duke Mu employed them. He annexed twenty states and thus became a hegemon over the western Rong tribes." "King Xiao employed Shang Yang's laws, transformed customs and traditions, enriched the people, strengthened the state, made its populace willing to serve, won the loyalty of feudal lords, defeated armies from Chu and Wei, seized thousands of li of territory, and ensured lasting stability and strength." "King Hui employed Zhang Yi's strategy, captured the Three Rivers region, annexed Ba and Shu to the west, reclaimed Shangjun in the north, seized Hanzhong in the south, incorporated the Nine Yi tribes, controlled Yan and Ying, held the strategic pass of Chenggao to the east, carved out fertile lands, and thus broke up the alliance of the six states, compelling them to face west and serve Qin. His achievements have lasted until today." "King Zhao obtained Fan Jiu, dismissed Rang Hou, expelled Huayang, strengthened the royal house, curtailed private factions, gradually devoured feudal states, and enabled Qin to accomplish imperial achievements." "These four kings all achieved their success through the contributions of guest officials." "From this, it is clear—how have these guests wronged Qin?" "If the four kings had rejected guests and refused to admit them, or dismissed talented men without employing them, then Qin would have lacked wealth and prosperity and never gained its reputation for strength."

## 6

"Now, Your Majesty possesses the jade from Kunshan Mountain, owns treasures like Sui and He, wears the pearl of Mingyue, wields the sword Tai'e, rides the fine horse Xianli, raises the emerald phoenix banner, and sets up the drum of Lingtu. "These several treasures were not produced in Qin at all, yet Your Majesty values them highly—why is that?" "If only things native to Qin are acceptable, then luminous jade would not adorn the court, ivory and rhinoceros artifacts would not be treasured as ornaments, women from Zheng and Wei would not fill the harem, swift horses like Juji wouldn't populate the stables, gold and tin from Jiangnan would go unused, and cinnabar and pigments from western Shu would not serve for dyes. "If only items originating from Qin can be used to decorate the harem, fill the lower chambers, please the mind and delight the senses, then hairpins of Wan zhu, earrings with Fuji pendants, Agao silk robes, and embroidered ornaments would not appear before Your Majesty. Nor would graceful, refined beauties from Zhao stand by your side." "Striking clay pots, tapping earthen jars, playing the zeng and slapping one's thighs while singing and shouting in a raucous manner to please the ears (and eyes) is truly Qin music; " "Music from Zheng, Wei, Sangjian, Zhao, Yu, Wu, and Xiang is the music of other states." "Now we abandon striking clay pots and tapping earthen jars in favor of Zheng and Wei music, discard the zeng for Zhao and Yu—why is this so?" "It is merely to satisfy immediate pleasure and provide suitable entertainment." "But when it comes to selecting people, this is not the case. " "They do not inquire whether one is capable or not, nor consider right from wrong; those who are not Qin-born are dismissed and guest officials are expelled." "Thus, what is valued lies in beauty, music, pearls, and jade, while what is despised lies in the people." "This is not the method by which to dominate within the seas and control the feudal lords."

## 7

"I have heard that a wide territory produces more grain, a large state has many people, and strong military forces make soldiers brave. " "Therefore, Mount Tai does not reject soil or dirt, which is why it can achieve such great height; " "rivers and seas do not choose between small streams, which is why they become so deep; " "a sovereign does not reject the common people, which is why he can illuminate his virtue." "Therefore, the land has no four directions as a boundary; people have no distinction of foreign or native. The four seasons are abundant and beautiful, and ghosts and gods bestow blessings—this is why the Five Emperors and Three Kings were invincible." "Now, however, we are abandoning the common people to enrich enemy states and expelling guests to benefit feudal lords. This causes talented men from all under heaven to retreat in fear of heading west and hesitate to enter Qin at all—this is what is called 'lending weapons to bandits and providing food for thieves.'"

## 8

"Objects not produced in Qin yet possessing great value are numerous; " "Scholars not born in Qin who still wish to be loyal are also many." "Now, by expelling guests to strengthen enemy states and harming the people to benefit our enemies, we weaken ourselves internally while sowing resentment among feudal lords externally. To seek a state free from danger is impossible."

## 9

King Qin then rescinded the order to expel guests, restored Li Si's position, and ultimately employed his strategies. He rose in rank to become Minister of Justice (Tingwei). After more than twenty years, Qin ultimately unified the entire world, honored its ruler as emperor, and appointed Li Si as Chancellor. He destroyed city walls of commanderies and counties, melted down weapons, and showed that they would not be used again. He ensured Qin did not grant even a square piece of land as fiefs, nor establish sons and brothers as kings or meritorious officials as feudal lords, so that future generations would be free from the troubles of war.

## 10

In the 34th year of Emperor Qin Shi Huang's reign, a banquet was held at Xianyang Palace. The Doctor of Literature, Pushe Zhou Qingchen and others praised the emperor's power and virtue. The Qi native Chunyu Yue offered advice, saying: "I have heard that the kings of Yin and Zhou ruled for over a thousand years by enfeoffing their sons, brothers, and meritorious officials to serve as supporting branches. "Now Your Majesty rules over all under heaven, yet your sons and brothers are commoners. Should sudden troubles arise like those of Tian Chang or the Six Ministers, with no one to assist or advise you, how can they be resolved?" "I have never heard of a matter that does not follow ancient precedents yet endures for long." "Now, Zhou Qingchen and others flatter Your Majesty openly, worsening your mistakes—they are not loyal ministers.'" Emperor Qin Shi Huang referred the debate to the Chancellor. The Chancellor refuted their argument, dismissed their words as fallacious, and submitted a memorial stating: "In ancient times, the world was fragmented and chaotic, with no one able to unify it. Thus, feudal lords arose in competition. Their speeches all cited antiquity to harm the present, adorned false words to confuse reality, and individuals praised their own private schools of thought while criticizing institutions established by Your Majesty. "Now that Your Majesty unifies all under heaven, distinguishing right from wrong to establish a single authority; " "private schools, however, conspire to teach doctrines contrary to the state's regulations. Upon hearing an edict issued, they immediately debate it according to their own private teachings. Within, they criticize in secret; outside, they gossip in alleyways, using criticism of Your Majesty as a name for virtue and claiming superiority by following different paths, leading subjects into spreading slander." "If this is not prohibited, then the sovereign's authority will weaken above while factions form below. "It would be advantageous to prohibit it." "I request that all who possess writings of literature, poetry, the Book of Songs and Shu, or works from a hundred schools be purged and dismissed." "Those who do not remove these writings within thirty days after the order is issued shall be branded and conscripted for labor at dawn on city walls." "The books that may remain are those related to medicine, divination, fortune-telling, and agriculture. " "If anyone wishes to study, the officials shall be their teachers.'" Emperor Qin Shi Huang approved this proposal and confiscated all books of poetry, history, and the hundred schools to dull the minds of the people, ensuring that no one in the world would use antiquity to criticize the present. Clarifying legal statutes and establishing laws and decrees all began with Emperor Qin Shi Huang. Standardizing written scripts. He constructed palaces and pavilions, scattered throughout the entire realm. The following year, he embarked on another tour of inspection, repelling the four barbarian tribes from beyond the borders—Li Si played a significant role in these efforts.

## 11

Li Si's eldest son, You, served as governor of the Three Rivers region; all his sons married princesses of Qin, and his daughters were all wed to princes of Qin. When the Three Rivers governor, Li You, returned to Xianyang on leave, Li Si hosted a banquet at his home. High-ranking officials all attended to offer their congratulations, and carriages and horsemen filled the courtyard in thousands. Li Si sighed deeply and said: "Alas! "I have heard from Xun Qing that 'things forbidden to grow too great will collapse.'" "I, Li Si, was once a commoner from Shangcai, an ordinary villager. The sovereign did not know my incompetence and yet promoted me to this position." "Now, among the ministers of the empire, none hold a higher rank than I. One might say my wealth and honor have reached their peak." "When things reach their extreme, they decline—I do not know where this journey will end!"

## 12

In the tenth month of the 37th year of Emperor Qin Shi Huang's reign, he embarked on a tour to Kuaiji, traveled along the coast, and went north as far as Langya. The Chancellor Li Si, the Director of the Central Chariot Office Zhao Gao—who also served concurrently as the official in charge of seals and insignias—both accompanied him. Emperor Qin Shi Huang had more than twenty sons; his eldest son, Fu Su, was often directly remonstrating with the emperor and thus was sent to supervise troops in Shangjun Commandery under General Meng Tian. His youngest son, Hu Hai, whom he loved dearly, requested to accompany him on the tour and was granted permission by the emperor. The other sons did not accompany him.

## 13

In the seventh month of that year, Emperor Qin Shi Huang arrived at Shachu and fell seriously ill. He ordered Zhao Gao to write a letter granting it to his son Fu Su: "Transfer your troops to Meng Tian and join the funeral procession in Xianyang for burial." The letter had been sealed but not yet given to an envoy when Emperor Qin Shi Huang died. The letter and the imperial seal were in Zhao Gao's possession. Only his son Hu Hai, Chancellor Li Si, Zhao Gao himself, and five or six favored eunuchs knew of Emperor Qin Shi Huang's death; none of the other officials did. Li Si believed that since the emperor had died while away from the capital and there was no clear designated crown prince, they should keep it secret. They placed Emperor Qin Shi Huang's body in a lacquered carriage, and as before, officials submitted reports and presented food. Eunuchs would immediately relay the emperor's approval of these matters from within the lacquered carriage.

## 14

Zhao Gao therefore withheld the imperial seal and letter that had been given to Fu Su, and told Prince Hu Hai: "The emperor has died without issuing an edict appointing any of his sons as kings, but instead gave a special letter only to the eldest son. "When the eldest son arrives and is immediately declared emperor, you will have not an inch of territory. What are we to do about this?" Hu Hai said: "That is indeed a problem." "I have heard that an enlightened ruler knows his ministers, and a wise father understands his son. "The emperor has given up his life without bestowing any fiefdoms upon his sons—what can be said about this!" Zhao Gao said, "That is not the case. "Now, the power of the entire empire—its survival or destruction—rests only with you, me, and the Chancellor. I hope you will consider this carefully." "Moreover, being a master over others or being mastered by others—exercising control or being controlled—are these things to be spoken of on the same day!" Hu Hai said, "To depose an elder brother and install a younger one is unjust; "to disobey the father's decree out of fear of death is unfilial; "with shallow ability and mediocre talent, yet relying on others' achievements to seize power—that is incompetence. These three violations of virtue—disobedience, unfilial piety, and incompetence—will lead the empire not to accept me; my life will be in peril, and ancestral sacrifices for our state may cease." Zhao Gao said: "I have heard that Tang and Wu killed their sovereigns, yet the empire praised them as righteous—this was not considered disloyalty. "The ruler of Wei killed his father, but the state of Wei recorded his virtue; Confucius wrote about it and did not consider him unfilial. "Great actions need not be bound by minor caution, and great virtue does not shun taking action; local customs vary in appropriateness, and officials differ in their duties. "Therefore, to focus on small matters while neglecting the great will surely bring harm later. "To hesitate and waver will inevitably lead to regret afterward." "Decide boldly and act with courage, and even ghosts and gods will yield; success will surely follow." "I hope you proceed with this!" Hu Hai sighed and said, "Now that the great funeral rites have not yet begun and mourning ceremonies are incomplete, how can I involve the Chancellor in such a matter!" Zhao Gao said: "Opportunity! Opportunity! There is no time to deliberate!" "Seize the moment with haste and vigor, for one fears being too late!"

## 15

Hu Hai accepted Zhao Gao's words. Zhao Gao said, "If we do not consult the Chancellor, I fear the matter will not succeed; allow me to discuss it with him on your behalf." Zhao Gao then said to Chancellor Li Si: "The emperor has died and left a letter for the eldest son, instructing him to join the funeral in Xianyang and be declared heir. "The letter has not yet been delivered; now that the emperor is dead, no one else knows about it." "The letter and seal given to the eldest son are in Prince Hu Hai's possession; the designation of a crown prince now rests solely on your words, Lord Chancellor, and mine." "What do you think we should do about this?" Li Si said, "How can you utter words that would bring ruin to the state! "This is not a matter for ministers to discuss!" Zhao Gao said: "Lord Chancellor, do you yourself think your ability compares with Meng Tian's? "Whose achievements surpass those of Meng Tian? "Who is more far-sighted and reliable than Meng Tian? "Who has fewer grievances from the people than Meng Tian? "And who is more trusted by the eldest son than Meng Tian?" Li Si said: "In all these five aspects, I am inferior to Meng Tian; yet why do you hold me to such a high standard?" Zhao Gao said: "I am merely an eunuch, fortunate enough to have entered the Qin palace through my clerical skills and served for more than twenty years. I have never seen a Qin emperor who dismissed or executed a prime minister or meritorious official while bestowing fiefs upon their successors; in the end, all such officials were punished and perished. "The emperor has more than twenty sons, a fact you yourself are aware of. "The eldest son is resolute, brave, and trustworthy, inspiring his soldiers. If he ascends the throne, he will surely appoint Meng Tian as chancellor. It is clear that you, Lord Chancellor, will never return to your hometown holding the seal of a marquis." Zhao Gao had been entrusted with teaching and instructing Hu Hai for several years, guiding him in legal matters; he had never seen any faults or mistakes. "He is kind, sincere, and generous, valuing scholars over wealth. Though wise in mind, he is reserved in speech, always courteous to men of virtue. Among the emperor's sons, none match his qualities—he would be a suitable heir." "You must deliberate and decide accordingly." Li Si said: "You should return to your proper place! "I follow the emperor's decree and accept Heaven's will—what is there for me to deliberate?" Zhao Gao said: "Safety can turn into danger, and danger can become safety. "If safety and danger are not fixed, how can we value wisdom?" Li Si said: "I, Li Si, am but a commoner from the lanes of Shangcai. The emperor favored me by appointing me chancellor and bestowing upon me the title of Tong Hou; my descendants have all attained high positions and generous salaries. Therefore, he entrusted to me the fate of life and death, safety and danger of the state. "How could I betray such trust!" "A loyal minister does not avoid death in order to achieve righteousness; a filial son does not neglect duty and face danger. All ministers need do is uphold their duties." "Do not speak of this again, or you will cause me to commit an offense." Zhao Gao said: "I have heard that a sage does not adhere to fixed ways, but adapts to change and follows the times; he sees the branches and knows the root, observes the direction and understands the destination. "Things have always changed in this way—how can there be a constant law!" "Now, the fate of the empire rests with Prince Hu Hai; I, Zhao Gao, will achieve my ambitions. "Moreover, to control from outside what is within is called confusion; to dominate the superior with the inferior is called banditry. "Therefore, when autumn frost falls, grass and flowers wither; when water stirs, all things arise—this is an inevitable result. "Why are you so slow to understand this?" Li Si said: "I have heard that when the state of Jin changed its crown prince, three generations suffered unrest; "the brothers of Duke Huan of Qi contended for the throne and ended up being killed in disgrace; "King Zhou of Shang executed his relatives and refused to heed remonstrances, resulting in the destruction of his state into a wasteland and endangering the dynasty: these three violations of Heaven's will led to ancestral temples no longer receiving sacrifices." "I am but an ordinary man like others—how could I possibly devise such plans!" Zhao Gao said: "When the ruler and subjects are united, stability can endure; "when the court and the realm are as one, there will be no duplicity in affairs. "If you follow my plan, you will forever hold the title of marquis and be honored as a lord for generations. You will surely enjoy the longevity of Qiao Song and the wisdom of Kongzi and Mozi." "If you now abandon this opportunity, disaster will befall your descendants—this is enough to chill one's heart. "The wise turn misfortune into fortune; where do you stand in such a choice?" Li Si then looked up to the sky and sighed, shedding tears as he lamented: "Alas! "I alone have encountered this chaotic age. Having already failed in my duty of death, to whom can I entrust my fate!" Thus Li Si agreed with Zhao Gao's plan. Zhao Gao then reported to Hu Hai: "I will convey the prince's command as if it were an imperial decree from the crown prince and present it to the Chancellor. Chancellor Li Si would dare not refuse the order!"

## 16

Thus, they conspired together and forged a document as if it were an edict from Emperor Qin Shi Huang to the Chancellor, declaring Prince Hu Hai as crown prince. They also forged another letter to be given to the eldest son Fu Su: "I have toured the empire, offering sacrifices at famous mountains and shrines to the gods in hopes of prolonging my life. "Now, Fu Su and General Meng Tian have led an army of hundreds of thousands to garrison the frontier for more than ten years. They have failed to advance, their troops are greatly depleted, and they have achieved no merit at all. Instead, they repeatedly submitted letters with blunt criticisms against my actions, complaining that I did not relieve them from duty and appoint Fu Su as crown prince; day and night, they harbor resentment." "As a son, Fu Su is unfilial. I hereby bestow upon him a sword to commit suicide with!" "General Meng Tian has lived in the frontier alongside Fu Su and failed to correct his behavior; he must have known of this plot. "As a minister, Meng Tian is disloyal. I hereby order him to be executed and transfer his troops to the deputy general Wang Li." They sealed the letter with the imperial seal, sent Hu Hai's guest to deliver it to Fu Su in Shangjun Commandery.

## 17

When the envoy arrived and delivered the letter, Fu Su wept, went into his private quarters, and was about to commit suicide. Meng Tian stopped Fu Su, saying: "The emperor is away from the capital and has not yet designated a crown prince. He entrusted me with thirty thousand troops to defend the frontier while you served as his supervisor—this was an immense responsibility for the empire. "Now, a single envoy arrives and you are about to kill yourself—how do we know this is not a deception? "Please request confirmation first. After receiving it, then commit suicide—it is not too late." The envoy repeatedly urged him to act. Fu Su, being a kind-hearted man, said to Meng Tian: "If the father commands his son to die, how can one still request confirmation!" And so he committed suicide. Meng Tian refused to die, and the envoy immediately handed him over to local officials, who imprisoned him in Yangzhou.

## 18

When the envoy returned with news of this, Hu Hai, Li Si, and Zhao Gao were greatly pleased. Upon arriving in Xianyang, they announced the emperor's death, and the crown prince was enthroned as Emperor Er Shi. Zhao Gao was appointed Minister of the Imperial Guards (Langzhong Ling) and remained in constant attendance, wielding power within the palace.

## 19

When Emperor Er Shi was at leisure, he summoned Zhao Gao and discussed matters with him, saying: "A man's life in this world is like a galloping six-horse chariot passing through a narrow gap. I have already taken control of the empire, and I wish to fully indulge my senses in what pleases them, to exhaustively pursue the joys that satisfy my heart and mind. In doing so, I hope to bring peace to our ancestral temple and happiness to all the people, thus securing lasting rule over the empire for the rest of my life. Is this a viable path?" Zhao Gao said: "This is something that a wise ruler can achieve, but it is precisely what an ignorant and chaotic ruler would prohibit. "Please allow me to explain; I dare not avoid the punishment of cleaver and axe, but I hope Your Majesty will give it some thought." "The plot at Shaqiu has caused doubts among the imperial princes and high-ranking ministers. These princes are all Your Majesty's elder brothers, and the ministers were appointed by the late emperor." "Now that Your Majesty has just ascended the throne, these men are all discontented and may not be obedient; they might even cause a rebellion." "Moreover, Meng Tian is already dead, and Meng Yi commands troops stationed outside. I tremble with fear, dreading that this situation will not end well." "How can Your Majesty possibly enjoy such pleasures?" Emperor Er Shi asked, "Then what should be done about this?" Zhao Gao said: "Enforce strict laws and harsh punishments, ordering that those who commit crimes be punished along with their families, even to the point of exterminating entire clans. Eliminate high-ranking ministers and distance close relatives; Make the poor rich and elevate those who are lowly to positions of honor. Remove entirely all the late emperor's old ministers, replacing them with those personally trusted by Your Majesty and placing them in close proximity to you. In this way, the hidden virtue will return to Your Majesty; evils are removed and treacherous plots blocked. All ministers will be blessed with benefits and receive great kindness from you. Then, Your Majesty may rest in peace, indulge your desires, and enjoy honor and pleasure without worry. "There is no better plan than this." Emperor Er Shi approved Zhao Gao's words and thus revised the laws. Therefore, whenever high-ranking ministers or imperial princes committed crimes, they were immediately referred to Zhao Gao, who was ordered to investigate and handle the cases. They executed high-ranking ministers such as Meng Yi, and twelve imperial princes were publicly beheaded in Xianyang market; ten princesses were similarly killed at Du. Their possessions were confiscated by the county authorities, and countless others were implicated through collective punishment.

## 20

Prince Gao wanted to flee, but feared that his entire clan would be executed. So he submitted a memorial saying: "When the late emperor was in good health, I was granted food when entering court and allowed to ride in a carriage when leaving. The robes from the imperial wardrobe were bestowed upon me; the fine horses from the central stables, I was also granted. I should have followed him in death but failed to do so; as a son, this is unfilial, and as a minister, it is disloyal. An unfaithful man has no name to stand in the world; I beg to be allowed to die with him, and wish to be buried at the foot of Lishan Mountain. I only hope Your Majesty will show me mercy." When the memorial was submitted, Hu Hai was greatly pleased. He summoned Zhao Gao to show it to him and said: "Can this be called urgent?" Zhao Gao replied, "A subject should be so preoccupied with the fear of death that he has no time to plot any rebellion!" Hu Hai approved his request and granted ten thousand coins for burial expenses.

## 21

The laws and punishments became increasingly harsh, causing every minister to live in constant fear; many were eager to rebel. They also constructed the Afang Palace, built the straight road and express roads, imposed even heavier taxes and levies, and conscripted endless numbers of men for military service and corvée labor. At this time, the Chu conscripts Chen Sheng and Wu Guang rebelled, rising in Shandong. Outstanding leaders emerged one after another, proclaiming themselves marquises and kings, rebelling against the Qin dynasty. Their forces advanced as far as Hongmen before retreating. Li Si repeatedly wanted to request a private audience to offer advice, but Emperor Er Shi did not permit it. Emperor Er Shi then questioned Li Si, saying: "I have heard a private discussion from Han Feizi, who said, 'When Yao ruled the empire, his hall was only three chi high, the beams were left uncarved, and the thatched roof was not trimmed. Even the accommodations of an inn would be less arduous than this." "In winter he wore a deer hide robe, and in summer a hemp tunic. His food was coarse grain porridge and stew made from wild vegetables; his rice was cooked in earthen bowls and his soup ladled with earthen ladles. Even the rations of a gatekeeper were not as meager as this." "Yu the Great chiseled open Longmen, connected Daxia, dredged nine rivers, constructed nine embankments, and drained stagnant water to send it into the sea. His thighs had no flesh left, his shins were hairless, his hands and feet calloused, and his face and complexion darkened with labor, until he died outside and was buried at Kuaiji. The hardships of a slave or captive could not surpass this." "Yet, if the value of possessing the empire is such, how could it be that one desires to endure physical hardship and mental strain, living like a guest in an inn, eating food no better than a gatekeeper's ration, and performing labor akin to that of a slave?" "This is what the unworthy strive for; it is not the pursuit of the virtuous." The virtuous man who rules the empire uses the entire world solely to satisfy himself; this is what makes possessing the empire valuable. The so-called virtuous ruler must be able to bring peace to the world and govern its people. Now, if one cannot even benefit himself, how can he possibly rule over all under heaven!" "I wish to be granted the will and desire for great things, to enjoy lasting dominion over the empire without harm. What should I do about this?" Li Si's son, Li You, was governor of Sanchuan Commandery. When the rebel forces led by Wu Guang and others moved westward to seize territory, he failed to stop them. Zhang Han defeated and drove back Wu Guang's forces, and imperial envoys investigated the Sanchuan region repeatedly, censuring Li Si for holding a high ministerial position yet allowing bandits to reach such levels. Li Si, fearing for his life and valuing his rank and salary highly, did not know what to do. So he flattered Emperor Er Shi's wishes in an attempt to gain favor, and submitted a written response saying:

## 22

"A wise ruler must be one who can fully uphold the Way and employ the method of strict supervision and accountability." "If he supervises and holds them accountable, then ministers will not dare to withhold their abilities but must strive wholeheartedly for their ruler. "This defines the fixed relationship between minister and sovereign, clarifies the proper conduct of superiors and subordinates; thus, all in the world—whether virtuous or unworthy—will dare not fail to exert their utmost efforts for their ruler." "Therefore, the sovereign alone holds absolute authority over all under heaven and is subject to no constraints. "He can thus attain the utmost in pleasure; this is a wise and enlightened ruler, one who cannot be ignored!"

## 23

Hence, the Shenzidian says: "To possess the empire yet not indulge freely is called 'bearing the empire as a fetter.' There is no other reason for this than the ruler's failure to enforce strict accountability and instead devoting himself laboriously to the people of the world, like Yao or Yu. Hence it is termed 'a fetter.'" "To fail to cultivate the clear methods of Shen Buhai and Han Feizi, or to follow the path of strict accountability, yet instead merely strive to endure physical hardship and exert mental effort for the sake of the people, sacrificing oneself for the common folk—this is nothing more than being a servant to the commoners, not ruling over all under heaven. What value can such an approach hold!" To make people serve oneself elevates one's own status while degrading others; to sacrifice oneself for the sake of others is to degrade oneself and elevate them. Therefore, those who serve others are lowly, while those for whom service is rendered are esteemed; from ancient times to the present, there has never been a different case. The reason why in antiquity the virtuous were honored was because they held high status; and the reason why the unworthy were despised was because of their lowliness. Yet Yao and Yu sacrificed themselves for the world, so people followed by honoring them. But this also deviates from the original intent of respecting virtue; it can truly be called a great error. To call such rulers "fetters" is not inappropriate, is it? This is the fault of failing to enforce strict accountability.

## 24

Hence Han Feizi said: "A kind mother has a wayward son, while a strict household has no unruly slaves"—why is this so? It is because punishment must be applied without fail. Therefore, the laws of Shang Yang prescribed corporal punishment for anyone who discarded ashes on the road. Discarding ashes is a minor offense, yet it was punished with corporal punishment—a severe penalty. Only an enlightened ruler can impose strict supervision for even minor offenses. If a light offense is met with severe oversight, then what of serious crimes? Therefore, the people dare not commit offenses. This is why Han Feizi said: "Common people do not let go of ordinary cloth and thread, while the bandit Zhi does not seize even a hundred dou of molten gold." This is not because common people value such things more or find them more profitable, nor because Zhi desires less. It is also not that Zhi considers a hundred yi of weight to be light in value based on his bandit conduct. If seizing such wealth inevitably leads to immediate punishment, then Zhi would not dare seize even a hundred yi; but if punishment is never certain, then common people will not let go of their ordinary gains. łoż This is why even a city wall five zhang high would not be lightly breached by Lou Ji; even though Mount Tai rises hundreds of ren in height, lame sheep still graze on its slopes. If Lou Ji finds it difficult to breach a five-zhang barrier, how could lame sheep find it easy to ascend the hundred-ren height of Mount Tai? It is because the nature and circumstances of the obstacles differ. The reason why enlightened rulers and sage kings can long remain in positions of honor, hold great power for extended periods, and exclusively monopolize the benefits of all under heaven is not because they follow a different path. Rather, it is because they alone possess the authority to make decisions and strictly enforce accountability, imposing severe punishments so that no one dares to commit offenses. Now, if one does not focus on measures to prevent wrongdoing but instead follows the ways of a kind mother who unintentionally spoils her son, then he has failed to understand the teachings of the sage. If one cannot practice the methods of the sages, then what else is he but a servant to all under heaven? What purpose does his existence serve?" How pitiable this is!

## 25

Moreover, if frugal and virtuous men of benevolence and righteousness hold positions in court, then licentious pleasures will cease; if ministers who advise with reason and logic stand at the ruler's side, then reckless ambitions will be curbed; and if loyal men who die for their principles are honored in society, then indulgent and decadent pursuits will be abandoned. Therefore, an enlightened ruler can set aside these three influences and solely wield the sovereign's method to control obedient ministers while refining his clear laws. This is why he attains personal prestige and great authority. All virtuous rulers must be able to oppose prevailing customs and eradicate what they dislike, establishing instead what they desire. Thus, when alive, they enjoy respect and authority; after death, they receive posthumous titles of virtue and wisdom. This is why an enlightened ruler makes decisions alone—thus the power does not lie with his ministers. Only then can he extinguish the path of benevolence and righteousness, silence those who speak eloquently but recklessly, hinder the actions of loyal men, block wisdom and insight from within, and rely solely on his own judgment. Thus, externally, he cannot be swayed by the conduct of virtuous and loyal individuals, nor internally can he be moved by arguments or disputes from advisors. Therefore, he is able to act with complete self-indulgence, following his own desires without anyone daring to oppose him. Only in this way can one be said to truly understand the methods of Shen Buhai and Hanfeizi, and to have refined the laws of Shang Yang. There has never been a case where clear laws and well-understood methods led to chaos in the world. Hence it is said, "The Way of kingship is concise and easy to practice." Only an enlightened ruler is capable of carrying it out. If this is done, then the principle of strict accountability will be genuinely implemented. When that happens, ministers will have no malice; when ministers are free from malice, the world will enjoy peace; with a peaceful world, the ruler gains authority and respect; with authority and respect, strict accountability becomes certain; with certainty in accountability, whatever is desired can be achieved; when desires are fulfilled, the state grows wealthy; and as the state prospers, the ruler enjoys abundant pleasures. Therefore, once the method of strict accountability is established, whatever one desires can be obtained without fail. Ministers and commoners will be so busy correcting their own mistakes that they dare not even consider rebellion. In this way, the path of imperial rule is complete, and one may be said to have truly mastered the art of ruler-minister relations. Even if Shen Buhai or Han Feizi were to return to life, they could not surpass this.

## 26

When the memorial was presented, Emperor Er Shi was pleased. Thus, the implementation of strict accountability became even harsher, and officials who heavily taxed the people were considered capable administrators. Emperor Er Shi said: "If this is so, then one may be said to truly possess the ability of strict accountability." The streets were half-filled with criminals, and corpses piled up daily in the marketplace. Those who killed many people were regarded as loyal ministers. Emperor Er Shi said: "If this is so, one may be said to possess the ability of strict accountability."

## 27

Initially, Zhao Gao served as Langzhongling (Minister of the Guards), and had killed many people or settled private grudges. Fearing that ministers might criticize him when they came to court to report affairs, he advised Emperor Er Shi: "The reason why a Son of Heaven is so respected is merely because his presence is heard but not seen; no minister can behold his face, hence the title 'Zhen' (I)." Moreover, although Your Majesty is young and may not yet fully understand all matters of state, if you now sit in court and criticize or reprimand someone unjustly, it will reveal your shortcomings before the ministers. This would not be a way to demonstrate divine wisdom to the people throughout the land. Furthermore, Your Majesty should remain in deep seclusion within the palace and consult with me and the attendants who are well-versed in legal matters. When affairs arise, we will have the means to assess them properly. In this way, ministers would dare not present doubtful or questionable matters, and the whole world would acclaim Your Majesty as a sage ruler." Emperor Er Shi adopted his plan. Thus he no longer sat in court to meet with ministers, but remained secluded within the palace. Zhao Gao often served as an attendant and held power; all affairs were decided by Zhao Gao.

## 28

Zhao Gao, having heard that Li Si had spoken against this, went to see the Chancellor and said: "There are many rebels in Guandong. Now His Majesty urgently orders more conscripts to be sent for building the Afang Palace and is gathering useless things like dogs and horses. I wish to remonstrate, but my position is too lowly. This truly falls within your Excellency's responsibility; why do you not remonstrate?" Li Si said, "That is true. I have long wanted to speak out on this matter. Now that the Emperor does not sit in court, but resides deep within the palace, whatever I wish to say cannot be conveyed. I have no opportunity to meet with him." Zhao Gao said: "If you are truly willing to remonstrate, please allow me to find an opportunity for you to speak with the Emperor." Thus Zhao Gao waited until Emperor Er Shi was engaged in leisure and entertainment, with women present before him. Then he sent someone to inform the Chancellor: "His Majesty is now free; you may come forward to report affairs." The Chancellor arrived at the palace gate and made his request, doing so three times. Emperor Er Shi became angry and said: "I often have many free days, yet the Chancellor never comes." "When I am engaged in private leisure, the Chancellor always comes to request an audience. "Does the Chancellor truly need me so much?" Or is it that he wants to bind me tightly?" Zhao Gao then said: "In this case, the situation is dangerous! The plot at Shaqiu involved the Chancellor as well. Now that Your Majesty has ascended to the throne, yet the Chancellor's rank and wealth have not increased; this indicates he must be hoping for a fiefdom of his own. Moreover, if Your Majesty does not ask me, I dare not speak. The Chancellor's eldest son, Li You, is the governor of Sanchuan. The rebel Chen Sheng and others from Chu are all sons of people from counties near the Chancellor; therefore, these rebels have acted openly in public, passing through Sanchuan, while the city's garrison refused to attack them. Zhao Gao heard of their correspondence but had not yet obtained conclusive evidence, so he dared not report it. Moreover, the Chancellor holds power outside the palace and his authority is greater than Your Majesty's." Emperor Er Shi believed this to be true. He wished to investigate the Chancellor, but fearing that the accusations might not be substantiated, he sent someone to examine and verify whether the governor of Sanchuan had indeed conspired with the rebels. Li Si heard about this.

## 29

At that time, Emperor Er Shi was at Ganquan, where he was watching performances of hudi and yupai entertainments. Unable to meet with the emperor, Li Si submitted a memorial denouncing Zhao Gao's faults and said: "I have heard that when a minister doubts his ruler, it is always dangerous for the state; when a wife suspects her husband, it is inevitably harmful to the family. Now there are ministers who act arbitrarily in taking benefits and inflicting harm, as if they were Your Majesty himself; this is most inconvenient. In the past, Sicheng Zihan served as prime minister of Song; he personally carried out punishments and enforced authority through fear. After a year, he eventually seized power from his ruler. Tian Chang was a minister of King Jian Gong. He held the highest rank in the state, and his private wealth equaled that of the royal treasury. By bestowing favors and virtue upon others, he gained support from both the common people below and the ministers above. Secretly, he seized control of Qi State, killed Zai Yu in the court, then assassinated King Jian Gong during a court session, thereby seizing complete power over Qi. These are facts well known to all under heaven. Now Zhao Gao harbors wicked and licentious ambitions, and engages in dangerous and rebellious actions—just like Sicheng Zihan of Song. His private wealth is comparable to that of the Tian clan in Qi. He combines the treacherous ways of Tian Chang and Sicheng Zihan to seize Your Majesty's authority and prestige, with ambitions akin to those of Han Qi when he became prime minister for King An of Han. If Your Majesty does not take action, I fear that he will soon instigate a rebellion." Emperor Er Shi asked: "What do you mean?" "Zhao Gao was originally a eunuch, yet he did not indulge in reckless desires for peace or change his mind when faced with danger. He maintained clean conduct and cultivated virtue, which brought him to this position. He advanced through loyalty and held office by trustworthiness. I truly regard him as virtuous, but you doubt him—why is that?" Moreover, I was young when my father passed away and knew nothing. I am inexperienced in governing the people, while you are old now; I fear that without your support, I will be cut off from the world. "If I do not entrust Zhao Gao, to whom else should I turn?" "Moreover, Zhao Gao is a man of precision and integrity, strong in ability. He understands the people's sentiments below and can properly attend to my needs above. You must not doubt him." Li Si said: "That is not true. Zhao Gao is originally a lowly person, ignorant of principles. He has insatiable greed and ceaseless desires for profit. Now he holds power second only to the Son of Heaven, yet his ambitions are endless. That is why I say that danger lies ahead." Emperor Er Shi had already placed trust in Zhao Gao and feared that Li Si might kill him, so he secretly informed Zhao Gao. Zhao Gao said: "The only person the Chancellor fears is me, Zhao Gao. If I die, the Chancellor will immediately act as Tian Chang did." Thus Emperor Er Shi said: "Hand Li Si over to Langzhongling!"

## 30

Zhao Gao investigated and punished Li Si. Li Si, bound in chains and confined in prison, looked up to the sky and sighed: "Alas! How tragic!" "What can one do for an unrighteous ruler?" In the past, Jie killed Guan Longfeng; Zhou killed Prince Bi Gan; King Fuchai of Wu killed Wu Zixu. These three ministers—were they not loyal? And yet, none escaped death; their deaths were the result of serving a ruler who was unworthy. Now my wisdom does not match that of these three men, yet Emperor Er Shi's tyranny surpasses even Jie, Zhou, and Fuchai. To die for loyalty is only fitting for me. Moreover, how corrupt has the rule of Emperor Er Shi become! He recently deposed his own brothers and seized the throne, executed loyal ministers while promoting lowly men. He built the Afang Palace at great cost to the people through heavy taxation. I did not fail to remonstrate—but he would not listen to me. All ancient sage kings observed moderation in food and drink, maintained limits on the number of carriages and utensils, regulated palace construction with proper standards. When issuing decrees or initiating projects, they prohibited any expenditure that incurred cost without benefiting the people—thus ensuring long-term peace and stability. Now he acts treacherously toward his brothers, disregarding the consequences; he murders loyal ministers without considering the calamity that will follow; he builds grand palaces and imposes heavy taxes on all under heaven, showing no care for the cost. With these three actions already taken, the people of the world will not obey him. Now, rebels have already seized half of the empire, yet his mind remains unawakened. He still relies on Zhao Gao as an assistant. I must see enemies arriving at Xianyang, with deer roaming freely in the imperial court!"

## 31

Thus Emperor Er Shi ordered Zhao Gao to investigate and imprison the Chancellor, determining his crimes. He charged Li Si and his son Li You with plotting rebellion, arresting all their relatives and guests. Zhao Gao interrogated Li Si, subjecting him to over a thousand beatings until he could no longer endure the pain and falsely confessed. The reason Li Si did not die was because he believed in his eloquence and past contributions, truly having no rebellious intent. He hoped to submit a memorial explaining himself, hoping Emperor Er Shi would awaken to the truth and grant him clemency. From prison, Li Si submitted a letter saying: "I have served as Chancellor in governing the people for over thirty years. I have led the consolidation of Qin territory through its narrow and perilous passes. In the time of previous kings, Qin's territory did not exceed a thousand li, with an army numbering only tens of thousands. I, with my limited abilities, carefully enforced laws and decrees. I secretly supported strategists by providing them with gold and jade, sending them to persuade the feudal lords. In secret, I strengthened military equipment and trained soldiers; I refined governance and education, appointed capable officials, honored meritorious ministers, and enriched their ranks and salaries. Thus, we eventually coerced Han and weakened Wei, defeated Yan and Zhao, subdued Qi and Chu, ultimately annexing all six states, capturing their kings, and establishing the Son of Heaven as ruler of Qin. "This is my first crime." The territory was not only vast, but we also drove the Hu and Mo people northward and pacified the Baiyue in the south to demonstrate Qin's strength. "This is my second crime." I honored high-ranking ministers and elevated their ranks to strengthen loyalty. "This is my third crime." I established ancestral temples and repaired the state altars to demonstrate the ruler's virtue. "This is my fourth crime." I standardized measurements, equalized the bushels and weights, regulated writing styles, and spread these reforms throughout the empire to establish Qin's prestige. "This is my fifth crime." I constructed the imperial roads and built pleasure pavilions to display the ruler's satisfaction. "This is my sixth crime." I eased punishments, reduced taxes and levies, to fulfill the ruler's desire for popular support. The people revered their sovereign, even in death they did not forget him. "This is my seventh crime." A minister who has acted in such a manner, his crimes are more than sufficient to warrant death long ago. "The sovereign has mercifully allowed me to use my full abilities, thus enabling me to survive until now; I hope Your Majesty will examine this matter carefully!" When the letter was submitted, Zhao Gao ordered officials to discard it without presenting it to the emperor, saying, "How could a prisoner write a petition!"

## 32

Zhao Gao sent more than ten of his followers to impersonate Yushi, Yezhe, and Shizhong officials, taking turns to interrogate Li Si repeatedly. Li Si answered truthfully each time, but Zhao Gao would then have him beaten again. Later, the Second Emperor sent someone to investigate Li Si; Li Si assumed it was the same as before and finally dared not change his story, thus he confessed. When the report was submitted for imperial approval, Emperor Er Shi rejoiced and said, "Without Lord Zhao, I would have nearly been deceived by the Chancellor." When the officials sent by Emperor Er Shi to investigate the governor of Sanchuan arrived, Xiang Liang had already attacked and killed him. When the envoys came, coinciding with the Chancellor's subordinates being arrested, Zhao Gao fabricated false accusations of rebellion against them.

## 33

In the seventh month of the second year of Emperor Er Shi's reign (August 28, 208 BCE), Li Si was subjected to all five punishments and executed by decapitation in Xianyang marketplace. As Li Si left the prison to be executed, he was accompanied by his second son. Looking at him, he said, "I had wished with you again to lead yellow dogs and go out through the eastern gate of Shangcai to chase hares; how could that ever happen now!" Thus father and son wept together, and then all three clans were exterminated.

## 34

After Li Si's death, Emperor Er Shi appointed Zhao Gao as the Grand Chancellor; all matters, great or small, were decided by Zhao Gao. Zhao Gao, realizing his power was growing, presented a deer and called it a horse. Emperor Er Shi asked those around him, "Is this not a deer?" Those around him all replied, "It is a horse." Emperor Er Shi was startled and began to doubt his own judgment, so he summoned the Grand Diviner and ordered him to cast a divination. The Grand Diviner said, "Your Majesty has neglected the spring and autumn sacrifices at the altar of the land and grain, failed to properly honor the ancestral temples and deities, and your fasting and purification rites were not sincere; this is why such confusion has occurred." "It would be appropriate for Your Majesty to observe a strict fast and purification in accordance with the virtue of the sage kings." Thus, he entered Shanglin Park to observe fasting and purification rites. Each day he roamed the park hunting; when a passerby entered Shanglin, Emperor Er Shi shot and killed him personally. Zhao Gao instructed his son-in-law Yan Le, the Xianyang magistrate, to report an unknown bandit who had killed a man and moved the body into Shanglin. Zhao Gao then advised Emperor Er Shi, "For an emperor to kill an innocent person without cause is forbidden by Heaven. The gods and spirits will not accept offerings, and calamity will descend from the heavens; you should move far away from the palace to avert this disaster." Emperor Er Shi then moved out to reside in Wangyi Palace.

## 35

After staying for three days, Zhao Gao issued a forged imperial decree to the palace guards, ordering them all to wear plain clothes and hold weapons facing inward. He then entered and informed Emperor Er Shi, "The rebel forces from east of the mountains are approaching in great numbers!" Emperor Er Shi looked out and saw them; he was terrified, and Zhao Gao then forced him to commit suicide. He took the imperial seal and fastened it around his waist, but none of the officials present followed suit; When he ascended the throne hall, the hall seemed to collapse three times. Zhao Gao realized that Heaven did not support him and that the officials would never accept his rule, so he summoned a younger brother of Emperor Qin Shi Huang and gave him the imperial seal.

## 36

After Ziying ascended the throne, he was troubled by Zhao Gao and pretended to be ill in order not to attend court affairs. He conspired with the eunuch Han Tan and his son to kill Zhao Gao. Zhao Gao went to court, requesting an audience while claiming illness; Ziying then summoned him in and had Han Tan stab and kill him. The entire Zhao clan was exterminated.

## 37

Three months after Ziying took the throne, Pei Gong's forces entered through Wuguan Pass and arrived at Xianyang. The officials and ministers all defected; there was no one to support him. Ziying tied a ribbon around his neck along with his wife and children, and surrendered by the roadside at Zhi. Pei Gong then handed him over to the officials for processing. Xiang Yu arrived and had him executed. Thus, the Qin dynasty lost its rule over all under heaven.

## 38

Grand Historian said: Li Si, originally a commoner from the 闾阎 (common people's quarters), traveled among the feudal states before entering service under Qin. He took advantage of opportunities and served as an advisor to Emperor Qin Shi Huang, ultimately helping him complete his imperial achievements. As Chancellor, Li Si was highly respected and entrusted with power; one might say he reached the highest position of honor and authority. Li Si understood the principles of the Six Arts, yet he did not strive to clarify governance in order to correct his ruler's shortcomings. Holding a position of great rank and wealth, he flattered and compromised for self-preservation, enforced severe punishments, listened to Zhao Gao's wicked counsel, deposed the legitimate heir, and installed an illegitimate one. When the feudal lords had already rebelled, Li Si only then wished to offer remonstrance—was this not too late? People all believe that Li Si was extremely loyal and thus suffered the five punishments before dying, but examining his actions closely reveals a different perspective from common opinion. Otherwise, Li Si's achievements would have ranked alongside those of Zhou Gong and Zhao Gong.
