# Genealogy of Prince Xiaowang of Liang

## 1

Liang Xiaowang Wu was a son of Emperor Xiaowen, and he shared the same mother as Emperor Xiaojing. His mother was Dou Taihou.

## 2

Emperor Xiaowen had four sons in total: the eldest son was the crown prince, who became Emperor Xiaojing; the second son was Wu; the third son was Can; the fourth son was Sheng. In the second year of Emperor Xiaowen's reign, Wu was made King of Dai, Can was made King of Taiyuan, and Sheng was made King of Liang. Two years later, the King of Dai was transferred to become King of Huaiyang. The entire territory of Dai was given to the King of Taiyuan, who was then called the King of Dai. Can reigned for seventeen years and died in the second year after Emperor Xiaowen's death, posthumously honored as King Xiao. His son Deng succeeded him to the throne, becoming King Gong of Dai. He reigned for twenty-nine years and died in the second year of Yuanguang. His son Yi succeeded him, becoming King of Dai. In the nineteenth year, Han expanded its borders to Guangguan, setting Changshan as a boundary and relocating King Dai's capital to Qinghe. The relocation of the King of Qinghe occurred in the third year of Yuanding.

## 3

Initially, Wu was King of Huaiyang for ten years when King Liang Sheng died and was posthumously honored as Prince Huai of Liang. Prince Huaiwang was the youngest son, favored differently from his other brothers. The following year, King Huaiyang Wu was transferred to become King of Liang. King Wu's initial enthronement as King of Liang occurred in the twelfth year of Emperor Xiaowen's reign. By this time, King Wu had already ruled over the state of Liang for eleven years.

## 4

In the fourteenth year of King Liang's reign, he paid a court visit. In the seventeenth and eighteenth years, he visited the court consecutively, staying behind; it was only in the following year that he returned to his fief. In the twenty-first year of his reign, King Liang paid a court visit again. In the twenty-second year of his reign, Emperor Xiaowen died. In the twenty-fourth year of his reign, he entered court again. In the twenty-fifth year, he once again visited the imperial court. At that time, the emperor had not yet appointed a crown prince. The emperor once dined and drank with King Liang, casually saying, "After I am gone for a thousand autumns and ten thousand years, the throne will be passed to you." King Wu declined in gratitude. Although he knew the words were not meant literally, he was secretly pleased inside. So was the empress dowager.

## 5

That spring, seven states—Wu, Chu, Qi, and Zhao—rebelled. Wu and Chu first attacked Liang's Jibi, killing tens of thousands. King Xiaowang of Liang defended the city at Suyang, appointing Han Anguo and Zhang Yu as grand generals to resist Wu and Chu. Wu and Chu took Liang as their limit, daring not to cross westward, engaging in a three-month standoff with Grand General Zhou Yafu. After Wu and Chu were defeated, the number of enemies killed, captured, or plundered by Liang was equal to that of Han's central forces. The following year, Han established a crown prince. Afterward, Liang was the most closely related to the imperial family, having rendered great service and being a major state occupying the richest lands in the empire. Its territory extended northward to Mount Tai and westward to Gaoyang, encompassing more than forty cities, many of which were large counties.

## 6

King Xiaowang was the youngest son of Empress Dowager Dou and was deeply loved by her; the rewards and gifts bestowed upon him were too numerous to recount. At that time, King Xiaowang built an eastern garden covering more than three hundred square li in area. He expanded the city of Suyang by seventy li. He greatly constructed palaces and built elevated corridors connecting from his palace to Pingtai, spanning more than thirty li. He was granted the imperial banners and flags of the Son of Heaven, with a retinue of thousands of chariots and tens of thousands of horsemen when he traveled. He hunted freely to the east and west, his manner resembling that of an emperor. When he went out, people were ordered to clear the way; when he returned, they announced a warning ahead. He recruited heroes and distinguished individuals from all directions as well as itinerant diplomats from Shandong. None failed to arrive, including Qi people such as Yang Sheng, Gongsun Gui, and Zou Yang. Gongsun Gui proposed many strange and devious strategies. Upon his first audience with the king, he was granted a thousand gold pieces and rose to the rank of Zhongwei (an official title). The state of Liang called him General Gongsun. Liang produced tens of thousands of weapons such as crossbows, bows, and spears, while its treasuries held nearly one hundred million coins, with pearls, jade, and treasures exceeding those in the imperial capital.

## 7

In the tenth month of the twenty-ninth year, King Xiaowang of Liang visited court. Emperor Xiaojing dispatched envoys bearing a ceremonial staff and four-horse chariots to meet King Liang at the pass. After paying his respects, he submitted a memorial requesting permission to remain in court due to his close ties with the empress dowager. When entering, he would accompany Emperor Xiaojing in the same palanquin; when leaving, they traveled together by carriage for hunting and shooting game in Shanglin. The attendants, palace officials, and gatekeepers of Liang who were registered in the records could enter and exit the emperor's palace gates without distinction from Han eunuchs.

## 8

In the eleventh month, Emperor Xiaojing deposed Crown Prince Li; Empress Dowager Dou secretly wished to name King Xiaowang as her successor. Ministers and Yuan Ang raised objections to Emperor Xiaojing, while Empress Dowager Dou, bound by principle, also ceased to mention the matter of naming King Liang as heir. Thus, it was not pursued further. As the affair remained secret, no one in later generations knew about it. King Xiaowang then submitted a request to return to his fief.

## 9

That summer, in the fourth month, Emperor Xiaojing established Prince Jiaodong as crown prince. King Liang resented Yuan Ang and the advisory ministers, so he conspired with Yang Sheng, Gongsungui, and others to secretly send assassins to kill Yuan Ang and more than ten other advisors. The authorities pursued the assassins but failed to capture them. At this time, Emperor Xiaojing suspected King Liang and ordered a pursuit of the assassins; indeed, they were found to have been sent by Liang. The emperor then dispatched envoys in rapid succession along the roads, investigating King Liang and capturing Gongsun Gui and Yang Sheng. Gongsun Gui and Yang Sheng had hidden in the king's inner palace. The imperial envoys urgently demanded action from the local officials. The prime minister Xuanqiubao and the Minister of Internal Affairs Han Anguo advised King Liang, who then ordered Sheng and Gui to commit suicide and turned them over. The emperor thus harbored resentment toward King Liang. King Liang, fearing the consequences, sent Han Anguo through the Crown Princess to apologize to Empress Dowager Dou; only then was he pardoned.

## 10

The emperor's anger gradually subsided, and King Liang submitted a memorial requesting another court visit. After arriving at the pass, Mao Lan advised King Liang to ride in a plain cart with only two attendants and hide within the Crown Princess's garden. Imperial envoys were sent to welcome King Liang, but by then he had already passed through the pass; his chariots and horsemen remained outside, unaware of where he was. Empress Dowager Dou wept and said, "The emperor has killed my son!" Emperor Xiaojing was anxious and fearful. Thus, King Liang prostrated himself with an axe at the palace gate to apologize; only then did Empress Dowager and Emperor Xiaojing feel relieved, weeping together and returning to their former harmonious relationship. All of King Liang's attendants were summoned into the pass. Nevertheless, Emperor Xiaojing gradually distanced himself from King Liang and no longer traveled with him in the same palanquin or carriage.

## 11

In winter of the thirty-fifth year, King Liang visited court again. He submitted a memorial requesting to remain at court, but the emperor did not grant permission. Returning to his fief, he felt restless and unhappy. While hunting northward at Liangshan, someone presented a bull with its hooves protruding from its back; King Xiaowang found this omen repulsive. In the sixth month, he fell ill with a fever and died on the sixth day, posthumously honored as "King Xiao."

## 12

King Xiaowang was filial and kind; whenever he heard of the empress dowager's illness, he could not eat or sleep well, often wishing to remain in Chang'an to attend to her. The empress dowager also loved him dearly. When she heard of King Liang's death, Empress Dowager Dou mourned bitterly and refused to eat, saying, "The emperor has indeed killed my son!" Emperor Xiaojing felt both grief and fear, not knowing what to do. After consulting with the Crown Princess, he divided Liang into five states and enfeoffed King Xiaowang's five sons as kings; his five daughters were all granted fiefs for their personal expenses. The plan was then presented to the empress dowager, who was pleased and added an extra meal for Emperor Xiaojing as a sign of approval.

## 13

King Xiaowang's eldest son Mai became King of Liang, known as King Gong; his son Ming was made King of Jichuan; his son Pengli was made King of Jidong; his son Ding was made King of Shanyang; his son Bushi was made King of Jiying.

## 14

While King Xiaowang was still alive, his wealth was counted in tens of millions and could not be fully enumerated. After his death, the remaining gold stored in his treasury still amounted to more than 400,000 jin, and other valuables were of comparable quantity.

## 15

In the third year of King Gong's reign over Liang, Emperor Xiaojing died. King Gong reigned for seven years before dying; his son Xiang succeeded him and became King Ping.

## 16

In the fourteenth year of Liang King Ping Xiang's reign, his mother was Empress Dowager Chen. King Gong's mother was Empress Dowager Li. Empress Dowager Li was the maternal grandmother of King Ping. King Ping's queen bore the surname Ren, known as Queen Wuhou. Queen Ren was greatly favored by King Xiang of Liang. Initially, while King Xiaowang was alive, there was a leizun wine vessel valued at one thousand gold pieces. King Xiaowang warned future generations to preserve the leizun carefully and not give it to anyone. Queen Ren heard about this and desired to obtain the leizun. King Ping's maternal grandmother, Empress Dowager Li, said: "The late king gave an order that the leizun must not be given to anyone. Other valuables, even if they amount to tens of millions, may still be freely disposed of." Queen Ren was determined to obtain it. King Xiang of Liang directly ordered someone to open the treasury and take the leizun, then gave it as a gift to Queen Ren. Empress Dowager Li became very angry. When Han envoys arrived, she wanted to speak with them herself, but King Xiang of Liang and Queen Ren blocked her way and locked the door. Empress Dowager Li struggled at the gate with them, tearing her fingers in the process, and thus could not meet the Han envoys. Empress Dowager Li also secretly had improper relationships with the head of the food office and officials such as Yin Ba. Because of this, King Xiang and Queen Ren sent people to discreetly warn Empress Dowager Li; thus, her immoral conduct ceased internally. Later she fell ill and died. While Empress Dowager Li was ill, Queen Ren never inquired about her condition; after her death, she did not observe mourning rites.

## 17

In the Yuan Shuo period, there was a man named Le Anfan from Suyang. A person had insulted his father and then traveled in the same carriage as a guest of the governor of Huaiyang. When the governor's guest got out of the carriage, Le Anfan killed his enemy in the carriage and then left. The governor of Huaiyang was angry and rebuked Liang's officials ranked at two thousand dan. Officials below the rank of two thousand dan urgently searched for Le Anfan and arrested his relatives. Le Anfan, knowing about secret affairs in the kingdom, submitted a report on changes in circumstances, fully informing them of the situation regarding Empress Dowager Li's dispute over the leizun with the king. At that time, chancellors and officials below them who learned of this wanted to harm senior Liang officials; their report reached the Son of Heaven. The Son of Heaven ordered officials to investigate and question, and it was confirmed that the matter was true. The ministers and high-ranking officials requested that King Xiang be deposed and reduced to a commoner. The Son of Heaven said, "Empress Dowager Li had immoral conduct, and King Xiang of Liang lacked good mentors, so he fell into injustice." Therefore, eight cities were stripped from the state of Liang, and Queen Ren's head was displayed on a pole in the marketplace. Liang still had ten remaining cities. King Xiang reigned for thirty-nine years before dying, and was posthumously honored with the title King Ping. His son Wu Shang succeeded him as King of Liang.

## 18

Jichuan Wang Ming was a son of King Xiaowang of Liang; he became King Jichuan in the sixth year of Emperor Xiaojing's Zhong period as Marquis of Huanyi. At the age of seven, he was implicated in shooting and killing his chief military officer. The Han authorities requested that he be executed; however, the Son of Heaven could not bear to execute him and instead deposed Ming as a commoner. He was exiled to Fangling, where his territory was incorporated into Han as a commandery.

## 19

Jidong Wang Pengli was a son of King Xiaowang; he became King Jidong in the sixth year of Emperor Jing's Zhong period. In the twenty-ninth year, Pengli became arrogant and unruly, lacking the manners of a ruler. At dusk, he privately went with his slaves and dozens of runaway youths to commit robberies and murders, taking valuables for pleasure. Over a hundred people killed by him were discovered; the entire state knew of this, and no one dared to travel at night. The children of those he had killed submitted petitions to report the matter. The Han authorities requested his execution, but the emperor could not bear to do so and instead deposed Pengli as a commoner, exiling him to Shangyong. His territory was incorporated into Han and became Dahan Commandery.

## 20

Shanyang Ai Wang Ding was a son of King Xiaowwang; he was made King Shanyang in the sixth year of Emperor Xing's Zhong period. He died after nine years, without any sons; his state was abolished and its territory incorporated into Han as Shanyang Commandery.

## 21

Jiyin Ai Wang Bushi was a son of King Xiaowan; he became King Jiyin in the sixth year of Emperor Xiao Jing's Zhong period. He died after one year, without any sons; his fief was abolished and the territory incorporated into Han as Jiyin Commandery.

## 22

Grand Historian said: Although King Xiaowang of Liang was granted fertile and rich land due to his close relationship with the imperial family, it was because the Han dynasty was flourishing and its people prosperous that he could accumulate wealth, expand palaces, and have carriages and clothing comparable to those of the Son of Heaven. Nevertheless, this was an act of overstepping proper bounds.

## 23

Chu Xiansheng said: When I served as a Lang, I heard it from old Lang officials in the palace who were fond of telling stories and praised it. I secretly believe that if King Xiaowang had harbored resentment or wished to do evil, the matter would have originated from within. Now Empress Dowager, a woman, because of her affection for her youngest son, wished to make King Liang the crown prince. The ministers did not promptly and clearly state why it was inappropriate, but instead flattered her with minor matters, privately expressing views to gain rewards and gifts. These were not loyal ministers. Had they spoken out clearly as Duke Dou Ying of Weiqi did in Qi, how could later calamities have occurred? Emperor Xiaojing once dined privately with the king and served Empress Dowager wine. Emperor Jing said, "After a thousand autumns and ten thousand years, it will be passed on to you, King." The empress dowager was delighted. Dou Ying, who was present before them, stood on the ground and said: "According to Han law's stipulations, the throne is passed to legitimate sons or grandsons. Now how can the emperor pass it to his younger brother? Is this not arbitrarily violating Emperor Gaozu's covenant?" At this, Emperor Jing fell silent and said nothing. The empress dowager's mood was displeased.

## 24

Therefore, King Cheng once stood with his young and weak younger brother under a tree, took a camphor leaf, gave it to him, and said: "I will use this to enfeoff you." Duke of Zhou heard about this, paid a visit, and said: "The Son of Heaven is going to enfeoff his younger brother. That's excellent." King Cheng said, "I was just playing with him." Duke of Zhou said: "A ruler must not make hasty decisions; he should never speak in jest. Whatever is spoken, one must carry out." Thus, the younger brother was enfeoffed with Ying Xian as his fief. After this, King Cheng never again dared to speak in jest for the rest of his life; whatever he said, he would carry out. The Classic of Filial Piety says: "Do not speak unless it is in accordance with law, and do not act unless it conforms to the Way." These are the words of sages. Now, it is not appropriate for the Son of Heaven to make kind remarks about King Liang. King Liang has the powerful support of his grandmother, the Empress Dowager. He had long been arrogant and haughty, often hearing Emperor Jing say kind words to him about passing on the throne after a thousand autumns, yet in reality, no action was taken.

## 25

Moreover, according to Han law, feudal lords were required to pay homage to the Son of Heaven only four times. Upon arrival, they had a minor audience; on the first day of the first lunar month, they presented deer hides, jade discs, and jade to celebrate the New Year, which was a formal audience according to law; three days later, a banquet was held for the king, and he was granted money and valuables; two days after that, they had another minor audience before departing. In total, their stay in Chang'an did not exceed twenty days. A minor audience meant a private meeting inside the palace gates and drinking within the imperial office, which common people could not enter. Now King Liang of Liang went west to pay homage but stayed behind, for nearly half a year. He entered and exited in the same palanquin or carriage as the Son of Heaven. This kind of behavior, making grandiose statements without following through in reality, will surely provoke resentment and lead to plots of rebellion. To then worry about it afterward is not too late! Unless one is a truly great sage, he does not know how to yield and step back. Now, according to the Han dynasty's rituals and regulations, when feudal lords paid homage for the New Year celebration, it was customary that one king and four marquises would attend together, occurring once every ten or so years. Now King Liang frequently enters the capital to pay homage year after year and stays for a long time. A common saying goes, "An arrogant son is an unfilial one," which is not harsh language. Therefore, feudal kings should appoint good mentors and loyal officials who speak the truth, such as Ji An or Han Zhangru, who dare to give direct and bold advice. How could there then be any dangers!

## 26

I have heard that King Liang went west to court, paid homage to Empress Dowager Dou, and had a private audience. He sat in attendance before the empress dowager together with Emperor Jing, engaging in private conversation. The empress dowager said to the emperor: "I have heard that the Yin dynasty valued kinship, while the Zhou dynasty emphasized respect for hierarchy; their principles are essentially the same. A grand procession with a palanquin should be arranged, entrusting King Xiaowang of Liang as intermediary." Emperor Jing knelt on the mat and raised his body to say: "Yes." After the banquet ended and they left, Emperor Jing summoned ministers such as Yuan Ang who were well-versed in Confucian classics and asked: "What does the empress dowager mean by saying this?" They all replied, "The empress dowager wishes to name King Liang as crown prince." The emperor asked for an explanation. Yuan Ang and others said, "The Yin dynasty's principle of valuing kinship meant that a younger brother would succeed." "The Zhou dynasty's principle of respecting hierarchy meant the throne was passed to one's son. The Yin system was simple and natural, following Heaven's law; it emphasized closeness to those who were close by blood, hence the succession of a younger brother. The Zhou system was more refined; it followed Earth's law and emphasized reverence for hierarchy, showing respect to the origin of things, hence the succession by the eldest son. In the Zhou system, if a crown prince died, his legitimate grandson would succeed him. The Yin way. If the crown prince died, they would name his younger brother as successor." The emperor asked, "What do you think of this?" They all replied: "Now the Han dynasty follows the Zhou system. Under the Zhou system, one cannot name a younger brother as heir; instead, the son must be named. That is why the Spring and Autumn Annals criticized Song Xuan Gong. Song Xuan Gong died without naming his son as heir but passed the throne to his younger brother. After that, when the younger brother who had inherited the state also died, they returned it once more to the elder brother's son. The younger brother's son contested this, claiming that he should succeed his father instead, and so he assassinated the elder brother's son. For this reason, the state fell into chaos, and calamities never ceased. Hence the Spring and Autumn Annals says: 'A gentleman should firmly uphold righteousness; the calamity in Song was caused by Duke Xuan.' I respectfully request to be granted an audience with the empress dowager to explain this." Yuan Ang and others entered to see the Empress Dowager: "Your Majesty, you said that you wished to name Prince Liang as heir. But if Prince Liang were to die, who would succeed?" The empress dowager replied, "I will then name the emperor's son." Yuan Ang and others reported to the empress dowager about how Duke Xuan of Song did not name a legitimate heir, which led to disaster; this turmoil continued for five generations without ceasing. They explained that small indulgence in personal feelings could harm great principles of righteousness. The empress dowager then understood and agreed, immediately ordering Prince Liang to return to his fiefdom. Prince Liang learned that this decision had been made by the ministers, especially Yuan Ang, and became resentful. He sent men to assassinate Yuan Ang. Yuan Ang looked at them and said, "I am the General Yuan you are looking for; are you sure you have not made a mistake?" The assassin replied, "Yes!" He attacked him. Yuan Ang caught the sword and it became stuck in his body. Yuan Ang examined the sword, which was newly forged. He asked the blacksmiths in Chang'an who had sharpened swords recently; they replied, "A certain attendant of Prince Liang came to have this sword forged." From this information, Yuan Ang discovered the plot and sent envoys to capture those involved. Alone among them were more than ten high-ranking ministers Prince Liang wished to kill; thorough investigation by the officials revealed that signs of a plot for rebellion had become evident. The empress dowager refused to eat and wept day and night without ceasing. Emperor Xiaojing was greatly distressed, so he asked the senior ministers and high officials. The ministers suggested that sending a learned official versed in Confucian classics to handle the matter would be the way to resolve it. Thus, they sent Tian Shu and Lü Jizhu to deal with the case. These two men were both well-versed in Confucian classics and knowledgeable about major rites and rituals. When they returned, upon arriving at Bachang Jiu (a place name), they took fire and completely burned all the documents that accused Prince Liang of rebellion. They came back to Emperor Xiaojing with empty hands. Emperor Xiaojing asked, "How did it go?" They replied, "Prince Liang Wang is unaware of the accusations. Those who fabricated these charges were only his favored ministers Yang Sheng, Gongsun Gui and their associates." "They have been properly executed for their crimes. Prince Liang is unharmed." Emperor Xiaojing was pleased and said, "Hurry to pay respects to the Empress Dowager." The empress dowager heard this and immediately sat up from her meal; her anger subsided. Therefore it is said: one who does not understand the Confucian classics and the great rites of ancient and modern times cannot serve as a Three Dukes or as an official close to the emperor. Those with narrow-mindedness are like people peering at the sky through a bamboo tube.
