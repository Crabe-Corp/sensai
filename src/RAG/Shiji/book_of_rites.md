# Book of Rites

## 1

The Grand Historian said: What a great virtue! Controlling all things, commanding the masses—could this be merely human effort? I went to the Grand Rites Office and observed the changes in rites of the Three Dynasties, and thus realized that rites were established according to human feelings and ceremonies were created based on human nature; their origin is very ancient.

## 2

Human affairs are complex and multifaceted, yet rules and standards permeate all aspects. People are guided forward by benevolence and righteousness, and restrained by punishments; thus those with great virtue hold high positions, and those with heavy emoluments receive honor and favor. This is how the empire is unified and its people made orderly. The human body, when mounted in a carriage, is adorned with golden carriages and elaborately decorated yokes to enhance its appearance; The eyes favor the five colors, so embroidered patterns are created to display one's ability; The ears delight in bells and stone chimes, so harmonious music of the eight tones is arranged to stir their hearts; The mouth enjoys the five flavors, so various delicacies with sour and salty tastes are prepared to achieve its pleasure; Feelings favor precious things, so jade is carved into ritual objects to convey their meaning. Therefore, the great ceremonial carriage is placed on simple mats, the leather cap is worn with plain robes, zithers are played in a deep and resonant manner, and plain broth is served with dark wine—these are done to prevent extravagance and to remedy decadence. Therefore, the order of ranks and statuses between rulers and ministers in court—upward to superiors and downward to common people—extends even to distinctions regarding carriages, clothing, palaces, food, marriage, funerals, and ancestral sacrifices. Each matter has its appropriate measure, and each object follows a proper standard. Confucius said: "Since the beginning of the 禘 ritual when the libation was poured, I have no desire to witness it anymore."

## 3

When the Zhou dynasty declined, rites and music fell into disrepair; distinctions between high and low were violated. Even the household of Guan Zhong included all three types of ritual returns. Those who follow laws and uphold righteousness are ridiculed by society, while those who indulge in excess and overstep their status are called illustrious and honored. Zi Xia, a high-ranking disciple of the school, still said: "When going out and seeing splendid sights I am pleased; when entering to hear Master's teachings I feel joy. The two feelings conflict within me, and I have not yet resolved this struggle." How much more so for those of average or lower ability, gradually influenced by poor education and conforming to established customs? Confucius said, "It must be the rectification of names," yet his residence in Wei did not align with this principle. After Confucius's death, his disciples who had received instruction became obscure and were not promoted; some went to Qi or Chu, others disappeared into the rivers and seas—was this not sorrowful!

## 4

When the Qin dynasty gained control of the empire, it incorporated all the rites and ceremonies of the six states, selecting their good aspects. Although these did not conform to the sacred system, they emphasized respect for the ruler and restraint on ministers, maintaining a dignified court as had been done since ancient times. By the time of Emperor Gaozu, who ruled over all under heaven, Shusun Tong made some additions and deletions. Nevertheless, for the most part, they followed the Qin's established practices. From the imperial title down to assistants, officials, and palace names, there were few changes made. When Emperor Xiaowen ascended the throne, officials proposed to establish formal rites and ceremonies. However, Emperor Xiaowen favored Daoist learning; he considered elaborate rituals as mere external displays that were of no benefit for governance, let alone personal moral transformation, so he abolished them. During the reign of Emperor Xiaojing, the Grand Censor Chao Cuo was well-versed in worldly affairs and legal doctrines. He repeatedly advised Emperor Xiaojing: "The feudal lords as vassal supports should be treated on par with ministers and sons; this is an ancient and modern system." "Now the great states administer their own special policies, not reporting to the capital. I fear this cannot be passed on to future generations." Emperor Xiaojing adopted his plan, but the six states rebelled. Because Chao Cuo's name was first mentioned in this matter, the emperor executed him to resolve the crisis. The affair is recorded within Yuan Ang's words. After this, officials merely maintained their relationships and secured their salaries; no one dared to discuss it again.

## 5

When the current emperor ascended the throne, he summoned Confucian scholars and ordered them to jointly establish rites; it took more than ten years without completion. Some said that in ancient times, when the world was at peace and all people were harmonious and joyful, auspicious signs appeared abundantly. Only then would they observe customs and establish rites and institutions. The emperor heard this and issued an edict to the Grand Censor: "When a ruler receives his mandate, each dynasty rises through its own path; though their ways differ, they all lead to the same goal. This means rites are created according to the people's needs and institutions follow established customs." Those who argue always praise the remote ancient past; what hope do common people have? The Han dynasty is also a single family's affair. If its institutions and laws are not passed down, how can they be transmitted to descendants? Those with lofty moral influence will have broad vision; those with shallow governance will have narrow-mindedness. Should we not strive!" Thus, in the first year of Taichu, he reformed the calendar and changed the colors of official robes; he performed the Fengshan ceremony on Mount Tai and established rites for ancestral temples and all government officials. These were set as permanent standards to be passed down to future generations.

## 6

Rites originate from human beings. Human life is accompanied by desires; when these desires are unfulfilled, people cannot help but feel resentment. If this resentment lacks restraint and measure, it leads to contention, and contention brings disorder. The sage kings of old abhorred such disorder, so they established rites and righteousness to nurture human desires and satisfy people's needs. They ensured that desire would not be exhausted by material things, nor would material things be overwhelmed by desire; the two coexist in balance and grow together—this is where rites originate. Therefore, rites are about nurturing. Rice, millet, and the five flavors nourish the mouth; Spices, orchids, and fragrant herbs nourish the nose; Bells, drums, flutes, and zithers nourish the ears; Carved patterns and embroidered designs nourish the eyes; Spacious rooms, beds, mats, and low tables nourish the body: thus rites are about nurturing.

## 7

A gentleman, having received this nurture, also values its distinctions. What is meant by "distinction" is that there are ranks between the noble and humble, differences in age between elders and juniors, and all matters of poverty or wealth, lightness or weight have their proper measures. Therefore, the Son of Heaven's great ceremonial carriage rests on simple mats—this is to nurture his body; Fragrant herbs are placed on the side—this is to nourish the nose; In front, there are elaborately decorated yokes—this is to nourish one's eyes; The harmonious sounds of the bells and chimes, their steps matching the rhythm of Wu Xiang, and sudden changes in accordance with Shao Hu—this is to nourish one's ears; The dragon-standard with nine tassels is for cultivating trust; Sleeping on a rhinoceros skin, holding a tiger-shaped pillow, and using 鲛 leather with dragon patterns—these are for cultivating authority. Therefore, the horses of the great ceremonial carriage must be well-trained and obedient before they can be ridden—this is for cultivating peace and stability. Who knows that a scholar's willingness to die for righteousness and integrity is the true way of nurturing life? Who knows that frugality and careful use are the way to nurture wealth? Who knows that respect, courteous speech, and yielding are the means of cultivating peace? Who understands that rites, righteousness, and proper order are how emotions are nurtured.

## 8

If a person merely seeks to live for appearances' sake, such a one is bound to die; If one pursues profit alone for show, such a person will inevitably suffer harm; To regard idleness as security, such a one is destined to face danger; To take emotional indulgence as peace, such a person will surely perish. Therefore, the sage unifies all in rites and righteousness—thus achieving both benefits; To unify everything in emotions and nature leads to losing both. Therefore, Confucians seek to make people gain both; Mohists seek to cause people to lose both. This is the distinction between Confucianism and Mohism.

## 9

The highest achievement of governance, the foundation for strength and stability, the way to project authority, and the summary of merit and fame. Kings and dukes followed this path, thereby unifying all under heaven and subjugating feudal lords; Failure to follow it leads to the loss of state and ancestral temple. Therefore, strong armor and sharp weapons are insufficient for victory; high walls and deep moats are not enough to ensure security; strict commands and numerous punishments do not suffice to establish authority. Following this principle ensures success, while neglecting it leads to failure. The people of Chu used 鲛 leather and rhinoceros hides for armor, as hard as gold and stone; The iron from Wan was so sharp it pierced like a scorpion's sting, light yet swift and agile, moving as fast as a sudden gale. Nevertheless, their troops were in peril during the crossing of Chusha, and Tang Mei died there; Zhuang Qiao rose up, dividing Chu into four rival factions. Was it because they lacked strong armor and sharp weapons? It was because the way they governed did not follow the right principles. They regarded the Ru and Ying rivers as barriers, the Yangtze and Hanshui Rivers as moats, used Denglin to block their way, and relied on Fangcheng for defense. Nevertheless, when Qin forces reached Yanyang, they captured it as easily as shaking dead leaves from a tree. Was this because they lacked strong fortifications and natural barriers? It was because the way in which they governed did not follow proper principles. Zhou dismembered Bi Gan, imprisoned Ji Zi, built the cauldron of punishment, and executed the innocent. At that time, his ministers were terrified, uncertain whether they would survive. Nevertheless, when Zhou forces arrived, his commands could not be carried out by the people below; he was unable to mobilize them. Was this because his orders were not strict or punishments severe? It was because the way he governed did not follow proper principles.

## 10

In ancient times, weapons were merely halberds, spears, bows, and arrows; yet enemy states would surrender without even a battle. They did not build city walls, dig moats, erect fortifications, or prepare for sudden changes; yet the state remained peaceful and unafraid of external threats. There was no other reason—by clarifying principles and distributing resources fairly, by timely employment and genuine care, the people responded as a shadow follows its form or an echo follows sound. Only those who disobey orders are then punished; thus the people understand what constitutes crime. Therefore, punishing one person brings the whole world to submission. The guilty do not blame their superiors; they know that the crime lies within themselves. Thus, punishments are few yet authority flows like a river—there is no other reason but that it follows the right way. Therefore, following this principle ensures success; neglecting it leads to downfall. In ancient times, when Emperor Yao governed the world, he executed one person and punished two others, yet the whole realm was at peace. The Records say: "Authority is so formidable that it need not be tested; punishments are prepared but never used."

## 11

Heaven and Earth are the fundamental source of life; Ancestors are the root of lineage; Rulers and teachers are the foundation of governance. Without Heaven and Earth, how could life exist? Without ancestors, from where would we come? Without rulers and teachers, how could governance be achieved? If any one of these three is missing, people cannot find peace. Therefore, rites involve honoring Heaven above and Earth below, respecting ancestors and elevating rulers and teachers—these are the three fundamental principles of rites.

## 12

Therefore, a king is the great ancestor under Heaven; feudal lords dare not harbor private loyalties; officials and scholars have fixed ancestral lineages—this is how ranks of nobility and humility are distinguished. When distinctions between noble and humble are properly maintained, the root of order is achieved.

## 13

The Son of Heaven performs sacrifices at the outer altar to Heaven; feudal lords perform rites at their local earth gods; officials and scholars participate in lesser ceremonies—this is how those of higher rank serve according to their status, and those of lower rank act accordingly. Matters are treated as great or small depending on what is appropriate.

## 14

Therefore, those who rule the realm serve rites for seven generations; those who govern a state serve rites for five generations; those with land sufficient for five chariots serve rites for three generations; those with land for three chariots serve rites for two generations; and those who eat only a single sacrificial animal may not establish ancestral temples. This is how the breadth of influence flows from accumulated virtue—those with deep accumulations have wide-reaching benefits, while those with shallow accumulations have narrow ones.

## 15

The grand sacrifice begins with the most sacred wine, raw fish is placed on the food tray first, and thick broth precedes other dishes—this honors the fundamental nature of food and drink.

## 16

In the grand sacrifice, the most sacred wine is offered first but diluted wine is used; millet and grain are served before rice and refined flour; thick broth is presented before other delicacies—this shows respect for fundamentals while also being close to practical use. Respecting the fundamental is called "wen" (culture), and being close to practical use is called "li" (reason). When these two are combined, they form culture that returns to Taiyi—the ultimate unity. This is known as great prosperity.

## 17

Therefore, honoring the most sacred wine above all, placing raw fish first on the food tray, and presenting thick broth before other dishes in the bean vessels—these are one principle.

## 18

The sacrificial cups are not tasted, the completed offerings on the food trays are not sampled, and the three invitations to eat do not result in consumption.

## 19

In a grand wedding before purification rites are completed, in the great ancestral temple before the symbolic corpse is placed inside, and at the beginning of mourning when small funerary wrappings have not yet been prepared—these are all one principle.

## 20

The plain canopy of the grand chariot, the hemp headdress for sacrifices at the suburbs, and the initial use of loose hemp in mourning attire—these are one principle.

## 21

Mourning for three years without ceasing, the song in the ancestral temple is sung once and followed by three sighs; a single bell is hung but accompanied by hand clapping on the frame; red strings are used with open resonators—these are one principle.

## 22

All rites begin with simplicity, become refined through culture, and conclude in completion. Therefore, when rites are most complete, both sincerity and form reach their full expression; Next, sincerity and form alternate in dominance; The lowest level is to return sincerity to the ultimate unity of Taiyi.

## 23

Heaven and Earth unite, the sun and moon shine clearly, the four seasons follow their order, stars move in their courses, rivers flow, all things flourish, likes and dislikes are regulated, joy and anger are expressed appropriately. If followed by those below, it brings harmony; if practiced by those above, it brings clarity.

## 24

The Grand Historian said: How ultimate this is! Establishing the highest rites as the standard, and no one in the world can add to or subtract from them. Roots and branches follow each other in order; beginnings and endings correspond with one another. The most refined culture has its distinctions, and the most thorough understanding has its explanations. When all under Heaven follow it, there is order; when they do not follow it, chaos ensues; Those who follow it find peace; those who do not follow it face danger. Common people are unable to emulate this.

## 25

The outward form of rites is indeed profound; the sharp distinctions between white and different, when entered into, become weak. Its grandeur is truly great; those who arbitrarily create narrow and crude doctrines, upon entering, look up in awe. Its majesty is truly lofty; those who are arrogant, reckless, and disdainful of common customs, believing themselves superior, upon entering will fall. Therefore, if the plumb line is truly set in place, one cannot be deceived about straightness or crookedness; If the balance beam is truly suspended, one cannot be deceived regarding weight; If compasses and squares are properly placed, one cannot be misled about squareness or roundness; When a gentleman examines rites carefully, he cannot be deceived by fraud or falsehood. Therefore, the plumb line represents perfect straightness; the balance beam represents perfect levelness; compasses and squares represent the utmost in squareness and roundness; rites are the ultimate principle of human conduct. However, those who do not abide by rites lack true propriety and are called people without standards; Those who follow and fulfill rites are called men of principle. Within rites, the ability to reflect and deliberate is called "being able to consider"; The ability to deliberate without being swayed by ease or convenience is called "steadfastness." To possess both the ability to consider and steadfastness, and to add a genuine love for them—this is sainthood. Heaven is the ultimate height; Earth is the ultimate depth; The sun and moon are the utmost in brightness; The infinite is the ultimate vastness; A sage is the ultimate embodiment of the Dao.

## 26

Rites use material goods as their means, distinguish noble and humble through formality, differentiate according to quantity, and emphasize the key aspects of grandeur and simplicity. When outward forms are elaborate but inner desires are restrained, this is the height of rites; When outward forms are simple but inner desires abound, this is the reduction in rites; When outward form and inner desire complement each other as surface and depth, proceeding together yet mingling—this represents the moderate level of rites. A gentleman elevates to the highest grandeur above, reduces to the simplest below, and resides in the middle as a balance. Steps, strides, galloping, and wide-ranging movement do not go beyond boundaries—thus the nature of a gentleman remains within the palace courtyard. This is the domain for human beings—the realm of virtuous scholars and gentlemen. Beyond this, one is merely an ordinary person. Thus, within the center, where all directions are fully encompassed and curved [or straight] elements follow their proper order—this is a sage. Therefore, depth comes from the accumulation of rites; greatness is the breadth of rites; height is the grandeur of rites; clarity is the full realization of rites.
