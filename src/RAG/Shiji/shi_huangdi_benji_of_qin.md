# Shi Huangdi Benji of Qin

## 1

Qin Shi Huangdi was a son of Qin Zhuangxiangwang. Zhuangxiangwang had been a hostage in Zhao, where he saw the concubine of Lu Buwei and was pleased with her; he took her as his wife and she gave birth to Shi Huang. He was born in Handan in the first month of the forty-eighth year of King Zhaoxiang of Qin's reign. After his birth, he was named Zheng and belonged to the Zhao clan. At thirteen years old, Zhuangxiangwang died, and Zheng succeeded him as King of Qin. At that time, the territory of Qin had already incorporated Ba, Shu, and Hanzhong, passed through Wan to reach Ying, and established Nanjun. To the north, it had collected Shangjun and areas east of it, including Hedong, Taiyuan, and Shangdang Jun. To the east, it reached Yingyang, where the two Zhou states were destroyed and Sanchuan Jun was established. Lu Buwei served as prime minister, was granted ten thousand households, and was given the title Wenxin Hou. He recruited guests and traveling scholars in an attempt to unify all under heaven. Li Si was a retainer. Meng Ao, Wang Yi, and Biao Gong were generals. The king was young when he first ascended the throne and entrusted state affairs to high-ranking ministers.

## 2

A rebellion broke out in Jinyang; in the first year, General Meng Ao suppressed it. In the second year, Biao Gong led troops to attack Juan and beheaded thirty thousand rebels. In the third year, Meng Ao attacked the state of Han and captured thirteen cities. Wang Yi died. In the tenth month, General Meng Ao attacked Chang and Yougui of the Wei state. There was a great famine that year. In the fourth year, Chang and Yougui were captured. In March, the military campaign was concluded. The Qin hostage returned from Zhao, and the Zhao crown prince departed back to his country. On the Gengyin day of the tenth month (October 23, 247 BC), locusts came from the east and darkened the sky. A plague spread throughout the land. Common people who contributed one thousand dan of grain to the state were granted a rank in the nobility system. In the fifth year, General Ao attacked Wei and captured Suanzao, Yan, Xu, Changping, Yongqiu, and Shanyang Cheng; all were taken, totaling twenty cities. The Dongjun was first established. Thunder occurred in winter. In the sixth year, Han, Wei, Zhao, Wei, and Chu jointly attacked Qin and captured Shouling. Qin sent out troops; the armies of the five states withdrew. Wei was captured, and Dongjun came under pressure; its ruler Jiao led his collateral relatives to move and settle in Yewang, using the mountains as a barrier to protect Henei of Wei. In the seventh year, a comet first appeared in the east, then was seen in the north; it was visible in the west in May. General Ao died. They attacked Long, Gu, and Qingdu, then turned their troops to attack Ji. The comet was again seen in the west for sixteen days. Summer Empress Dowager died. In the eighth year, King Wang's younger brother Chang'an Jun Chengjiao, as a general, attacked Zhao but rebelled. He died in Tunliu; all military officers were executed, and their people were relocated to Lintao. General Bi died; the soldiers stationed in Tunliu and Puhe rebelled, and his corpse was dismembered as punishment. Large numbers of river fish surfaced; light chariots and heavily laden horses moved eastward in search of food.

## 3

Lao Ai was granted the title Changxin Hou. He was given territory in Shanyang and ordered to reside there. Palaces, carriages, horses, clothing, parks, hunting grounds were all freely provided for Lao Ai's use. No matter how small or large the affairs were, they were decided by Lao Ai. The Jun of Hexi and Taiyuan was also changed to become a fief for Lao Ai. In the ninth year, a comet appeared, stretching across the entire sky in some places. They attacked Yuan and Puyang of Wei. In April, the king stayed overnight in Yong. On the Jiyou day (April 18, 246 BC), the king was crowned and wore a sword. Changxin Hou Lao Ai instigated a rebellion and was discovered. He forged the king's imperial seal and the Empress Dowager's seal to mobilize county troops, palace guards, official cavalry, Rong 翟 jun Gong, and retainers, intending to attack Qiniangong in an uprising. The king learned of this and ordered Chancellor Changping Jun and Changwen Jun to mobilize troops to attack Lao Ai. The battle took place in Xianyang, where hundreds were beheaded; all who participated were granted noble ranks. Eunuchs involved in the fighting also received a rank each. Lao Ai and his followers were defeated and fled. The king immediately issued an order to the country: anyone who captured Lao Ai alive would be granted 100,000 coins; those who killed him would receive 50,000. Lao Ai and all his followers were eventually captured. Weiyi Jie, Neishi Si, Zuoyi Jie, Zhongdasha Lingqi and twenty others were all decapitated and their heads displayed publicly. Lao Ai was subjected to the punishment of being torn apart by chariots as a public warning, and his clan was exterminated. As for his retainers, those who were less severely punished became "guixin," meaning they had to gather firewood for ancestral sacrifices as punishment labor. More than four thousand families were stripped of their noble ranks and exiled to Shu, where they settled in Jialiang. (4) [This] month was extremely cold; some people died from the freezing weather. Yang Duanhe attacked Yanshi. A comet appeared in the west, then again in the north, visible from Dou to the south for eighty days. In the tenth year, Chancellor Lu Buwei was dismissed due to his association with Lao Ai. Huan Yi became a general. Qi and Zhao came to offer banquets. A man from Qi named Mao Jiao advised King Qin, saying: "Qin is now preoccupied with unifying the world, yet Your Majesty has acquired a reputation for moving your mother, the Empress Dowager. I fear that if the feudal lords hear of this, they may turn against Qin." King Qin then welcomed the Empress Dowanger from Yong back to Xianyang and had her reside once again in Ganhuan Palace.

## 4

A large-scale search was conducted, and guests were expelled; however, Li Si submitted a memorial to advise against this, and the order expelling guests was thus halted. Li Si then advised King Qin that they should first attack Han to intimidate other states, and so the king sent Li Si to Han. The King of Han was worried about this. He plotted with Han Feizi to weaken Qin. A man from Daliang named Weiliao came and advised King Qin, saying: "Given the strength of Qin, the feudal lords are like magistrates in commanderies and counties. I am only afraid that they will form a coalition and launch an unexpected attack; this is precisely why Zhibo, Fuchai, and King Min lost their states." "I hope Your Majesty will not be stingy with wealth, but bribe their powerful ministers to disrupt their plans. For no more than thirty thousand gold pieces, all the feudal lords can be subdued." King Qin followed his strategy and received Weiliao with great courtesy, sharing clothes, food, and drink with him. Weiliao said: "King Qin is a man with a sharp nose, long eyes, the chest of a bird of prey, and the voice of a jackal. He has little mercy and a tiger-like or wolf-like heart. When in hardship, he easily submits to others; when successful, he readily devours people." "I am merely a commoner, yet whenever I see him, he personally treats me with humility." "If King Qin achieves his ambitions over all under heaven, the whole world will become his captives." "Therefore, I cannot stay in close association with him for long." He then left and went into hiding. King Qin realized this, insisted on keeping him, appointed him as a military officer of the state of Qin, and ultimately employed his strategies. Meanwhile, Li Si took charge of affairs.

## 5

In the eleventh year, Wang Jian, Huan Yi, and Yang Duanhe attacked Ye and captured nine cities. Wang Jian attacked Eyu and Laoyang, which were all incorporated into a single army. Wang Jian led the campaign for eighteen days; troops below the rank of doushi returned, and two out of every ten were selected to follow the army in capturing Ye and Anyang, under the command of Huan Yi. In the twelfth year, Wenxin Hou Lu Buwei died and was secretly buried. Those retainers of his who attended the funeral were from Jin; they were expelled. All Qin officials above the rank of six hundred dan had their noble ranks stripped and were exiled; those below five hundred dan who did not attend the funeral were also exiled, but without having their noble titles revoked. From now on, those who govern state affairs in an unrighteous manner like Lao Ai and Lu Buwei will have their families registered for punishment, as is the case here. In autumn, those retainers of Lao Ai who had been exiled to Shu were returned. At that time, there was a severe drought throughout the land; rain finally fell from June until August.

## 6

In the thirteenth year, Huan Yi attacked Pingyang of Zhao, killed Zhao's general Hu Zhe, and beheaded ten thousand. The king went to Henan. In the first month, a comet appeared in the east. In October, Huan Yi attacked Zhao. In the fourteenth year, they fought Zhao's army at Pingyang, captured Yian, defeated them, and killed their general. Huan Yi pacified Pingyang and Wucheng. łoż Han Feizi was sent to Qin, but Han Fei was detained by Qin using Li Si's plan and died in Yunyang. The King of Han requested to become a subject of Qin.

## 7

In the fifteenth year, a large army was mobilized; one force went to Ye and another to Taiyuan, capturing Langmeng. An earthquake occurred. In the ninth month of the sixteenth year, conscripts were dispatched to receive the territory of Nanyang in Han from Jian Shou Teng. For the first time, an order was issued requiring men to record their ages in writing. Wei submitted territory to Qin. Qin established the Li Yi commandery. In the seventeenth year, Neishi Teng attacked Han, captured King An of Han, and incorporated all its territory. He established this area as a commandery named Yingchuan. An earthquake occurred. The Huayang Taihou passed away. There was a great famine among the people.

## 8

In the eighteenth year, a large army was raised to attack Zhao. Wang Jian led troops from Shangdi and captured Jingxing; Duan He led forces from Hedong, while Qiang Kui attacked Zhao, with Duan He surrounding Handan city. In the nineteenth year, Wang Jian and Qiang Kui completely pacified and captured Dongyang of Zhao's territory, capturing the King of Zhao. They led their troops to attack Yan but stationed in Zhongshan. The King of Qin went to Handan, and all those who had grudges with the king's maternal family from when he was born in Zhao were buried alive. The King of Qin returned, passing through Taiyuan and Shangjun Commandery back to his capital. The mother of Emperor Qin Shi Huang, the Taihou, passed away. Zhao Gongzi Jia led hundreds of his clan members to Dai, declared himself King of Dai, and joined forces with Yan in the east, stationing troops at Shanggu. There was a great famine.

## 9

In the twentieth year, Crown Prince Dan of Yan feared that Qin troops would reach his country and became terrified; he sent Jing Ke to assassinate the King of Qin. The King of Qin discovered this, dismembered Jing Ke and displayed his body publicly, then dispatched Wang Jian and Xin Sheng to attack Yan. Yan and Dai mobilized troops to strike the Qin army; the Qin forces defeated them west of Yishui in Yan. In the twenty-first year, Wang Ben attacked (Ji)[Jing]. More conscripts were then dispatched to join General Wang Jian's forces, and they eventually defeated the army of Crown Prince Dan of Yan, captured the city of Ji in Yan, and obtained the head of Crown Prince Dan. The King of Yan fled eastward to collect control over Liaodong and declared himself king there. Wang Jian resigned from his post due to illness and returned in old age. Xinzheng rebelled. Changping Jun was relocated to Ying. Heavy snow fell, reaching a depth of two chi and five cun.

## 10

In the twenty-second year, Wang Ben attacked Wei, diverted river channels to flood Daliang; the city walls of Daliang collapsed. Its king requested surrender, and all its territory was captured.

## 11

In the twenty-third year, King of Qin again summoned Wang Jian, strongly urging him to return, and ordered him to lead troops against Jing. They captured Chen and the area south to Pingyu, taking King Jing captive. The King of Qin toured as far as Yingchen. Jing's general Xiang Yan declared Changping Jun the King of Jing, and rebelled against Qin in Huainan. In the twenty-fourth year, Wang Jian and Meng Wu attacked Jing, defeated their army; Changping Jun died, and Xiang Yan then committed suicide.

## 12

In the twenty-fifth year, a large army was mustered; Wang Ben was appointed as general and ordered to attack Liaodong of Yan, capturing King Xi of Yan. He then returned to attack Dai and captured its king Jia. Wang Jian thus pacified the southern regions of Jing's territory across the Yangtze River; He subdued the ruler of Yue and established Kuaiji Commandery. In May, a grand public feast was held throughout the empire.

## 13

In the twenty-sixth year, King Jian of Qi and his prime minister Hou Sheng mobilized troops to defend their western border, refusing to communicate with Qin. Qin dispatched General Wang Ben to attack Qi from the south of Yan and captured King Jian of Qi.

## 14

When the King of Qin first unified the empire, he issued an order to the prime ministers and imperial censors: "In the past, the King of Han submitted his territory and seal, requesting to be a vassal subject. Yet later he violated the agreement, forming an alliance with Zhao and Wei against Qin; therefore, we raised arms to punish him and captured their king. I thought this was good, hoping it might bring peace to warfare. The King of Zhao sent his prime minister Li Mu to negotiate an alliance, so I returned their hostage prince. Yet later they violated the alliance and rebelled in Taiyuan of my territory; therefore, we raised troops to punish them and captured their king. Zhao Gongzi Jia declared himself King of Dai on his own initiative, so I launched a campaign to attack and annihilate him. The King of Wei initially agreed to submit and entered Qin, but later conspired with Han and Zhao to attack Qin; our troops and officials punished them and eventually defeated them. The King of Jing offered the area west of Qingyang, but later violated the agreement and attacked our Nanjun Commandery; therefore, I dispatched troops to punish them, captured their king, and thus pacified their territory in Jing. The King of Yan was corrupt and disorderly; his crown prince Dan secretly ordered Jing Ke to commit an assassination. Our troops and officials punished the attackers, and destroyed their state. The King of Qi followed the plan of Hou Sheng, cut off Qin envoys, and intended to rebel; our troops and officials suppressed them, captured their king, and pacified the territory of Qi. With my humble self, I raised troops to punish violence and disorder; relying on the divine protection of our ancestral temple, all six kings have been made to bear their guilt, and now the empire is at peace. Now that titles remain unchanged, there will be no way to honor my achievements or pass them on to future generations. Let us deliberate on the imperial title." The prime minister Wan, the imperial censor Jie, and the Minister of Justice Si all said: "In former times, the Five Emperors ruled a thousand li in each direction; beyond that were marquisates and barbarian territories where feudal lords sometimes paid homage or not, and even the Son of Heaven could not control them. Now, Your Majesty has raised righteous troops to punish the cruel and wicked, pacified the empire, established commanderies and counties throughout the realm, and unified laws under a single system. Since ancient times, there has never been such an achievement; even the Five Emperors could not match it. We, your humble subjects, have carefully discussed with the scholars and say: "In ancient times there was Tianhuang, Dihuang, and Taihuang; among them, Taihuang is the most exalted." We dare to respectfully present a title of honor for Your Majesty: "Taihuang". Commands shall be called 'Zhi', orders shall be called 'Zhao,' and the Son of Heaven shall refer to himself as 'Zhen'." The king said: "Drop the word 'Tai,' and use 'Huang.' Adopt the ancient title of 'Di,' and call it 'Huangdi.'" Other matters shall follow your deliberations." The decree said: "Approved." Posthumously honored King Zhuangxiangwang as Taishang Huang. The decree said: "I have heard that in the most ancient times, rulers had titles but no posthumous names; in the middle ages, they had titles and after death were given posthumous epithets based on their conduct. If so, then sons would judge fathers, and ministers would judge sovereigns; this is meaningless, and I do not accept it. From now on, the system of posthumous names shall be abolished. I am the First Emperor. Posterity shall count by generations: Second Emperor, Third Emperor, up to the Ten Thousandth Emperor, passing it on endlessly."

## 15

The First Emperor traced the cycle of the Five Elements from beginning to end and believed that Zhou had acquired the Fire virtue, while Qin succeeded the Zhou virtue by following what it could not overcome. Now is the beginning of the Water virtue; the calendar year shall be changed, and court congratulations will begin on the first day of October. Clothing, banners, flags, and insignia should all be black. Numbers shall use six as the standard; talismans and official hats should all measure six cun, carriages six chi in length, a step measured by six chi, and six horses for each carriage. The river was renamed De Shui, considered the beginning of the virtue of water. He was firm and resolute, decisive in all matters according to law, strict without mercy or benevolence, only then aligning with the numbers of the Five Virtues. Thus, laws were enforced strictly, and long-term offenders were not pardoned.

## 16

The prime minister Wan and others said: "Now that the feudal lords have just been defeated, Yan, Qi, and Jing are far away; if we do not appoint kings there, we will have no one to garrison them. We request that Your Majesty establish your sons as kings in these regions; may it please the Son of Heaven to grant permission." The First Emperor submitted this proposal for discussion among his ministers, and all the officials considered it advantageous. The Minister of Justice Li Si argued: "The descendants and relatives of King Wen and King Wu of Zhou were numerous, but as their relations became distant, they attacked each other like enemies. Feudal lords successively waged wars against one another, yet the Son of Heaven of Zhou could not stop them. Now, under Your Majesty's divine guidance, the empire has been unified and is divided into commanderies and counties. The sons of the emperor and meritorious officials may be generously rewarded with public taxes; this would be more than sufficient for control. If there is no dissent in the empire, it will be a strategy to ensure peace and stability. Therefore, establishing feudal lords would not be beneficial." The First Emperor said: "The people of the empire have all suffered from ceaseless warfare because there were feudal kings and lords. Thanks to the divine protection of our ancestors, the empire is now at peace; if we were again to establish states, it would be like planting the seeds for war. To seek tranquility under such circumstances would surely be difficult! The Minister of Justice's argument was correct."

## 17

Divide the empire into thirty-six commanderies, each with a governor, military commander, and inspector. The people were renamed "Qianshou." A grand public feast was held. All weapons from across the empire were collected and brought to Xianyang, melted down to cast bells and other instruments; twelve bronze statues of men were made, each weighing a thousand dan, placed in the palace courtyards. Unify standards for measurements such as weights, stones, length, and volume. Standardize the width of cart axles to ensure uniformity in roads. Unify written characters into a standardized script. The territory extended east to the sea and Chaoxian, west tolintao and Qiangzhong, south to Beixianghu, and north along the Yellow River as a frontier defense, connecting Yinhshan all the way to Liaodong. A total of 120,000 households of wealthy and influential families from across the empire were relocated to Xianyang. All ancestral temples as well as Zhangtai and Shanglin Park were located south of the Wei River. Whenever the Qin defeated a feudal state, they would have replicas of its palaces built on the northern slope of Xianyang, facing south toward the Wei River. From Yongmen to the east all the way to Jing and Wei rivers, palace halls, covered walkways, and surrounding pavilions were connected in succession. Beautiful women and musical instruments captured from the defeated feudal states were brought there to fill them.

## 18

In the twenty-seventh year, the First Emperor toured Longxi and Beidi, passed through Jitou Mountain, and went past Huizhong. He built the Xingong Palace in the southern Wei River area; later it was renamed Jitemple, symbolizing the celestial pole. From Jitemple a road was built to Lishan Mountain, where the Qian Hall of Ganhquan was constructed. A covered passage was built connecting Xianyang to it. In this year, a first-rank noble title was bestowed as a grant. Construct express roads.

## 19

In the twenty-eighth year, the First Emperor traveled east through commanderies and districts, ascending Zouyi Mountain. Stone tablets were erected; discussions took place with Confucian scholars from Lu, and stone inscriptions were carved to extol the virtue of Qin. Discussions also occurred regarding the ceremonies for sacrifices at Mount Tai and offerings to mountains and rivers. He then ascended Mount Tai, erected stone tablets, performed the ritual of feng (capping), and offered sacrifices at a shrine. On his way down, heavy rain and wind suddenly arrived; he took shelter under a tree and thus bestowed the title of Wudaifu upon it. He performed the chán ceremony at Liangfu Mountain. The stone tablets that were erected were inscribed with the following words:

The Emperor ascended the throne, established regulations and clarified laws; his ministers and subordinates maintained discipline. In the twenty-sixth year, having first unified the empire, all regions submitted without exception. He personally toured distant lands and common people, ascended this Mount Tai, and surveyed the easternmost reaches. ## 21

At that time, he proceeded eastward across Bohai, passed through Huang and Zhui, reached the end of Chengshan Mountain, ascended Zhaifu, erected stone inscriptions to extol Qin's virtue, and then departed.

He relocated 30,000 Qianshou households to beneath Langya Terrace, exempting them from taxes and labor duties for twelve years. He built the Langya Terrace and erected stone inscriptions to praise Qin's virtue, clearly proclaiming his achievements and triumphs. The inscription read:

## 24

When the King of Qin unified the empire and took the title Emperor, he traveled eastward to pacify the land as far as Langya. The Marquis of Wucheng Hou Wang Li, the Marquis of Tongwu Hou Wang Ben, Lunhou Jiancheng Hou Zhao Hai, Lunhou Changwu Hou Cheng, Lunhou Wuxin Hou Feng Wuze, Chancellor Kuilin, Chancellor Wang Wan, Minister Li Si, Minister Wang Wu, Dadafu Zhao Ying, and Dadafu Yang Jiu all accompanied him and participated in discussions by the sea. They said: "In ancient times, emperors ruled no more than a thousand li of land; feudal lords each guarded their own fiefs, some paying homage and others not. They invaded one another, causing endless chaos and destruction. Yet they still carved inscriptions on gold and stone to record their achievements for posterity. The Five Emperors and Three Kings of ancient times had different teachings, unclear laws and regulations. They falsely claimed divine authority to deceive distant regions; their actual achievements did not match their titles, so they could not endure for long. Before their deaths, feudal lords had already rebelled against them and the laws were no longer enforced. Now the Emperor has unified all under heaven into commanderies and districts; peace prevails throughout the empire. He clearly honored ancestral temples, embodied the Way through virtue, and his title of honor was fully realized. The ministers together recited the Emperor's virtues and achievements, carving them on gold and stone as a lasting record."

## 25

After this, Qi native Xu Shi and others submitted a memorial stating that there were three divine mountains in the sea named Penglai, Fangzhang, and Yingzhou, where immortals resided. They requested to be allowed to purify themselves with rituals and take along young boys and girls in search of them. Thus, the Emperor sent Xu Shi, who mobilized thousands of young boys and girls to go into the sea in search of immortals.

## 26

After returning, the First Emperor passed through Pengcheng, observed rituals and made offerings at shrines in hopes of retrieving the Zhou Dynasty's ritual tripod from Si River. He sent a thousand men to dive into the water in search of it, but they failed to find it. Thus he crossed the Huai River southwestward and went to Hengshan and Nanjun. He traveled by boat along the Yangtze River, arriving at Xiangshan Temple. They encountered a violent storm and nearly failed to cross. The Emperor asked the scholars, "Is Xiangjun a deity?" The scholars replied: "We have heard that she was Yao's daughter and Shun's wife, who was buried here." Thus the First Emperor became greatly enraged and ordered 3,000 convicts to cut down all trees on Xiangshan Mountain, leaving it bare. The Emperor himself returned from Nanjun via Wuguan Pass.

## 31

Afterward, he proceeded to Langya via Shangdang.

## 32

In the thirtieth year (July 7, 218 BC), there were no major events.

## 33

On the twelfth month of the thirty-first year (January 30, 217 BC), the name of the winter solstice festival was changed to "Jiaping." The Emperor bestowed six shi of rice and two sheep upon each village's common people. The First Emperor made a secret visit to Xianyang, traveling with four warriors. At night he went out and encountered bandits at Lanchi, where they were cornered. The warriors killed the bandits, prompting a twenty-day large-scale search throughout Guanzhong. The price of rice was 1,600 coins per shi.

## 34

In the thirty-second year (July 23, 215 BC), the First Emperor went to Jieshi and sent Yan native Lu Sheng in search of Xianmen and Gaoshi. He had an inscription carved at the gate of Jieshi Mountain. They destroyed city walls and breached dikes to clear flood defenses. The inscription read:

## 35

He raised armies, punished the lawless, and extinguished rebellion. With military might he eliminated violent rebels; with virtue he restored innocence, and all hearts submitted in awe. He showed benevolence by rewarding merits, bestowing rewards even on oxen and horses, spreading grace across the land. The Emperor displayed his might; virtue united all feudal lords, bringing initial peace to the realm. He destroyed city walls, breached river defenses, and leveled dangerous obstacles. With the terrain now stable, the common people had no burdens; peace was extended throughout the world. Men found joy in their fields, women pursued their occupations, and all affairs were well-ordered. Benevolence extended to all produce; lands from distant regions were united under one rule, with none failing to find peace in their place. The ministers praised his great achievements and requested that they be carved on this stone as a lasting model for conduct.

## 36

He then sent Han Zhong, Hou Gong, Shi Sheng to seek immortals and the elixir of eternal life. The First Emperor toured the northern frontier, entering from Shangjun Commandery. A Yan native named Lu Sheng, after being sent to the sea and returning with matters concerning ghosts and gods, submitted a report on recorded books and maps, stating: "The one who will destroy Qin is Hu." Upon hearing this, the First Emperor ordered General Meng Tian to mobilize 300,000 troops to attack Hu in the north and seize the area south of the Yellow River.

## 37

In the thirty-third year (July 14, 214 BC), convicts who had previously fled from service, in-laws, and merchants were mobilized to seize the Lu Liang region, establishing Guilin, Xiang Jun, and Nanhai Commanderies for garrison deployment. To the northwest, they drove out the Xiongnu. From Yuzhong along the Yellow River eastward to Yinshan Mountain, they established fourteen counties and built fortifications along the river as a defensive barrier. They also sent Meng Tian across the Yellow River to capture Banque, Yangshan, and Beijiazhong, building watchtowers and barriers to drive out the Rong people. Convicts were relocated and settled in these newly established counties. Worship was prohibited. A bright star appeared in the west. In the thirty-fourth year (July 5, 213 BC), convicts and corrupt officials involved in unjust legal cases were deployed to build the Great Wall and fortify areas south of the Yangtze.

## 38

The First Emperor held a banquet at Xianyang Palace, where seventy scholars presented longevity offerings before him. The Minister of Works Zhou Qingchen offered praise, saying: "In the past, Qin's territory was no more than a thousand li. Thanks to Your Majesty's divine wisdom and sacred virtue, you have pacified the realm, driven out the southern and northern barbarians, and under whose rule even where the sun and moon shine, none fail to submit in awe." You have replaced feudal lords with commanderies and counties; all people find peace and happiness, free from the troubles of war, to be passed on for ten thousand generations. The might and virtue of Your Majesty surpass even those of ancient times." The First Emperor was pleased. A scholar from Qi named Chunyu Yue then spoke up, saying: "I have heard that the kings of Yin and Zhou ruled for over a thousand years by enfeoffing their sons, brothers, and meritorious ministers as branches to support the central authority. Now, although Your Majesty rules over all under heaven, your sons and brothers are commoners. Should there suddenly arise ministers like Tian Chang or the Six Ministers of Jin without any support or opposition, how could one possibly rescue the situation?" I have never heard of a case where affairs not following ancient precedents can endure for long. Now Qingchen is flattery in person, further adding to Your Majesty's faults; he is no loyal minister." The First Emperor referred the matter for discussion. Chancellor Li Si said: "The Five Emperors did not repeat each other's ways, and the Three Dynasties did not follow one another. Each governed according to their own methods; this was not because they were opposed, but due to changing times. Now that Your Majesty has initiated great undertakings and established achievements for ten thousand generations, it is naturally beyond the understanding of ignorant Confucian scholars. Moreover, what Chunyu Yue said refers to matters from the Three Dynasties; how could such things be worth following? In former times, when feudal lords competed for power, they lavishly recruited traveling scholars. Now that the realm is unified and laws emanate from one source, commoners should focus on farming and craftsmanship, while scholars must study laws and prohibitions. Nowadays, many scholars do not follow the present but instead learn from antiquity to criticize current affairs, confusing and misleading the common people. Minister Li Si respectfully stated: "In ancient times, the realm was fragmented and no one could unify it, so feudal lords arose in competition. They all spoke of antiquity to harm the present, adorned false words to confuse reality, favored their own private schools of thought, and criticized what Your Majesty has established." Now that His Majesty unifies all under heaven, distinctions between right and wrong are made clear, and one supreme authority is established. Private schools, however, teach doctrines contrary to the law. When people hear imperial decrees issued, they each use their own teachings to criticize them; privately they harbor doubts and publicly discuss in alleyways, boasting of loyalty for reputation, seeking novelty as a mark of superiority, and inciting subordinates to spread slander. If such behavior is not prohibited, the authority of the sovereign will weaken above, while factions will form below. It would be beneficial to prohibit this. I request that all historical records not written by the state of Qin be burned. Unless they are under the charge of official scholars, anyone in all the realm who dares to possess copies of poetry, historical texts, or writings from the Hundred Schools must deliver them for burning by magistrates and military officers. Anyone daring to discuss poetry or history in pairs shall be executed publicly. Those who criticize the present by citing antiquity shall have their entire clans exterminated. Officials who know of such acts but fail to report them will share the same punishment. If within thirty days after this decree is issued, books are not burned, those responsible shall be branded and conscripted for labor at dawn in city construction. The only books that will not be burned are those on medicine, divination, and agriculture. If anyone wishes to study laws and decrees, let the officials serve as their teachers." The imperial edict stated: "Approved."

## 39

In the thirty-fifth year, a road was constructed from Jiuyuan to Yunyang. Mountains were cut through and valleys filled in order to create a direct passage. At this time, Emperor Qin Shi Huang considered the population of Xianyang to be large and the palaces of previous kings too small. He had heard that King Wen of Zhou established his capital at Feng, and King Wu of Zhou at Hao; he believed the area between Feng and Hao was a suitable site for an imperial capital. Therefore, he began construction of the court palace within the Shanglin Garden to the south of the Wei River. First, they built the Qian Dian Afang Hall, which was 500 bu wide from east to west and fifty zhang long from north to south. It could accommodate ten thousand people on top and hold a five-zhang flag at its base. Circumferential roads were built as elevated corridors, extending directly from beneath the hall to Mount Nanshan. The peaks of Mount Nanshan were marked as gate towers. An elevated corridor was constructed, crossing the Wei River from Afang and connecting to Xianyang, symbolizing the celestial axis' elevated path that spans across the Milky Way to reach the Yingshi constellation. The Afang Palace was not yet completed; once it is finished, the emperor wished to choose a more auspicious name for it. Since construction of the palace began at Afang, people throughout the empire referred to it as the Afang Palace. More than 700,000 convicts and laborers were mobilized; some worked on the Afang Palace while others labored at Lishan. Stone slabs from Beishan Mountain were transported, and materials from Shu and Jing regions were also brought in for construction. In Guanzhong there were a total of three hundred palaces; outside the pass, over four hundred more. Thus, stone pillars were erected in Qushui at the eastern sea to mark it as Qin's eastern gate. As a result, thirty thousand households were relocated to Liyi and fifty thousand to Yunyang; all of them were exempt from taxes for ten years.

## 40

Lu Sheng advised Emperor Qin Shi Huang, saying: "We have often failed to find the rare herbs and elixirs of immortals we seek; it seems that harmful forces are obstructing us. "In alchemical texts, it is said that when a sovereign occasionally travels incognito to ward off evil spirits, the evil spirits will be driven away and true immortals will arrive. "If the sovereign's residence is known by his ministers, it harms the divine." "True immortals can enter water without getting wet and walk through fire unburned; they ride on clouds, living in harmony with heaven and earth for eternity." "Now that Your Majesty rules the realm, you have not yet achieved tranquility and peace. "I hope that no one knows where Your Majesty resides; only then may the elixir of immortality be attainable." Thus, Emperor Qin Shi Huang said: "I admire true immortals. From now on I shall call myself 'True Person,' and no longer use the title 'Zhen.'" He then ordered that within two hundred li around Xianyang, 270 palaces and pavilions be connected by elevated corridors and covered passageways. Each was filled with curtains, bells, drums, and beautiful women; they were assigned to their posts permanently without relocation. Anyone who revealed the location where the emperor had stayed would be put to death. Emperor Qin Shi Huang once visited Liangshan Palace, and from atop the mountain he saw a large retinue accompanying the prime minister's carriage; this did not please him. Some palace attendants informed the prime minister, and thereafter the prime minister reduced his retinue. Emperor Qin Shi Huang became angry and said: "One of these palace attendants has revealed my words." After investigation, no one confessed. At that time, the emperor issued an order to arrest all those present at the scene and had them executed. From then on, no one knew where the emperor would travel or reside. Official business was conducted and all court officials received their decisions at Xianyang Palace.

## 41

Hou Sheng and Lu Sheng conspired together, saying: "Emperor Qin Shi Huang is by nature rigid, harsh, and self-willed. He rose from among the feudal lords to unify all under heaven; now he feels triumphant and indulges his desires, believing no one since ancient times can rival him. "He entrusts authority solely to prison officials, who thus gain his favor and intimacy. "Although there are seventy official scholars, they serve only as placeholders and are not actually used." "The prime minister and other high ministers merely accept decisions already made; all matters depend on the emperor's judgment. "He takes pleasure in using punishment and executions to establish authority, so people throughout the realm fear crime but cling to their positions; none dare offer complete loyalty." "The emperor hears no criticism and grows increasingly arrogant, while ministers below cower in fear, flattering and deceiving to gain favor. "Under Qin law, if alchemists fail to produce verified results from their combined methods, they are immediately executed." "Yet there were as many as three hundred astrologers and diviners, all of whom were capable men; they feared reprisals and flattered the emperor out of caution, never daring to openly criticize his faults." "All affairs in the realm, great or small, are decided by the emperor. He personally weighed official documents with a balance and stones to measure their volume; day and night he received reports, and if they did not meet his expectations, he could not rest." "His greed for power is so extreme. For such a man, we cannot seek elixirs of immortality." Thus, they fled and left. When Emperor Qin Shi Huang heard of their escape, he became furious and said: "Previously I had collected all books in the realm that were not useful and burned them. "I summoned a great number of scholars, literary men, and alchemists in hopes of achieving peace and prosperity; these alchemists wished to refine elixirs to find miraculous medicines. "Now I have heard that Han Zhong fled without reporting, Xu Shi and others spent tens of thousands in resources yet still failed to obtain the elixir; instead, daily reports reveal deceitful pursuits for profit. "Lu Sheng and others were highly honored with generous rewards by me, yet now they slander me to worsen my reputation for virtue." "Scholars in Xianyang—I have sent men to investigate, and some are found spreading false words that confuse the common people." Thus, he ordered the imperial censors to thoroughly investigate all scholars. The scholars passed accusations among themselves, and in the end, 460 or more were found guilty of violating laws; they were all buried alive in Xianyang so that all under heaven would know, as a warning for those who followed. More convicts were sent to the frontier regions as exiles. Qin Shi Huang's eldest son, Fu Su, advised: "The realm has just been pacified; the common people in distant regions have not yet settled. These scholars all recite Confucian teachings. Now Your Majesty punishes them severely according to law. I fear this will cause unrest throughout the land. "I beg Your Majesty to consider this carefully." Emperor Qin Shi Huang was furious and sent Fu Su northward to supervise Meng Tian in Shangjun Commandery.

## 42

In the thirty-sixth year, Mars remained stationary over the constellation Shen (Heart). A falling star fell in Dongjun Commandery, and upon hitting the ground became a stone. Some common people carved on it: "When Emperor Qin Shi Huang dies, the land will be divided." Emperor Qin Shi Huang heard about this and sent imperial censors to investigate. No one confessed, so he executed all the residents living near the stone and burned it to destroy evidence. Emperor Qin Shi Huang grew unhappy, so he ordered the official scholars to compose poems praising immortal sages. As he traveled throughout the realm, he had musicians accompany him by singing and playing these songs. In autumn, an imperial envoy traveling at night from east of the Guan Mountains along the Huayin Pingshu Road was stopped by a man holding a jade disc. The man said: "Give this to Haochi Jun for me." He then added, saying: "This year the ancestor dragon will die." The envoy asked him why this was so, but suddenly the man vanished and left only the jade disc behind. The envoy presented the jade disc to the emperor and reported everything in detail. Emperor Qin Shi Huang remained silent for a long time, then said: "Mountain spirits can only know what will happen within one year." Later he remarked: "The ancestor dragon refers to the progenitor of mankind." When the imperial treasury examined the jade disc, they discovered it was the same one that had been submerged in a river during the twenty-eighth year. Thus, Emperor Qin Shi Huang consulted diviners and received an auspicious omen for traveling and relocating. He relocated 30,000 households to Yuzhong in the Behe region. They were each granted a rank increase of one level.

## 43

On the Gouchou day of the tenth month in the thirty-seventh year, Emperor Qin Shi Huang embarked on an imperial tour. The Left Chancellor Li Si accompanied him, while the Right Chancellor Qu Ji remained to govern in his place. His youngest son, Hu Hai, expressed deep affection and requested to accompany the emperor; this was granted by His Majesty. In November, he traveled as far as Yunmeng, where he performed a distant sacrifice to Yu Shun at Jiuyi Mountain. He sailed down the Yangtze River, visited Jike, and crossed Haizhu Island. He passed through Danyang and arrived at Qiantang. Approaching the Zhejiang River, he found the waters turbulent; thus, he turned westward 120 li and crossed through a narrow passage. He ascended Kuaiji Mountain to worship Dayu, gazed toward the South China Sea, and erected stone inscriptions praising Qin's virtue. The inscription read:

## 44

The emperor's great achievements bring peace and unity to the realm; his virtue and grace are enduring. In the thirty-seventh year, he personally toured the empire, surveying distant lands. He then ascended Kuaiji Mountain, proclaimed his decrees to understand local customs, and the common people observed solemn rites in reverence. Ministers praised His Majesty's achievements, traced back the origins of his deeds, and honored the lofty wisdom of his ancestors. The holy Qin dynasty governs the realm; laws and punishments were first established, clearly displaying ancient regulations. Initially establishing legal standards, carefully distinguishing official duties to establish enduring order. The six kings were arrogant and defiant; greedy and cruel, they led their people in self-aggrandizement. They acted with cruelty and arrogance, relying on strength to grow proud, frequently waging war. Secretly dispatching envoys, they formed alliances against Qin, acting in defiance of the law. Internally, they plotted deceit; externally, they invaded borders, thus bringing disaster upon themselves. Righteous might punished them, extinguishing violence and madness; rebels were destroyed. The holy virtue is vast and profound; within the six directions of the world, its grace extends boundlessly. The emperor unifies the realm, hears all matters, and both near and far are fully enlightened. He governs all things with wisdom, examines facts, and records each one by name. High and low are equally known; good or bad is laid bare before him, no secrets hidden. Those who adorn themselves with false virtue, having a son yet remarrying, betray death and remain unfaithful. Preventing internal and external corruption, prohibiting licentiousness, ensuring men and women maintain purity and sincerity. A man who is a mere pig for hire, killing him incurs no guilt; males uphold the code of righteousness. Wives who elope and remarry, their children lose their mothers—this all transforms into integrity and purity. Great governance purifies customs; the empire follows its example, embracing this blessed doctrine. All abide by laws and standards, harmonious and peaceful, diligently obedient to commands. The common people cultivate virtue; all rejoice in shared principles, rejoicing in the preservation of peace and prosperity. Posterity reveres and upholds the law; perpetual governance without end, the imperial vessel remains upright. Ministers recite His Majesty's great deeds, requesting these words be inscribed on stone, to shine forth as a lasting monument.

## 45

Returning past Wu, he crossed the river from Jiangcheng. He traveled along the eastern coast and went north to Langya. The alchemist Xu Shi and others went to sea in search of elixirs, but after several years they found nothing. Their expenses were great, and fearing punishment, they fabricated a story: "The elixir at Penglai can be obtained, yet we are often hindered by large sharks, which is why we have not reached it. We wish to request skilled archers to accompany us; when the sharks appear, we will shoot them with repeating crossbows." Qin Shi Huang dreamed of fighting a sea deity that appeared human-like. When he inquired about the dream, scholars interpreted it by saying: "Water deities cannot be seen directly; their presence is indicated by large fish and dragons." "Now, although the emperor has offered prayers with utmost reverence, this malevolent deity appears. It should be driven away so that benevolent deities may come." He then ordered those going to sea to carry equipment for capturing large fish, and personally waited with a repeating crossbow to shoot any big fish that appeared. From Langya northward to Rongcheng Mountain, none were seen. When they reached Zhifu, a large fish was sighted and one was shot and killed. He then proceeded westward along the coast.

## 46

When he arrived at Pingyuan Jin, he fell ill. Qin Shi Huang detested the mention of death, and none among his ministers dared to speak about it. As His Majesty's illness worsened, he composed a sealed letter with his personal seal and bestowed it upon Gongzi Fusu, saying: "Join the funeral procession in Xianyang for burial." The letter had already been sealed and was kept at Zhongchufu Ling Zhao Gao, who handled the emperor's seal and insignia; it had not yet been given to an envoy. On the Bingyin day of the seventh month (August 11, 210 BC), Qin Shi Huang died at the Pingtai in Shachou. The prime minister Li Si, fearing that the emperor's death abroad might cause unrest among the imperial princes or the people, kept it secret and did not announce his passing. The coffin was placed inside a cool carriage; thus, the favored eunuch served as an attendant driver and continued to present food at each stop. Officials submitted reports as usual, while the eunuch would immediately relay their matters from within the cool carriage for approval. Only the emperor's son Hu Hai, Zhao Gao, and five or six favored eunuchs knew of his death. Zhao Gao had previously taught Hu Hai writing as well as legal codes and procedures, and Hu Hai privately favored him. Zhao Gao then conspired with Gongzi Hu Hai and the prime minister Li Si to destroy the sealed letter from Qin Shi Huang that had been given to Gongzi Fusu. Instead, they forged a document falsely claiming it was an imperial decree left by Qin Shi Huang at Shachou, appointing Hu Hai as crown prince. They also forged another letter to be given to Gongzi Fusu and Meng Tian, listing numerous accusations against them, ordering their deaths. The details are recorded in the biography of Li Si. They proceeded, traveling from Jingxing to Jiuyuan. lando As the summer heat set in and the smell from the sealed carriage became unbearable, an imperial decree was issued to the attendants ordering them to load a cart with one dan of preserved fish to mask the odor.

## 47

They traveled along the straight road to Xianyang and then announced the emperor's passing. The crown prince Hu Hai succeeded to the throne, becoming Emperor Er Shi. In September, Qin Shi Huang was buried at Lishan Mountain. When Qin Shi Huang first ascended the throne, he began construction of his tomb at Lishan. After unifying the empire, over 700,000 people were conscripted from across the land to transport materials and labor there. They dug down through three layers of springs, poured molten copper into the underground structure to secure the outer coffin, and filled it with palaces, halls, rare artifacts, and treasures moved from all over the empire. They ordered craftsmen to install mechanical crossbows and arrows; anyone who approached too closely would be shot immediately. Mercury was used to represent the hundred rivers, great rivers, and vast seas; mechanisms were installed to circulate it. The ceiling depicted celestial phenomena, while the floor illustrated geography. They used the fat of man-fish as candle wax, which was said to burn for a long time without extinguishing. Emperor Er Shi said: "Those in the late emperor's harem who had no children should not be allowed to leave." All were ordered to commit suicide, and many perished. After the burial was completed, some claimed that craftsmen had set up mechanisms; all secrets of the tomb were known to them. If they revealed these secrets, the value of the treasures would be greatly diminished. Once all major tasks were completed and the treasures placed, the middle chamber was sealed, then the outer gate was closed. All craftsmen and those who had stored items inside were locked in—none allowed to escape. Trees and vegetation were planted around the tomb to resemble a mountain.

## 48

The first year of Emperor Er Shi's reign, when he was twenty-one years old. Zhao Gao served as Langzhongling and held the power to make decisions. Emperor Er Shi issued an edict, increasing sacrifices for Qin Shi Huang's ancestral temple and the hundred rites honoring mountains and rivers. He ordered his ministers to deliberate on how best to honor the temple of Qin Shi Huang with greater reverence. The ministers all kowtowed and said: "In ancient times, emperors had seven ancestral temples, feudal lords five, and high officials three. Even after countless generations, none would be removed or destroyed. "Now that Qin Shi Huang has been honored with the highest temple, all within the four seas contribute tribute and fulfill their duties. Sacrifices have increased, and all rites are fully prepared—there is nothing more to add." The ancestral temples of previous kings were either in Xiyong or in Xianyang. According to imperial protocol, the emperor should personally offer wine and perform sacrifices exclusively at Qin Shi Huang's temple. Temples of kings from Duke Xiang Gong onward were removed or destroyed. In total, seven ancestral temples were established. The ministers performed rites in accordance with protocol to honor the temple, elevating Qin Shi Huang's as the imperial ancestor temple for future emperors. The emperor once again referred to himself as "Zhen."

## 49

Emperor Er Shi and Zhao Gao plotted together, saying: "I am young and newly enthroned; the common people have not yet gathered in loyalty. "The late emperor toured the commanderies and counties to display his strength, subduing all within the seas through awe." "Now that I remain idle without touring, it appears weak; how can I command the loyalty of the people?" In spring, Emperor Er Shi traveled eastward through the commanderies and counties, with Li Si accompanying him. He traveled to Jieshi, followed the coast, and went south as far as Kuaiji. At each location where Qin Shi Huang had previously inscribed stone tablets, he added new carvings with the names of ministers who accompanied him beside the stones, in order to highlight the late emperor's great achievements and virtuous deeds:

## 50

The emperor said: "All stone inscriptions are works of Emperor Qin Shi Huang. Now that I have inherited the title, if these stone inscriptions do not mention Emperor Qin Shi Huang by name, in the distant future they may be mistaken for works of later generations and fail to reflect his great achievements and virtue." The prime minister, Minister Li Si and Minister Qu Ji, along with the Grand Censor-in-Chief, Minister De, respectfully submitted: "We request that Your Majesty's decree be fully inscribed on stone tablets; this will make everything clear. We humbly implore Your Majesty to grant our request." The emperor decreed: "Approved."

## 51

They then proceeded to Liaodong and returned.

## 52

Thus, Emperor Er Shi followed Zhao Gao's advice and reinforced the laws and decrees. He secretly plotted with Zhao Gao, saying: "The high ministers are not obedient; the officials remain powerful. Moreover, the imperial princes will surely contend against me—how can we deal with this?" Zhao Gao replied: "I have long wished to speak but dared not. "The late emperor's ministers are all renowned and noble men of generations past, whose accumulated achievements have been passed down for a long time. "Now I am lowly by nature; it is only through Your Majesty's favor that I have been elevated to a high position and entrusted with important affairs. "The ministers are proud and arrogant, merely outwardly obeying me while in their hearts they remain discontented." "Now that the emperor is on tour, we should not miss this opportunity to investigate and execute corrupt commandery and county officials. This will demonstrate imperial authority to all under heaven and eliminate those who have long been a thorn in Your Majesty's side." "Now is not the time to rely on words but to decide matters through force. I hope Your Majesty will follow this course without hesitation, and then no minister will dare to plot against you." "A wise ruler gathers the people, elevates the lowly to nobility, enriches the poor, and brings distant ones near—thus unity is achieved between high and low, and the nation finds peace." Emperor Er Shi said: "Well spoken." He then carried out the executions of high ministers and imperial princes, citing their crimes to arrest junior officials and three palace attendants who had been close to him. No one was allowed to remain in office; six imperial princes were executed at Du. Gongzi Jianglv and his two brothers were imprisoned within the palace, their punishment deliberated last of all. Emperor Er Shi sent an envoy to order Jianglv: "You have committed treason, and your crime deserves death. Officials will now carry out the law." Jianglv replied: "I have never failed to follow the proper court etiquette in the imperial palace; In the ancestral temple, I have never neglected my duties or compromised propriety; When receiving commands and responding to them, I have never failed in my words." What is meant by 'treason'? "I would be content to hear the charge against me and then die." The envoy said: "I am not involved in deliberations, but must carry out my orders as written." Jianglv then looked up to the heavens and cried out three times: "Heaven! Heaven!" I am innocent!" Jianglv's two brothers, hearing this, all wept and drew their swords to commit suicide. The imperial clan was greatly frightened. Ministers who dared to advise were accused of slander, high officials clung to their positions for self-preservation, and the common people trembled in fear.

## 53

In the fourth month, Emperor Er Shi returned to Xianyang and said: "The previous emperor found the Xianyang court too small, so he began construction of Afang Palace as a grand hall. It was not yet completed when the emperor died; work was halted and the laborers were sent back to Lishan Mountain for burial preparations. "The funerary works at Lishan have been fully completed; if we now abandon the construction of Afang Palace, it would appear that the late emperor's projects were excessive." Construction of Afang Palace was resumed. Externally, he pacified the four barbarian groups as had been planned by Emperor Qin Shi Huang. He conscripted fifty thousand elite soldiers to serve as garrisons in Xianyang, ordering them to train in archery and the hunting of dogs, horses, and wild animals. With so many people to feed, the supply was insufficient. The imperial court ordered commanderies and counties to transport beans, grains, straw, and fodder, requiring everyone to bring their own provisions. Within three hundred li of Xianyang, no one could eat locally grown grain. The application of the law became even harsher.

## 54

In the seventh month, conscripted soldiers such as Chen Sheng rebelled in the former Jing region and declared themselves "Changchu." Chen Sheng proclaimed himself King of Chu, based at Chen, and dispatched his generals to seize territory. The young men of the Shandong commanderies and counties, suffering under Qin officials, all killed their local commanders, magistrates, and assistants in rebellion to support Chen She. They declared themselves marquises or kings, forming a coalition marching westward, claiming to attack the Qin dynasty—too numerous to count. An imperial envoy returning from the east reported the rebels to Emperor Er Shi. Emperor Er Shi was furious and had him handed over to the authorities for punishment. Later envoys arrived, and when asked by the emperor, they replied: "These are merely bandits; local commanders and magistrates are already pursuing them. They have all been captured now—there is no need to worry." The emperor was pleased. Wu Chen declared himself King of Zhao, Wei Jiu became King of Wei, and Tian Dan became King of Qi. Pang Gong rose in Pei. Xiang Liang raised an army in Kuaiji Commandery.

## 55

In the winter of the second year, Zhou Zhang, sent by Chen She, led his troops westward to Xi, with an army numbering hundreds of thousands. Emperor Er Shi was greatly alarmed and discussed with his ministers: "What should we do?" Shao Fu Zhang Han said: "The rebels have already arrived, and their numbers are great. It is too late to raise troops from the nearby counties now." "There are many convicts at Lishan; I request that they be pardoned and armed to fight against them." Emperor Er Shi then issued a general pardon throughout the empire, appointing Zhang Han as commander. He defeated Zhou Zhang's forces and drove them back, killing General Zhang Caoyang in the process. Emperor Er Shi further dispatched the chief clerk Sima Xin and Dong Yi to assist Zhang Han in fighting the rebels. They killed Chen Sheng at Chengfu, defeated Xiang Liang at Dingtao, and destroyed Wei Jiu's forces at Linji. With the famous rebel generals of Chu now dead, Zhang Han then crossed the Yellow River to attack King Xie of Zhao and others at Julu.

## 56

Zhao Gao advised Emperor Er Shi: "The late emperor ruled the empire for a long time, so officials dared not act improperly or spread false doctrines. Now that Your Majesty is young and newly enthroned, why should you decide matters in court with the ministers?" If any mistake occurs, it will reveal your weakness to all the officials. "As an emperor, you should be called 'Zhen'; it is natural that no one hears your voice." Thus Emperor Er Shi often remained within the palace and made all decisions together with Zhao Gao. After this, ministers rarely had the opportunity to present themselves at court; bandits multiplied further, and imperial troops from Guanzhong continued to be dispatched eastward without pause. The Right Chancellor Qu Ji, the Left Chancellor Li Si, and General Feng Jie advised: "Rebels have risen together in Guandong. The Qin dynasty has sent troops to suppress them, killing many, yet they still do not cease." There are so many rebels because the people suffer from harsh conscription and heavy corvée labor, as well as high taxes. We request that construction of Afang Palace be halted for now and that the garrisons and transport duties on the borders be reduced." Emperor Er Shi replied: "I have heard from Master Han that 'Yao and Shun used unplaned wooden beams, uncropped straw for their roofs, ate from earthen bowls, and drank from rough clay cups. Even the food of a gatekeeper would not be worse than this.' "Yu the Great excavated Longmen, connected Daxia, dredged the He Ting River, and diverted its waters to the sea. He personally carried tools for digging, his legs so worn that no hair grew there—yet the labor of a slave could not be harder than this.'" The reason why those who rule the empire are honored is that they may indulge their desires without restraint. ## 58

Emperor Er Shi then observed a purification ritual at Wangyi Palace, intending to offer sacrifices to the Jing River by casting four white horses into it. He sent envoys to reprimand Zhao Gao for his failure in dealing with the rebels. Zhao Gao was terrified and secretly conspired with his son-in-law Yan Le, the commandant of Xianyang, and his brother Zhao Cheng: "The emperor will not listen to advice. ## 59

Yan Le returned and reported to Zhao Gao, who then summoned all the ministers and imperial sons, informing them of Emperor Er Shi's execution. He said: "Qin was originally a kingdom; the First Emperor ruled over all under heaven, hence he assumed the title of emperor. Now that the six states have once again declared themselves independent and Qin's territory has become even smaller, it is inappropriate to continue using an empty title of emperor. It would be better to revert to being a king as before." They then installed Gongzi Ying, the nephew of Emperor Er Shi, as King of Qin. Emperor Er Shi was buried in the Yichun Garden to the south of Du with the rites for a commoner. They ordered Gongzi Ying to observe a period of purification, and he was to appear at the ancestral temple and receive the king's seal. After five days of purification, Gongzi Ying and his two sons plotted: "The chancellor Zhao Gao killed Emperor Er Shi at Wangyi Palace. Fearing that the ministers would execute him, he pretended to place me on the throne out of righteousness. I have heard that Zhao Gao has made an agreement with Chu to exterminate the Qin imperial family and rule Guanzhong as a king. Now he is making me observe purification rites at the ancestral temple; this must be an attempt to kill me there." "I will claim illness and refuse to go. The chancellor will surely come himself, and when he does, we shall kill him." Zhao Gao sent several envoys to summon Gongzi Ying, but he refused to go. Zhao Gao then went in person and said: "The ancestral temple ceremony is a matter of great importance; how can you refuse to attend?" Gongzi Ying then assassinated Zhao Gao in the purification palace and publicly executed three generations of his family in Xianyang as a warning. Gongzi Ying had been King of Qin for only forty-six days when the Chu general Pei Gong defeated the Qin forces and entered Wuguan Pass, advancing to Baling. He then sent envoys to offer terms for Gongzi Ying's surrender. Gongzi Ying tied a ribbon around his neck, rode in a white horse and plain chariot, carried the imperial seal and symbols of authority, and surrendered by the roadside at Zhi. Pei Gong then entered Xianyang, sealed off the palaces, government offices, and treasuries, and withdrew his troops back to Baling. After more than a month, the armies of the feudal lords arrived. Xiang Ji, as supreme commander, killed Gongzi Ying and all the remaining members of the Qin imperial family. He then sacked Xianyang, burned its palaces, took captive the men and women of Qin, seized its treasures and wealth, which were divided among the feudal lords. After the fall of Qin, the territory was divided into three parts and given to three rulers: King Yong, King Sai, and King Zhai. These were collectively known as Sanqin (Three Qins). Xiang Yu became the Hegemon-King of Western Chu and assumed authority to divide the empire among the feudal lords, and thus Qin was finally extinguished. Five years later, the empire was unified under Han.

During the transition between Yin and Xia dynasties, they gradually declined and became obscure. By the time of Zhou's decline, Qin rose to prominence and established its fief in Xichui (the western frontier). Since Duke Mu, they gradually devoured the feudal states like silkworms eating mulberry leaves, eventually becoming the First Emperor. The First Emperor considered his achievements to surpass those of the Five Emperors and his territory to be broader than that of the Three Kings, and thus he was ashamed to rank equal with them. How well Jia Sheng put it in his analysis! He said:

## 61

"Qin annexed more than thirty commanderies in the region east of Mount Hangu, repaired river crossings and passes, occupied strategic fortresses, maintained military equipment, and defended them. Yet Chen She, with only a few hundred scattered conscripts and disorderly troops, raised his arm and cried out in defiance. He did not use bows or halberds but wielded hoes and sticks, eating from house to house as he marched across the land at will. The people of Qin failed to defend their natural barriers, did not close the passes and bridges, did not raise their long halberds for attack, nor did they fire their powerful crossbows. Had Gongzi Ying possessed even mediocre leadership and merely had competent advisors, Qin's territory could have been preserved intact despite the chaos in the east, and the ancestral sacrifices would not have come to an end.

## 62

The land of Qin was protected by mountains on one side and rivers on another, making it a naturally fortified country surrounded by four barriers. From Duke Mu to King Qin, over twenty rulers, they were consistently the dominant power among the feudal states. Were all of them truly wise generation after generation? It was simply the result of their advantageous position. Moreover, once all under heaven had united in purpose and strength to attack Qin. At that time, wise and capable men stood side by side; able generals led their armies, and virtuous ministers coordinated strategies. Yet they were hindered by Qin's natural barriers and could not advance. Qin then invited them to battle and opened its gates, causing hundreds of thousands of attackers to flee in defeat and ultimately collapse. Was it because their courage, strength, or wisdom were lacking? No—it was simply that the terrain and strategic situation worked against them. Qin combined small towns into large fortified cities, stationed troops at strategic passes and fortresses, built high walls without engaging in battle, closed the gates to control key positions, and defended with halberds. The feudal lords who rose from commoners united only for their own interests; they did not possess the moral authority of a true Son of Heaven. Their alliances were not close, and their subordinates had not yet pledged loyalty; though they claimed to have destroyed Qin, in reality, it was for their own benefit. They saw how difficult it was to breach Qin's defenses and naturally withdrew their troops. If they had allowed the people to rest in peace, waited for their enemies' strength to wane, strengthened the weak and supported the exhausted, then commanded the rulers of great states, there would have been no fear of failing to achieve dominance over all under heaven. To be as noble as an emperor and as wealthy as the world, yet end up captured like a bird in a cage—this was because their efforts to save themselves from defeat were misguided.

## 63

The kings of Qin trusted only themselves and did not seek counsel, so they persisted in their mistakes without change. Emperor Er Shi inherited this situation and made no changes; he grew increasingly tyrannical and deepened the calamity. Gongzi Ying was isolated without allies, weak and unsupported. Three rulers were deluded and never realized their mistakes until the end; thus, their downfall was entirely to be expected! At that time, the world was not lacking in wise and enlightened men who understood how to bring about change. Yet they dared not offer complete loyalty or correct their rulers' mistakes because Qin's customs were filled with prohibitions against criticism; before a loyal word could leave one's mouth, he would already be executed and his name erased. Thus, the scholars of all under heaven listened with bated breath, stood on tiptoe in fear, and clamped their mouths shut, unwilling to speak. This is why the three rulers lost their way: loyal ministers dared not advise, wise men dared not plan. When all under heaven was already in chaos, no one reported the treachery to the throne—how tragic! The ancient kings knew that being isolated from truth and obstructed by flattery would harm the state, so they appointed chancellors, ministers, officials, and scholars to uphold laws and establish punishments, thereby bringing order to all under heaven. When their power was strong, they suppressed violence and punished disorder, and the world submitted. When their strength waned, the Five Hegemons rose to dominance and the feudal lords followed them. When they were weakened further, they maintained internal stability and secured external alliances, thus preserving their state. Thus, when Qin was at its height, it imposed numerous laws and harsh punishments, and all under heaven trembled in awe; But by the time it declined, the common people resented and looked upon it with hopelessness, and rebellion spread throughout the empire. Therefore, Zhou followed five orderly systems in accordance with the Way, and its dynasty endured for over a thousand years without interruption. Qin lost both its fundamentals and its outward order, which is why it did not endure long. From this we can see that the difference between security and danger lies in how far one strays from the right path. A popular proverb says, "He who does not forget past events will find a teacher for future affairs." Therefore, a wise ruler governing the state should look to ancient times for guidance, verify with contemporary circumstances, consider human affairs, examine the principles of rise and decline, assess what is appropriate in terms of authority and power, act decisively when necessary, and adapt at the right time. In this way, he ensures long-term stability and peace for his state.

## 64

King Xiao of Qin occupied the strong position of Xiaohan and controlled Yongzhou. His rulers and ministers firmly held their ground, watching for an opportunity to challenge the Zhou dynasty. They harbored ambitions to sweep across the world, encompass all within its borders, enclose the four seas in a bag, and swallow up the eight directions. At that time, Shang Jun assisted him by establishing laws and regulations internally, encouraging agriculture and weaving, and preparing for defense and war. Externally, he pursued a policy of horizontal alliances to contend with the feudal lords. Thus, the people of Qin easily captured territory beyond the Xihe River.

## 65

After King Xiao's death, Kings Hui and Wu inherited his legacy and followed the established policies. They annexed Hanzhong to the south, captured Ba and Shu to the west, carved out fertile lands to the east, and seized key strategic commanderies. The feudal lords were alarmed and formed alliances to weaken Qin. They spared no precious treasures, valuable artifacts, or fertile lands in order to attract scholars from all under heaven, uniting them through vertical alliances into a single force. At that time, Qi had Mengchang, Zhao had Pingyuan, Chu had Chunshen, and Wei had Xinling. These four lords were all wise and trustworthy, broad-minded and compassionate. They respected the virtuous and valued scholars, forming vertical alliances to break horizontal ones, uniting the forces of Han, Wei, Yan, Chu, Qi, Zhao, Song, Wei, and Zhongshan. At that time, scholars from the six states such as Ningyue, Xushang, Su Qin, and Du He served as their strategists. Scholars like Qiming, Zhou Zui, Chen Zhen, Zhao Hua, Lou Huan, Di Jing, Su Li, and Yue Yi conveyed their intentions. Military leaders including Wu Qi, Sun Bin, Dai Tuo, Erliang, Wang Liao, Tian Ji,廉颇 (Lian Po), and Zhao She commanded their armies. They often attacked Qin with ten times the territory and a million troops, pounding at its gates in war. The people of Qin opened their gates to receive the enemy, yet the armies of nine states hesitated and fled without daring to advance. Qin suffered no loss of arrows or bolts, yet all the feudal lords were already exhausted. Thus, the vertical alliances collapsed and treaties dissolved; the states competed to offer land as tribute to Qin. Qin, with remaining strength, took advantage of their exhaustion and pursued the defeated forces, leaving hundreds of thousands of corpses lying on the battlefield and blood flowing so much it could float shields. Taking advantage of favorable circumstances, Qin carved up the world, divided rivers and mountains; powerful states requested submission while weak ones came to court. Extending into the reigns of King Xiaowenwang and Qin Zhuangxiang Wang, their time on the throne was brief, and there were no major events in the state.

## 66

When it came to the First Emperor of Qin, he continued the legacy of six generations, raised his long whip to rule within the realm, swallowed the two Zhou dynasties and eliminated all feudal lords, ascended to supreme authority and controlled the entire world. He wielded a stick to punish the people under heaven, and his might reverberated across the four seas. To the south he conquered the lands of Baiyue, establishing Guilin and Xiang commanderies. The chieftains of Baiyue bowed their heads with necks bound, surrendering to low-ranking officials. He then dispatched Meng Tian to build the Great Wall in the north as a defensive barrier, repelling the Xiongnu over seven hundred li. The Hu people dared not descend southward to pasture their horses, and soldiers did not dare bend their bows to take revenge. Thus, he abolished the ways of previous kings, burned the writings of a hundred schools of thought in order to delude the common people. He destroyed famous walled cities, killed the heroic and talented, collected all weapons from across the land to gather in Xianyang, melted sharp blades into bells, casting twelve golden statues, in order to weaken the common people. Then he cut through Mount Hua to build a city wall and used the Yellow River as a ferry crossing, occupying a ten-thousand-zhang high fortress and facing an unfathomable ravine for defense. Valiant generals with powerful crossbows guarded key strategic locations, while loyal ministers and elite soldiers stood ready with sharp weapons to question anyone. Thus the world was pacified. The First Emperor of Qin believed that the security of Guanzhong, with its golden city walls stretching for a thousand li, would be an eternal imperial legacy for his descendants.

## 67

After the First Emperor's death, Qin's remaining might still reverberated among distant peoples. Chen She was the son of a man with a clay window and rope door latch, a common laborer who had been exiled. His talent did not surpass that of an average person; he lacked the virtue of Confucius or Mozi, nor the wealth of Tao Zhu or Yidun. Rising from among the ranks of foot soldiers, he suddenly emerged as a leader in his hundred-man unit, commanding a few hundred dispirited and scattered troops to turn against Qin. They cut down trees for weapons and raised poles as banners. The people from all over the land gathered like clouds in response, carrying their own provisions to follow him like shadows. Thus, heroes and talented men from east of the mountains rose up together and brought about the downfall of the Qin clan.

## 68

Moreover, the world was not smaller or weaker; the land of Yongzhou and the strategic strength of Xiaohan remained as before. Chen She's position was not more exalted than that of the rulers of Qi, Chu, Yan, Zhao, Han, Wei, Song, Wei, or Zhongshan; His farming tools and thorny sticks were no sharper than the curved halberds and long spears; The conscripted soldiers he led were not a match for the armies of the nine states; His strategies, long-term planning, and methods of military command did not equal those of the scholars from previous times. Yet their successes and failures were different, and their achievements opposite. If one were to compare the states east of the mountain with Chen She in terms of length and breadth, or weigh their power and strength, they could not be mentioned in the same breath. Yet Qin, with its small territory and power of a thousand chariots, was able to bring eight provinces under subjugation and make fellow feudal lords come to court for more than one hundred years. Then, regarding the entire world as his home and Xiaohan as his palace, when a single man rose in rebellion, seven ancestral temples were destroyed. His body perished by another's hand, becoming a laughingstock to all under heaven—why was this so? It is because benevolence and righteousness were not practiced, leading to a reversal in the balance of attack and defense.

## 69

Qin unified the realm, annexed the feudal lords, faced south to assume imperial title, and ruled over all under heaven. Scholars from across the world flocked toward its culture—why was this so? It is said: for a long time in recent antiquity there had been no true emperor. The Zhou dynasty had become weak and humble, the Five Hegemons were already dead, and royal commands could not be enforced throughout the land. Therefore, feudal lords wielded power through force; the strong invaded the weak, the numerous oppressed the few, warfare never ceased, and both soldiers and civilians became exhausted. Now Qin faces south to rule over all under heaven; this means there is an emperor above. The common people, longing for peace and safety of their lives, all looked up to the emperor with sincerity. At this time, preserving power and securing achievements—this was where the foundation of stability or peril lay.

## 70

The First Emperor of Qin harbored greedy and narrow-minded intentions, acted with self-serving wisdom, did not trust meritorious ministers or care for the people. He abandoned the royal way, established private authority, banned written records while enforcing harsh laws and punishments, valued deceit and force over benevolence and righteousness, and thus began an era of tyranny for all under heaven. Those who seek to conquer value deception and force; those who seek stability prize harmony and authority. This is the saying that the methods of taking and holding power are different. Qin emerged from the Warring States to rule all under heaven, yet its methods and policies remained unchanged—thus, the way it took power was no different from how it sought to hold onto it. Isolated in its rule over all things, its downfall could be expected immediately. Had the First Emperor of Qin considered the affairs of previous generations and followed the examples of Yin and Zhou to regulate his governance, then even later rulers who were licentious or arrogant would not have faced imminent danger. Therefore, the Three Kings established their rule over the world with illustrious titles and enduring achievements.

## 71

Now that Qin's Second Emperor has ascended the throne, all under heaven look forward eagerly to observing his governance. The cold find warmth in rough cloth; the hungry are content with bran and chaff. The suffering people of the world are a resource for a new ruler. This is to say that it is easy to show benevolence toward weary and oppressed people. Had the Second Emperor possessed the conduct of an ordinary ruler and entrusted power to loyal and virtuous ministers, with emperor and subjects united in worrying over the people's hardships, wearing plain robes to correct his predecessor's mistakes, dividing land and people to enfeoff the descendants of meritorious officials, establishing states and appointing rulers according to rites for all under heaven, emptying prisons and pardoning punishments, abolishing laws that confiscated families or defiled reputations, allowing people to return to their hometowns, opening granaries and distributing wealth to support the lonely and impoverished, reducing taxes and burdens to relieve the urgent needs of the common folk, simplifying laws and lessening punishments for future stability—then all under heaven would have been able to reform themselves, cultivate virtue, and carefully conduct their lives. This would have fulfilled the people's expectations, and through authority and virtue, he could have united the world. Then within the four seas, all would rejoice and find peace in their places, fearing only change. Even if there were cunning people, they would have no desire to oppose authority; thus, unscrupulous ministers could not display their schemes, and violent rebels would be brought to a halt. The Second Emperor did not follow this path, but instead imposed tyranny upon the people, destroying ancestral temples and disturbing the populace. He initiated construction of the Afang Palace anew, enforced harsh punishments and severe executions, appointed officials who governed with cruelty, awarded rewards and punishments unjustly, levied taxes without restraint, caused chaos throughout the land, overwhelmed local officials to the point they could not manage affairs, and left the people in dire straits while the ruler showed no concern or compassion. Then, fraud and deceit arose together, with officials at all levels evading responsibility. Many were wrongly accused; along the roads, those awaiting execution could be seen in droves, and the people of the world suffered greatly from this. From the emperor and high officials down to common people, everyone harbored fears for their own safety. They personally endured hardship and poverty, feeling insecure in their positions, so they were easily stirred into action. Therefore, Chen She did not need the virtue of Tang and Wu or the prestige of dukes and marquises; when he raised his arm in Daze, all under heaven responded—because the people were on the brink of peril. Therefore, previous kings observed the changes from beginning to end and understood the keys to survival or destruction; thus, their way of governing the people was simply to ensure stability. Even if there were rebellious ministers in all under heaven, they would surely find no support or response. Hence it is said: "Stable people can be guided toward righteousness, while those in peril are easily led to wrongdoing." This is what that means. Though noble as an emperor and rich with all under heaven, if one cannot escape being killed, it is because of a wrong course. This was the fault of the Second Emperor.

King Huwenwang was enthroned at the age of nineteen. In his second year, currency was first introduced. There was a newborn infant named "Qin Qiangwang."

## 107

King Dao Wuwang was enthroned at nineteen years old. In his third year, the Wei River turned red for three days.

## 108

King Zhaoxiangwang was enthroned when he was nineteen years old. In his fourth year, farmland boundaries were first established by opening up fields and paths.

## 109

King Xiaowenwang was enthroned at fifty-three years old.

## 110

King Zhuangxiang Wang was enthroned when he turned thirty-two. In his second year, Taiyuan territory was captured. In the first year of King Zhuangxiang Wang's reign, a general pardon was issued; rewards were given to meritorious ministers of previous kings; kindness and favor were extended to close relatives; and benevolence was bestowed upon the people. Dongzhou conspired with various feudal lords against Qin, so the State of Qin sent Chancellor Lu Buwei to punish them, completely annexing their territory. Qin did not extinguish their ancestral rites; it granted Yangren territory to the Zhou ruler, allowing him to continue the ancestral sacrifices.

## 111

Shi Huangdi reigned for thirty-seven years. He was buried in Liyi. He had Emperor Er Shi as a son. Shi Huangdi was enthroned at thirteen years old.

## 112

Emperor Er Shi reigned for three years. He was buried in Yichun. Zhao Gao became Chancellor and the Marquis Anwu. Emperor Er Shi was enthroned at twelve years old.

## 113

From You Qin Xianggong to Emperor Er Shi, it spanned 610 years.

## 114

On the Yichou day of the fifteenth of the tenth month in the seventeenth year of Emperor Xiaoming Huangdi's reign, it was said:

## 115

The Zhou calendar has already changed; benevolence cannot replace a mother. Qin held its position uprightly, but Lü Zheng was cruel and oppressive. Yet by conquering thirteen feudal lords and uniting the empire under one rule, he indulged in unrestrained desires and provided for his relatives. In thirty-seven years, its military forces were deployed everywhere; laws and decrees established then were applied to later kings. Possibly having gained the might of a sage, with the River God presenting a map, occupying Lang and Hu, treading upon Shen and Fa, assisting in governance to drive away enemies, he resisted opposition and was thus called Shi Huang.

## 116

After Shi Huang's death, Hu Hai was extremely foolish; before the Lishan Mausoleum was completed, he began construction of Afang Palace to continue previous plans. It is said: "Whoever values possessing the empire does so by indulging in unrestrained desires, with ministers even wishing to abandon what previous kings had done." Li Si and Qu Ji were executed; Zhao Gao was appointed instead. How painful are these words! Human heads and the cries of livestock. Without strength or punishment for evil, without firmness or truth to avoid destruction, resistance cannot hold out; cruelty only hastens the end. Even in a country with favorable geography, it still could not survive.

## 117

Ziying, upon succeeding to the throne in due order, wore a jade crown and adorned himself with embroidered sashes; he rode in a yellow-roofed carriage, followed by a hundred officials, and paid homage at the seven ancestral temples. Lowly men occupying improper positions all lost their composure, seeking only daily peace and comfort. Only he could maintain long-term vigilance and foresight; father and son acted decisively, drawing close from within the household, ultimately executing treacherous ministers and avenging the king by punishing rebels. After Zhao Gao's death, the wedding guests had not yet finished their toasts; food was still in his mouth and wine had barely touched his lips when Chu forces already razed Guanzhong. The true ruler flew over Bashang, arriving in a plain carriage with ribbons around its yoke, presenting the imperial seal and jade scepter to return them to the new emperor. Duke Zheng bore the ceremonial flag and bird-shaped blade; King Yan withdrew his troops. A breached river cannot be dammed again, and rotten fish cannot be restored to perfection. Jia Yi and Sima Qian said: "Had Ziying possessed the talent of an average ruler, had he merely received moderate assistance, even though Shandong was in chaos, Qin's territory could have been preserved intact, and the ancestral rites would not have perished." Qin's accumulated decline led to the collapse of its empire like crumbling earth and shattered tiles. Even had he possessed the talents of Zhou Dan, there was no opportunity left for him to display his skill; yet blame was placed on a lone ruler in one day—how mistaken that is! The common belief that Emperor Qin Shi Huang began the evils, and Hu Hai reached their peak, indeed captures the truth. To further blame a young man, claiming Qin's territory could have been saved, is to be what is called "ignorant of the changing times." Ji Ji offered Xi; in the Spring and Autumn Annals, he was not named. When I read the records of Qin's history and came to the account of Ziying having Zhao Gao torn apart by chariots, I have never failed to admire his resolve and feel sympathy for his determination. Ziying's understanding of life and death was complete.
