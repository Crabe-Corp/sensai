# Biographies of General Li

## 1

General Li Guang was a native of Chengji, Longxi. His ancestor was Li Xin, who served as a general during the Qin dynasty and was the one who pursued and captured Crown Prince Dan of Yan. They were originally from Huaiyi, but later moved to Chengji. The Li Guang family passed down the art of archery through generations. In the fourteenth year of Emperor Xiaowen's reign, the Xiongnu launched a large-scale invasion through Xiao Pass. At that time, Guang joined the army as a son of an upstanding family to fight against the Hu people. Because he was skilled in horseback riding and archery and killed many enemies, he was appointed as a Han Zhonglang (a mid-ranking official). Guang's cousin, Li Cai, also became a Lang officer; both served as Wuqi Changshi (elite cavalry attendants), holding the rank of eight hundred dan. He once accompanied an expedition, where he broke through enemy lines and overcame obstacles as well as fought fierce beasts. Emperor Wendi said: "Alas! You were unfortunate not to meet the right time!" "If you had served during Emperor Gaozu's reign, would a title of ten thousand households be worth mentioning at all!"

## 2

When Emperor Xiaojing first ascended the throne, Guang was appointed as the Duwei (military officer) of Longxi and later transferred to serve as a Qilang Jiang. During the Wu and Chu rebellion, Guang served as Xiaoqi Duwei (a cavalry commander) and followed Taiwei Yafu to fight against the armies of Wu and Chu. He captured their banner and gained fame for his distinguished service at Changyi. When Prince Liang gave Guang a general's seal, upon returning, he did not receive official recognition or reward. He was transferred to serve as Taishou (governor) of Shanggu, where the Xiongnu frequently engaged in battles with him. Gongsun Kunye, the Dianshuugu mainstream official, wept before the emperor and said: "General Li Guang's talent is unparalleled in the world. He relies on his own abilities and frequently fights against the enemy; I fear he may lose his life." Thus, it was decided to transfer him to serve as Taishou of Shangjun. Later, Guang was transferred to serve as the governor of a border commandery and moved to Shangjun. He once served as the Taishou of Longxi, Beidi, Yanmen, Daijun, and Yunzhong, all known for his reputation in fierce battles.

## 3

When the Xiongnu launched a major invasion into Shangjun, the Son of Heaven sent an important imperial official to accompany Guang in training troops and fighting against the Xiongnu. The imperial official led several dozen cavalrymen forward, encountered three Xiongnu warriors, and engaged them in battle. The three Xiongnu turned to shoot back, wounding the imperial official and killing nearly all of his cavalrymen. The imperial official fled to General Guang for help. Guang said, "These must be the archers who shoot eagles." So Guang immediately led a hundred cavalrymen to charge at the three men. The three Xiongnu, having lost their horses, walked on foot for several tens of miles. Guang ordered his cavalry to spread out on both flanks, while he personally shot at the three Xiongnu men. He killed two of them and captured one alive, who indeed turned out to be a Xiongnu eagle shooter. After tying the captive onto a horse, they looked up and saw several thousand Xiongnu cavalrymen. Upon seeing Guang, they thought he was a decoy force and were all startled, retreating to form ranks on the mountain. Guang's hundred cavalrymen were greatly frightened and wanted to gallop back in retreat. Guang said, "We are several tens of miles away from the main army. If we now flee with just a hundred cavalrymen, the Xiongnu will chase and shoot us down immediately." "Now if I stay behind, the Xiongnu must think that I am a decoy for our main force and will certainly dare not attack me." Guang ordered his cavalrymen: "Forward!" Before they reached the Xiongnu formation by about two li, they stopped. Guang gave orders: "All of you dismount and remove your saddles!" His cavalrymen said, "The enemy is numerous and nearby; if there's an emergency, what shall we do?" Guang said: "The enemy thinks that we are fleeing. Now by removing our saddles, we show them that we will not run away, thus strengthening their resolve." Thus, the Xiongnu cavalry dared not attack. A Xiongnu general on a white horse came out to protect his troops. Li Guang mounted his horse and, with more than ten cavalrymen, rushed forward to shoot and kill the Xiongnu's white-horse general. Then he returned to his own cavalry, removed his saddle again, and ordered all soldiers to let their horses lie down. At that time night was falling, and the Xiongnu troops remained suspicious throughout, daring not to attack. At midnight, the Xiongnu forces also thought that Han troops had hidden nearby and were planning to attack them at night. Thus, they withdrew their army. At dawn, Li Guang returned to his main force. The main army did not know where General Guang had gone and therefore did not follow him.

## 4

After a long time, Emperor Xiaojing died and Emperor Wu ascended the throne. Those around him considered Guang to be a famous general; thus, Guang was appointed from his position as Taishou of Shangju to serve as Weiyi of Weiyang Palace, while Cheng Bushi also became Weiyi of Changle Palace. Cheng Bushi had previously served together with Li Guang as border commandery generals in charge of garrison duties. When they went out to fight the Hu, General Guang did not maintain strict ranks or formations. He chose to encamp near good water and grass at his convenience, allowing each soldier to rest as he pleased. They did not use weapons for mutual defense, and the general's headquarters handled few official documents and paperwork. However, he also kept distant scouts and had never encountered harm. Cheng Bushi, on the other hand, maintained strict ranks and formations for his troops. He enforced weapons training, had officers meticulously manage military records until dawn, and did not allow soldiers to rest; yet he also never encountered harm. Cheng Bushi said: "General Li Guang's army is extremely simple and relaxed, but if the enemy suddenly attacks, there will be no way to stop them; yet his soldiers are also carefree and happy, all willing to die for him. My army may be troublesome and strict, but the enemy cannot attack us either." At that time, both Li Guang and Cheng Bushi were famous generals in the Han border commanderies. However, the Xiongnu feared General Li Guang's strategy, while soldiers also preferred to follow Li Guang and found service under Cheng Bushi burdensome. During Emperor Xiaojing's reign, Cheng Bushi was appointed Tai Zhongdafa for his frequent and direct remonstrations. He was a man of integrity and meticulous about legal procedures.

## 5

Later, the Han dynasty used Ma Yi city to lure Chanyu and stationed a large army in ambush in the valleys around Ma Yi. At that time, Li Guang served as Xiaoqi Jiangjun (Cavalry General) under the command of Hujun Jiangjun (Commander-in-Chief). At that time, Chanyu became aware of the plan and withdrew; thus, the Han army achieved no success. Four years later, Guang was appointed as Jiangjun (general) from his position of Weiyi and led an expedition through Yanmen to fight the Xiongnu. The Xiongnu had a large force, defeated Guang's army, and captured him alive. Chanyu had long heard of Li Guang's virtue and gave orders: "If you capture Li Guang, bring him back alive." The Xiongnu cavalry captured Guang. At that time Guang was injured and ill; they placed him between two horses, tied him up, and made him lie down in a cradle-like position. After traveling more than ten li, Guang pretended to be dead. He glanced sideways and saw a Xiongnu boy riding a fine horse. Guang suddenly leaped up onto the boy's horse, pushed the boy off, took his bow, and whipped the horse southward for several tens of li before rejoining the rest of his troops and leading them back into the frontier pass. Hundreds of Xiongnu cavalrymen in pursuit gave chase, but Guang retrieved the bow from the Xiongnu boy and shot down several pursuers. Thus he managed to escape. After returning to Han territory, the court placed General Guang under investigation by officials. The officials determined that Guang had suffered heavy losses and had been captured alive by the enemy, which was punishable by death. However, he paid a ransom to avoid execution and was reduced to commoner status.

## 6

After some time, he remained at home for several years. The Li Guang family lived in the southern mountains of Lantian with Sun Pingye, a descendant of Marquis Yingyin, hunting and shooting game. He once went out at night with one mounted attendant to drink in a field with his companions. On their return, they arrived at Balin Ting (a roadside station), where the Balin Wei (an official) was drunk and shouted to stop Guang. Guang's attendant said: "This is General Li, formerly of high rank." The official replied: "Even a current general cannot travel at night; how much less so for an old one!" He made Guang stop and stay under the pavilion of the roadside station. Not long after, the Xiongnu invaded and killed the Taishou of Liaoxi, defeating General Han. Later, General Han was transferred to Youbeiping. At this time, the Son of Heaven summoned Guang and appointed him as Taishou (Governor) of Youbeiping. Guang immediately requested to bring the Balin Wei with him, and upon arriving at his military post, he had him executed.

## 7

While Guang was stationed in Youbeiping, the Xiongnu heard of it and called him "the Flying General of Han," avoiding confrontation for several years and daring not to enter Youbeiping.

## 8

While hunting, Guang saw a stone in the grass and mistook it for a tiger; he shot at it, penetrating the stone with his arrow. Upon closer inspection, he realized it was just a stone. He then tried shooting again, but could never penetrate the stone as before. Whenever the commandery where Guang was stationed heard of tigers, he often shot them himself. When he was in Youbeiping and shot a tiger, the tiger leaped up and injured Guang; however, Guang eventually shot and killed it.

## 9

Guang was upright and whenever he received rewards or gifts, he would distribute them among his subordinates, sharing food and drink with the soldiers. Throughout his life, Guang held positions of the rank of two thousand dan for more than forty years, yet his family had no surplus wealth. He never spoke about matters related to personal property. Guang was tall and had long, flexible arms like a gibbon; his skill in archery was innate. Even among his descendants or others who studied it, none could match Guang's ability. Guang was a quiet man with few words; when he stayed among others, he would draw military formations on the ground and shoot at targets of varying widths as part of drinking games. He treated archery purely as a game until his death. When General Guang led troops, in times of scarcity and hardship, when soldiers saw water but had not all drunk, he would not approach the water. When they had not all eaten, he would not taste food either. He was lenient and not harsh; soldiers therefore loved and were willing to serve under him. When it came to archery, if the enemy was not within several dozen paces and he estimated that his shot would not hit, he would not fire. But once he did shoot, the target would fall as soon as the bowstring was released. Because of this, his command had often faced hardships and humiliations; even when shooting fierce beasts, he too was once injured.

## 10

After a short while, Shi Jian died, so the emperor summoned Guang to replace him as Langzhongling (Minister of Justice). In the sixth year of Yuanshuo, Guang was again appointed as Hou Jiangjun (Rear General) and followed the Da Jiangjun's army to depart from Dingxiang for a campaign against the Xiongnu. Many of the other generals captured enemy leaders and were ennobled as marquises for their achievements, but General Guang's army achieved no merit. Two years later, Guang led four thousand cavalrymen from Youbeiping as Langzhongling. Marquis Bolang Hou Zhang Qian commanded ten thousand cavalrymen and marched with Guang but took a different route. After traveling about several hundred li, the Left Xianwang of the Xiongnu led forty thousand cavalrymen to surround Guang. His soldiers were all frightened, so Guang sent his son, Gan, to charge forward against them. Gan rode ahead with only several dozen cavalrymen, directly charging through the Xiongnu ranks, breaking out from both sides and returning to report to Guang: "The enemy is easy to deal with." The soldiers then calmed down. Guang arranged his troops in a circular formation facing outward, and the Xiongnu attacked fiercely, arrows raining down like rain. More than half of the Han soldiers were killed, and their arrows were nearly exhausted. Guang then ordered his soldiers to keep their bows drawn but not to fire, while he personally used a large yellow bow (a powerful weapon) to shoot at the enemy's subordinate generals, killing several of them. The Xiongnu gradually began to retreat. As night fell, all the officers and soldiers looked pale with fear, but Guang remained calm and composed, continuing to organize his troops. From then on, the army came to admire his bravery. The next day, they fought fiercely again, and Marquis Bolang's forces also arrived; the Xiongnu army then withdrew. The Han army was exhausted and could not pursue them. At that time, General Guang's forces were nearly wiped out and returned in defeat. According to Han law, Marquis Bolang had delayed his departure and arrived late, which was punishable by death; he paid a fine instead and was demoted to commoner status. General Guang's military achievements were as usual, yet he received no reward.

## 11

Initially, Li Cai, Guang's cousin, had served alongside Guang under Emperor Xiaowen. During the reign of Emperor Jingdi, Cai accumulated enough merit to attain a position equivalent to that of an Erqianshi official. During the reign of Emperor Xiaowudi, he rose to become the governor of Dai Commandery. In the fifth year of Yuanshuo, he was appointed as a Light Chariot General and followed the Grand General in attacking Youxian Wang. For his meritorious service, he was enfeoffed as Marquis Le'an. In the second year of Yuanshou, he succeeded Gongsun Hong as Chancellor. Li Cai was a man of mediocre ability, and his reputation was far less than Guang's. Yet while Guang received no title or fief, and held no higher office than that of one of the Nine Ministers, Cai became a marquis and rose to the rank of Three Excellencies. Some of Guang's officers and soldiers received titles of nobility. Guang once conversed in private with Wang Shuo, an expert in divining omens from the sky's appearance, saying: "Since the Han began attacking the Xiongnu, I have never been absent from these campaigns. Yet many of the officers and men under me, whose abilities are no better than those of ordinary people, have received titles for their military achievements against the Hu—there are dozens of them. But I, who am not lacking in ability or experience, yet have not received even a fief or title, why is that?" Is it because my physiognomy does not indicate the destiny of being enfeoffed as marquis? Or is this simply fate?" Shuo said, "General, do you yourself recall ever having done anything regrettable?" Guang said, "I once served as the governor of Longxi. When the Qiang rebelled, I lured them into surrendering; over 800 surrendered. I then deceived them and had them all killed on the same day." Until now, this is my only great regret." Shuo said, "There can be no greater misfortune than killing those who have surrendered. This is why General has not been enfeoffed."

## 12

Two years later, when the Grand General and the Biaoqi General launched a major campaign against the Xiongnu, Guang repeatedly volunteered to join. The emperor considered him too old and did not approve; After a long time, the emperor finally approved his request and appointed him as the Vanguard General. This year was Yuanshou Year Four.

## 13

After General Guang joined the Grand General Qing in attacking the Xiongnu, once they had crossed the frontier, Qing captured a prisoner who revealed where the Chanyu was located. Qing then led his elite troops directly toward him and ordered Guang to join forces with the Right General's army and take the eastern route. The eastern route was somewhat winding and distant, while the main force had to travel through an area with scarce water and grass, making it impossible for them to march in formation. Guang made a personal request, saying: "My unit was appointed as the Vanguard General. Now the Grand General has ordered me to take the eastern route instead. Moreover, since I first took up arms against the Xiongnu in my youth, this is the one opportunity for me to confront the Chanyu directly. I wish to lead the charge and die before the Chanyu." Grand General Qing had also secretly received instructions from the emperor, who considered Li Guang old and unlucky, and did not want him to face the Chanyu directly for fear that he would fail to achieve his objectives. At that time, Gongsun Ao had recently lost his marquis title and was serving as the Central General under the Grand General. The Grand General also wanted to give Ao a chance to confront the Chanyu and thus transferred the Vanguard General Guang away. Guang, who was aware of this at the time, firmly petitioned the Grand General for a change in assignment. The Grand General did not listen and ordered his chief clerk to deliver the written orders to Guang's headquarters, saying: "Hurry to your post as instructed in the letter." Guang did not thank the Grand General but immediately set out, deeply resentful and disgruntled. He led his troops to join with those of the Right General Shi Qi and marched eastward along the eastern route. The army lacked guides, some lost their way, and arrived behind schedule compared to the Grand General's forces. The Grand General engaged in battle with the Chanyu, but the Chanyu escaped and could not be captured; thus, he returned. While returning south across the desert, the Grand General encountered the Vanguard General and the Right General. After meeting with the Grand General, Guang returned to his own troops. The Grand General sent the chief clerk with provisions and wine to give to Guang, while also inquiring about how Guang and Shi Qi had lost their way. Qing intended to report the details of the military campaign to the emperor. Before Guang could respond, the Grand General ordered his chief clerk to urgently summon Guang's headquarters for an official inquiry. Guang said: "The various commanders are innocent; it was I who lost the way myself." I will now submit my own report."

## 14

When he arrived at the headquarters, Guang said to his subordinates: "Since I first took up arms, I have fought in more than seventy battles against the Xiongnu. Now I am fortunate to be following the Grand General out to confront the Chanyu's forces, yet the Grand General moved my unit on a long and winding route, which led me to lose my way—was this not fate?" Moreover, I am now over sixty years old and will never again be able to face the officials who wield brushes like swords." He then took out his sword and committed suicide. All the officers and soldiers in Guang's army wept bitterly. The common people, whether they knew him or not, young or old, all shed tears upon hearing the news. Meanwhile, the Right General was alone brought before the authorities and sentenced to death; he paid a ransom instead and was demoted to the status of a commoner.

## 15

General Guang had three sons: Danghu, Jiao, and Gan, who all served as Lang officials. The emperor was playing with Han Yan, but Yan showed no deference to the young Danghu, who struck Yan and caused him to flee. At this, the emperor considered Danghu brave. Danghu died young; Jiao was later appointed as governor of Dai Jun, but both predeceased Guang. Danghu had a posthumous son named Ling. At the time of Guang's death in battle, Gan was serving under General Biaoqi. The year after Guang's death, Li Cai was implicated in the crime of encroaching on land adjacent to Emperor Jingdi's mausoleum and was supposed to be handed over for trial. Cai committed suicide before facing interrogation; his fiefdom was abolished as a result. Li Gan, as a commander, followed General Biaoqi in attacking the Left Chanyu of the Xiongnu. He fought bravely, captured the enemy's drum and banner, and killed many foes. For his valor, he was granted the title of Guannei Hou (a marquis within the Pass) with a fief of 200 households, succeeding Guang as Minister of the Central Secretariat. Soon after, out of resentment toward Grand General Qing for his father's disgrace, Gan attacked and wounded the Grand General. Qing concealed and covered up the incident. Not long after, Gan accompanied the emperor to Yong, where they arrived at Ganzhuan Palace for a hunting expedition. Biaoqi General Wei Qing had a close relationship with General Qing and shot and killed Gan. At that time, Wei Qing was highly favored by the emperor; it was said to be concealed that a deer had gored him to death. A year or so later, Wei Qing died. Gan had a daughter who was a concubine of the Crown Prince and enjoyed his favor; Gan's son Yu also received affection from the Crown Prince, but he was greedy for profit. The Li family gradually declined into obscurity.

## 16

When Li Ling grew up and became strong, he was selected as Jianzhang Jian (a supervisor of the imperial stables), overseeing all the cavalry. He was skilled in archery and loved his soldiers. The emperor, considering the Li family to be a lineage of generals, appointed him to command 800 cavalrymen. He once led his forces deep into Xiongnu territory for over two thousand li, passing through Juyan to scout the terrain. Finding no enemy troops, he returned. He was then appointed as Qidouwei (Cavalry Commander) and given command of 5,000 men from Danyang and Chu. He trained them in Jiuquan and Zhangye to guard against the Xiongnu raids.

## 17

Several years later, in the autumn of Tianhan Year Two, General Li Guangli led 30,000 cavalry to attack Youxian Wang of the Xiongnu at Qilian Tianshan. The emperor ordered Ling to lead his 5,000 archers and infantrymen more than a thousand li north of Juyan in an attempt to divide the enemy forces and prevent them from concentrating against General Guangli's army. After Ling had reached his destination on schedule, the Chanyu surrounded and attacked his forces with 80,000 troops. Ling's army of 5,000 had exhausted its weapons and arrows; more than half the soldiers were killed. Yet they also inflicted over ten thousand casualties on the Xiongnu forces. While retreating and fighting, they engaged in continuous battles for eight days. Still more than a hundred li away from Juyan, the Xiongnu blocked their narrow escape route. With food supplies exhausted and no reinforcements arriving, the enemy intensified their attacks and urged Ling to surrender. Ling said: "I have no face to return to report to the emperor." And so he surrendered to the Xiongnu. All of his troops were lost, and only more than 400 soldiers managed to escape back to Han.

## 18

After capturing Ling, the Chanyu had long heard of his family's reputation and, impressed by Ling's valor in battle, married him to his daughter and granted him a high status. When Han learned of this, they executed all of Ling's mother, wife, and children. From then on, the Li family's reputation was ruined, and all scholars from Longxi who served under them regarded it as a disgrace.

## 19

Grand Historian said: The Classic says: "If the leader himself is upright, he can lead without issuing orders; if he is not upright, his commands will go unheeded." Is this not a reference to General Li Guang? I observed that General Li was modest and unassuming, like an ordinary man, and lacked the eloquence to speak well. Yet on the day of his death, people throughout the land—whether they knew him or not—all mourned for him deeply. Was it not because he had a sincere and loyal heart that earned the trust of the literati? The proverb says, "Peaches and plums need not speak; beneath them, paths form naturally." Though this saying may seem small in scale, it can convey a great truth.
