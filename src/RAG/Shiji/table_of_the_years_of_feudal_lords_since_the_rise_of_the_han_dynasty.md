# Table of the Years of Feudal Lords since the Rise of the Han Dynasty

## 1

The Grand Historian said: The Yin dynasty and those before it have long passed away. The Zhou dynasty established five ranks of enfeoffment: gong, hou, bo, zi, nan. Yet when they enfeoffed Boqin and Kangshu to Lu and Wei, each was granted a territory of four hundred li; this demonstrated the principle of showing affection for close relatives and rewarding virtue. Taigong at Qi received land equivalent to all five marquisates combined, as a sign of respect for his diligence and service. King Wu, King Cheng, and King Kang enfeoffed hundreds of vassals, among whom fifty-five were of the same clan. Their territories ranged no more than a hundred li at the upper end and thirty li at the lower end, all to serve as support and defense for the royal court. Guan, Cai, Kangshu, Cao, Zheng—some received more than their due, others less. After the reigns of Kings Li and You, the royal house declined, and powerful states led by marquises and dukes arose; the Son of Heaven became weak and was unable to restore order. This was not because virtue had entirely vanished, but because their position had become weak.

## 2

When the Han dynasty rose to power, it established a system of two ranks. In the later years of Emperor Gaozu, any non-Liu clan member who became a king, or anyone without merit whom the emperor had not appointed as marquis, was to be universally executed by all under heaven. Emperor Gaozu enfeoffed nine of his sons and brothers as kings in the same clan, although only Changsha was an exception with a different surname; more than one hundred meritorious ministers were made marquises. From Yanmen and Taiyuan eastward to Liaoyang, this area formed the states of Yan and Dai; south of Changshan, turning left from Daxing, crossing the Yellow River and Ji River, from E and Zhen eastward to the Bohai Sea, formed the states of Qi and Zhao; From Chen westward, south to Jiuyi, and east along the rivers Jiang, Huai, Gu, and Si reaching as far as Kuaiji, formed the states of Liang, Chu, Huainan, and Changsha: all these were externally adjacent to the Hu and Yue peoples. Within the interior, from north of the mountains eastward all the way to the territories of the feudal lords, some large states controlled as many as five or six commanderies, with dozens of walled cities and full complements of officials and palaces, rivaling even the Son of Heaven in splendor. The Han dynasty alone controlled Sanhe, Dongjun, Yingchuan, and Nanyang; from Jiangling westward to Shu, and north from Yunzhong to Longxi, together with the Neishi, making a total of fifteen commanderies, among which princesses and marquises held fiefs. Why was this so? At the beginning of the dynasty's founding, there were few close relatives of the same surname; therefore, they widely enfeoffed powerful collateral branches to pacify and stabilize all under heaven, using them to support and defend the Son of Heaven.

## 3

During the hundred years of Han rule, relatives became increasingly distant; some feudal lords grew arrogant and luxurious, listening to wicked ministers' schemes and plots that led to licentious disorder. The more powerful ones rebelled, while even lesser ones violated laws, endangering their lives and ultimately losing both their states and themselves. The Son of Heaven, observing the practices of ancient times, then bestowed grace upon the feudal lords, allowing them to distribute their domains and fiefs among their sons and younger brothers through imperial favor. Thus, Qi was divided into seven parts, Zhao into six, Liang into five, Huainan into three, as well as royal collateral branches being made kings and princes' collateral descendants becoming marquises—more than a hundred in total. During the time of Wu and Chu, later feudal lords were sometimes deprived of territory due to their legitimate status. Thus, Yan and Dai lost northern border commanderies; Wu, Huainan, Changsha lost southern border commanderies; while the outlying districts, famous mountains, lakes, and seas under Qi, Zhao, Liang, and Chu all came under Han control. The feudal lords gradually diminished in power; the largest states controlled no more than a dozen walled cities, and minor marquises held territory of only dozens of li. This was sufficient at the top to fulfill their duties of tribute and service, and at the bottom to support ancestral sacrifices, thereby assisting and defending the imperial capital. Meanwhile, the Han commanderies numbered eighty to ninety, strategically interspersed among the feudal states with overlapping borders like interlocking dog teeth. They controlled key mountain passes and advantageous terrain, strengthening the central authority while weakening peripheral powers. With clear distinctions of rank established, all affairs found their proper place.

## 4

I, Qian, respectfully record the feudal lords from Emperor Gaozu to Taichu, compiling a genealogy of their gains and losses over time so that later generations may review them. Though strategic advantages might be strong, the essential foundation lies in benevolence and righteousness.

## 5

The Grand Historian's commentary: The enfeoffment of feudal lords was a system established since ancient times; it is not something I have invented.
