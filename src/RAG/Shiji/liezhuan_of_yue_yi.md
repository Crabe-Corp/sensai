# Liezhuan of Yue Yi

## 1

Yue Yi was a man whose ancestor was named Yue Yang. Yue Yang was a general of Wei Wenhou, and he attacked and captured Zhongshan. Wei Wenhou granted Yue Yang the fief of Lingshou as a reward. After Yue Yang died, he was buried in Lingshou, and his descendants later settled there permanently. Zhongshan regained its statehood, but during the reign of Zhao Wulingwang, Zhongshan was once again destroyed. It was then that a descendant of the Yue family named Yue Yi emerged.

## 2

Yue Yi was virtuous and fond of military affairs, and he was recommended by the people of Zhao. When King Wuling of Zhao encountered the Shaqiu rebellion, Yue Yi left Zhao and went to Wei. He heard that King Zhao of Yan had suffered a great defeat at the hands of Qi due to the turmoil caused by Zi Zhi, and King Zhao of Yan harbored resentment toward Qi. He never forgot for even one day his desire for revenge against Qi. Yan was a small and remote state, lacking the strength to dominate others. Therefore, King Zhao humbled himself before scholars and first honored Guowei with courtesy in order to attract virtuous men. Yue Yi then served as an envoy of Wei Zhaowang to Yan, and King Zhao received him with the courtesy due to a guest. Yue Yi declined the honor at first, but eventually pledged his loyalty and became a subject of Yan. King Zhao of Yan appointed him as an assistant minister (Ya Qing), and he held this position for a long time.

## 3

At that time, King Mǐn of Qi was powerful. To the south, he defeated Tang Mei, the prime minister of Chu, at Chongqiu; to the west, he crushed the Three Jin states at Guanjin. He then joined forces with the Three Jin against Qin, helped Zhao destroy Zhongshan, and conquered Song, expanding his territory by more than a thousand li. He competed with King Zhao of Qin for the title of emperor but later abandoned it and returned to the previous system. All the feudal lords wished to turn away from Qin and submit to Qi instead. King Mǐn grew conceited, and the people could no longer endure his rule. At this time, King Zhao of Yan inquired about launching a campaign against Qi. Yue Yi replied, "Qi is the legacy of a hegemonic state; it has vast territory and numerous people. It would not be easy to attack alone." "If Your Majesty wishes to attack Qi, there is no better course of action than to ally with Zhao and also with Chu and Wei." Thus, he sent Yue Yi to make an alliance with King Huwen of Zhao and dispatched others separately to connect with Chu and Wei. He also ordered Zhao Lan to persuade Qin about the benefits of attacking Qi. The feudal lords, resenting King Mǐn's arrogance and cruelty, all vied to join a coalition with Yan against Qi. After Yue Yi returned with the report, King Zhao of Yan mobilized all his troops and appointed Yue Yi as supreme general. King Huwen of Zhao conferred on Yue Yi the seal of prime minister. Yue Yi thus led the combined forces of Zhao, Chu, Han, Wei, and Yan to attack Qi and defeated them at the western bank of the Ji River. lington After the allied forces withdrew, Yue Yi's Yan army alone continued in pursuit, advancing all the way to Linzi. After King Mǐn of Qi suffered defeat at the western bank of the Ji, he fled and took refuge in Ju. Yue Yi remained alone to pacify Qi, but all the cities were defended by Qi forces. Yue Yi captured Linzi and seized all of Qi's treasures, valuables, and ritual vessels, transporting them to Yan. King Zhao of Yan was greatly pleased and personally went to the banks of the Ji River to 慰劳 his troops, rewarding and entertaining the soldiers. He granted Yue Yi the fief of Changguo and bestowed upon him the title "Lord of Changguo." At this time, King Zhao of Yuan collected the captives and spoils from Qi and returned them to Yan, while sending Yue Yi back with his troops to pacify any remaining cities in Qi that had not surrendered.

## 4

Yue Yi remained in Qi for five years, subduing more than seventy cities. He established them as commanderies and counties under Yan's control, with the exception of Ju and Jiimo, which had not yet surrendered. At this time King Zhao of Yan died, and his son ascended the throne as King Hui of Yan. King Hui, while still a crown prince, had once been dissatisfied with Yue Yi. Upon ascending the throne, Tian Dan of Qi learned about this and sent a spy to Yan, saying: "Only two cities in Qi have not yet fallen." "The reason they have not been captured sooner is that we heard Yue Yi has a conflict with the new King of Yan, and he intends to remain in Qi with his troops, declaring himself king there." "What Qi fears most is that another general will come." Thus, King Hui of Yan had already suspected Yue Yi. Upon receiving the spy's report from Qi, he sent Qi Jie to replace him as general and summoned Yue Yi back. Yue Yi knew that King Hui of Yan would not treat his replacement kindly, so he feared punishment and therefore surrendered to Zhao in the west. Zhao granted Yue Yi the fief at Guanjing and bestowed upon him the title "Lord Wangzhu." Zhao honored and favored Yue Yi in order to serve as a warning to Yan and Qi.

## 5

Later, Tian Dan of Qi fought against Qi Jie and indeed used deception to mislead the Yan army. He defeated Qi Jie beneath Jiimo and then led his forces in a series of battles that drove out the Yan troops as far north as the Yellow River. All the cities of Qi were thus recovered, and he welcomed King Xiang back from Ju, bringing him into Linzi.

## 6

King Hui of Yan later regretted having sent Qi Jie to replace Yue Yi, as a result of which his army was defeated, his general lost, and Qi was lost. He also resented Yue Yi for surrendering to Zhao, fearing that Zhao might employ Yue Yi and take advantage of Yan's weakness to attack it. King Hui of Yan therefore sent someone to reprimand Yue Yi and also apologized, saying: "The late king entrusted the entire state to you, General. You destroyed Qi for Yan and avenged our late ruler's grudge; all under heaven were astonished. How could I possibly forget even one day your great achievements, O General!" "It was only when the late king left us that I assumed the throne recently. My attendants misled me." "I sent Qi Jie to take your place because you had been exposed to the hardships of war for too long, and I wished to recall you so that we could discuss matters together." "You misunderstood me, thinking there was a rift between us, and thus abandoned Yan for Zhao." "If you acted in your own interest, that is understandable. But how can you repay the late king's kindness and trust toward you?" Yue Yi responded with a letter to King Hui of Yan:

## 7

"I am not eloquent or capable enough to carry out Your Majesty's commands and please those around you. I feared that doing so would tarnish the late king's wisdom and harm your virtue, so I fled to Zhao." "Now that Your Majesty has sent someone to accuse me of crimes, I fear the attendants may not understand why the late king cherished and trusted me, nor comprehend my loyalty in serving him. Therefore, I dare to respond with this letter."

## 8

I have heard that a virtuous and wise ruler does not bestow official positions or rewards out of favoritism toward relatives. He rewards those who achieve much, and appoints to office only those whose abilities are suitable. Therefore, a ruler who assigns positions according to ability is one who achieves success. A man of virtue who forms friendships based on character is the kind who establishes a good reputation. I secretly observed how the late king selected his officials, and saw that he had a mind above ordinary rulers. Thus, I served as an envoy in Wei and was personally recognized by Yan. The late king made a generous appointment, placing me among his guests and above all the ministers without consulting my father or elder brothers, appointing me as an assistant minister. I did not fully understand the honor myself and assumed that by following orders and receiving instruction, I would be fortunate to remain without blame. Therefore, I accepted the command without refusal.

## 9

The late king commanded me: "I have deep resentment and anger toward Qi, and without considering my own weakness, I wish to make Qi the target of my efforts." I replied: "Qi is a legacy of a hegemonic power and the most formidable remaining force. Its soldiers are well-trained, its weapons and armor refined, and its people experienced in warfare and siege." "If Your Majesty wishes to attack it, you must plan with the whole world against it." Of all ways to plan with the world, none is better than forming an alliance with Zhao. Moreover, the regions of northern Huai and Song are what Chu and Wei desire. If Zhao agrees to form an alliance with four states to attack Qi, then Qi can be utterly defeated." The late king considered this advice sound and provided me with official credentials to serve as envoy to Zhao. After I returned from my mission, the campaign against Qi was launched. By virtue of Heaven's will and the late king's spiritual guidance, the lands north of the He River rallied to follow him at the banks of the Ji. The army at the Ji received orders to attack Qi and decisively defeated its forces. Light infantry and elite troops advanced swiftly all the way into the heart of Qi's territory. King of Qi fled to Ju, barely escaping with his life; All the treasures, jewels, wealth, chariots, armor, and precious artifacts were seized and brought into Yan. The treasures of Qi were displayed on the Ning Tai, the great bell Da Lü was placed in Yuan Ying, ancient tripods were returned to their proper place at Li Shi, and trees from Jiqiu were planted in Wenhuang. Since the Five Hegemons, no ruler had achieved a feat comparable to that of the late king. The late king felt this fulfilled his ambition and therefore divided territory to grant me fiefdoms, allowing me to rank among the lesser feudal lords. I did not fully realize my own worth and assumed that by carrying out orders and following instruction, I would be lucky to remain blameless. Therefore, I accepted the appointment without refusal.

## 10

I have heard that a wise and virtuous ruler does not neglect those who have achieved great deeds, which is why their accomplishments are recorded in the annals of history. Those with foresight and wisdom achieve lasting fame without disgrace, hence they are honored by later generations. The late king avenged his grudges and washed away shame, subduing a powerful state with ten thousand chariots, seizing the accumulated wealth of eight hundred years. Even on the day he left us, his teachings had not faded; those who governed and carried out affairs followed laws and decrees carefully, guarded against potential threats from lesser branches of the royal family, and extended their care even to commoners and servants—all these actions could serve as lessons for future generations.

## 11

I have heard it said that one who begins a task well may not necessarily complete it successfully, and one who starts well may not always finish well. In the past, Wu Zixu's advice was heeded by Helü, and King Goujian of Yue pursued his vengeance to Ying. King Fuchai did not heed this, but instead gave him a leather container and had him drowned in the river. King Fuchai failed to realize that Wu Zixu's earlier arguments could have led to success, so he let him drown without regret. Wu Zixu did not foresee the difference in his ruler's character early enough; thus, he ended up being cast into the river without redemption.

## 12

To preserve one's life and achieve success while clarifying the legacy of the late king is a subject's highest strategy. To be subjected to disgraceful slander and damage the name of the late king—this is what I fear most. Facing an unpredictable punishment, hoping for profit from it would be something a man of virtue dares not do.

## 13

I have heard that in ancient times, gentlemen did not utter harsh words even when their friendships ended; a loyal minister who leaves his country does not seek to clear his name. Though I am not eloquent, I have often received instruction from gentlemen. I fear that those in attendance may be swayed by the words of your close advisors and fail to examine the actions of those who are distant. Therefore, I dare submit this letter for Your Majesty's consideration, hoping you will give it careful thought.

## 14

Thus, King Wanyan restored Yue Yi's son, Yue Jian, as Lord of Changguo; and Yue Yi traveled back and forth between Yan again, with both Yan and Zhao regarding him as a guest minister. Yue Yi died in Zhao.

## 15

Yue Jian lived in Yan for more than thirty years, when King Xi of Yan followed the plan proposed by his prime minister Li Fu and wished to attack Zhao. He consulted Lord Yue Jian of Changguo. Yue Jian said, "Zhao is a state constantly at war; its people are experienced in military affairs. It would be unwise to attack it." King Xi of Yan did not listen and proceeded with the attack on Zhao. Zhao sent Lin Po to fight against them, and at Hao, he decisively defeated Li Fu's army, capturing both Li Fu and Yue Cheng. Yue Cheng was a relative of Yue Jian. At this time, Yue Jian fled to Zhao, and Zhao then launched a siege on Yan. Yan was forced to cede territory in order to make peace with Zhao, who then lifted the siege and withdrew.

## 16

King Xi of Yan regretted not having heeded Yue Jian's advice. Since Yue Jian was now in Zhao, the king sent him a letter saying: "In the time of King Zhou, Ji Zi did not serve at court, yet continued to offer remonstrances without tiring, hoping that his words would be heard; Shang Rong was not appointed and suffered personal disgrace, yet he still hoped for a change in the ruler's ways. Only when the people's resolve could no longer be swayed and prisoners broke out of their cells did these two men finally withdraw into seclusion. Thus, although King Zhou bore the accumulated guilt of cruelty and tyranny, the two men did not lose their reputation for loyalty and virtue. Why is that? Because they had exhausted all efforts to relieve the people's suffering. Now, although I am foolish, I am not as tyrannical as King Zhou; the people of Yan may be in disorder, but not to the same extent as the people of Yin. If there are words spoken within a house that cannot be fully expressed, they should be conveyed to one's neighbors and fellow villagers. These two things, I will not allow my ministers to do for me."

## 17

Yue Jian and Yue Cheng resented the fact that Yan did not follow their advice, so they remained in Zhao until their deaths. Zhao granted Yue Cheng the title of Wu Xiang Jun.

## 18

The following year, Yue Cheng and Lian Po led Zhao in a siege against Yan; Yan offered generous gifts to make peace, and the siege was lifted. Five years later, King Xiao Cheng of Zhao died. King Xiang sent Yue Cheng to replace Lian Po. Lian Po attacked Yue Cheng, who fled; Lian Po then escaped and went into Wei. Sixteen years later, Qin destroyed Zhao.

## 19

More than twenty years later, Emperor Gaozu passed through Zhao and asked: "Does Yue Yi have any descendants?" The reply was: "There is a Yue Shu." Emperor Gaozu granted him the surname Yue Qing and bestowed upon him the title Hua Cheng Jun. Hua Cheng Jun was the grandson of Yue Yi. Among the Yue clan were Yue Xia Gong and Yue Chen Gong; as Zhao was about to be destroyed by Qin, they fled to Gaomi in Qi. Yue Chen Gong was skilled in studying the teachings of Huangdi and Laozi, gaining prominence in Qi and being regarded as a virtuous teacher.

## 20

Grand Historian said: At the beginning, when Qiu Tong of Qi and Zhu Fu Yan read Yue Yi's letter to King Xi of Yan, they never failed to set down their books and weep. Yue Chen Gong studied the teachings of Huangdi and Lao Zi; his original teacher was known as He Shang Zhangren, but no one knows where he came from. He Shang Zhangren taught An Qi Sheng, who in turn taught Mao Xi Gong; Mao Xi Gong taught Yue Xia Gong, who taught Yue Chen Gong, and Yue Chen Gong taught Gai Gong. Gai Gong taught in Gaomi and Jiaoxi of Qi, becoming the teacher of Chancellor Cao Can.
