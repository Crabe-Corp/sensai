# Biographies of Fan Jui and Cai Ze

## 1

Fan Jui was a native of Wei, courtesy name Shu. He traveled to persuade the feudal lords and wished to serve King Wei, but due to his poor family circumstances, he could not support himself. Therefore, he first served Xu Jia, a central official of Wei.

## 2

Xu Jia was sent by King Zhao of Wei to Qi, and Fan Jui followed him on the mission. They stayed for several months without receiving a reply. King Xiang of Qi, having heard of Jui's eloquence, sent someone to give him ten jin of gold and a cow with wine. Jui declined the gifts and dared not accept them. Xu Jia learned of this and became very angry, believing that Jui had revealed Wei's secret affairs to Qi, which was why he received these gifts. He ordered Jui to accept the cow and wine but return the gold. After returning, Xu Jia remained angry at Jui and reported this matter to the prime minister of Wei. The prime minister was a nobleman from the royal family of Wei named Wei Qi. Wei Qi became very angry and ordered his attendants to beat Jui with canes, breaking his ribs and knocking out some of his teeth. Jui pretended to be dead; they then wrapped him in a straw mat and placed him in the latrine. The guests who were drinking became drunk and urinated on Jui, thus humiliating and punishing him to serve as a warning for others not to speak recklessly. Jui called out from inside the straw mat to the guard, saying, "If you can get me out, I will certainly reward you handsomely." The guard then requested permission to remove and discard the dead body in the straw mat. Wei Qi was drunk and said, "Yes, you may do so." Fan Jui thus managed to escape. Later, Wei Qi regretted his actions and sent people out again to search for him. A native of Wei named Zheng Anping heard about this and so took Fan Jui away, hiding him in concealment and changing his name to Zhang Lu.

## 3

At that time, King Zhao of Qin sent a court official named Wang Ji on a mission to Wei. Zheng Anping pretended to be a soldier and served as an attendant for Wang Ji. Wang Ji asked, "Is there any talented person in Wei with whom I can travel westward together?" Zheng Anping said, "In my hometown, there is a Master Zhang Lu who wishes to meet you and speak about affairs of the world." "He has an enemy and dares not appear in daylight." Wang Ji said, "Then come to me at night." Zheng Anping met with Zhang Lu and introduced him to Wang Ji at night. Before their conversation was finished, Wang Ji realized that Fan Jui was a man of virtue and said to him, "Master, wait for me at the southern side of Santing." He made a private arrangement with him and then left.

## 4

Wang Ji bid farewell to Wei and departed, secretly taking Fan Jui along on the journey into Qin. When they reached Hu, they saw a procession of chariots and horsemen coming from the west. Fan Jui asked, "Who are those people coming over?" Wang Ji replied, "They are the prime minister of Qin, Mang Hou, returning eastward after touring his fief." Fan Jui said, "I have heard that Mang Hou monopolizes the power of Qin and dislikes foreign guests from other states. He might humiliate me if he sees me. I would rather hide in the carriage for now." 歷 After a short while, Mang Hou indeed arrived and greeted Wang Ji. He then stood by his chariot and asked, "What changes have occurred in the eastern passes?" Wang Ji replied, "Nothing has changed." Mang Hou then said to Wang Ji, "Did you perhaps bring any guests from the feudal states with you?" "They are of no benefit and only cause disorder in a country." Wang Ji replied, "I dare not do so." Then they parted ways. Fan Jui said, "I have heard that Mang Hou is a man of wisdom, but he was slow to notice. Earlier, he suspected there might be someone in the carriage and yet forgot to search it." At this, Fan Jui got out of the carriage and ran away, saying, "He must regret not searching now." After traveling more than ten li, they indeed sent riders back to search the carriage. Finding no guest there, they stopped their pursuit. Wang Ji then proceeded with Fan Jui into Xianyang.

## 5

After reporting on his mission, he added, "There is a Master Zhang Lu in Wei who is an eloquent man of the world. He said, 'The state of King Qin is more precarious than eggs stacked on top of each other; with my assistance, it will find peace. However, this cannot be conveyed through written words.'" That is why I brought him here." King Qin did not believe this and had him lodged in a place where only simple food was provided. He waited for an order for more than a year.

## 6

At that time, King Zhao had already been on the throne for thirty-six years. To the south, he captured Yanying of Chu; King Huai of Chu was confined and died in Qin. To the east, Qin defeated Qi. King Min had once declared himself emperor but later abandoned the title. He repeatedly subdued the three Jin states. King Qin was weary of eloquent men from around the world and trusted no one.

## 7

Mang Hou, Huayang Jun, were the brothers of Queen Dowager Xuan, King Zhao's mother; Jingyang Jun and Gaoling Jun were all King Zhao's full younger brothers. Mang Hou served as prime minister, while the other three alternated in commanding armies and held fiefs. Because of Queen Dowager's influence, their private families grew wealthier and more powerful than the royal household itself. When Mang Hou became a general of Qin, he planned to cross through Han and Wei to attack Qi's Ganglei, intending to expand his fief in Tao. Fan Jui then submitted a memorial stating:

## 8

I have heard that an enlightened ruler establishes governance: those who have rendered service must be rewarded, and those with ability must be given official positions. Those who have made great contributions receive generous salaries; those with many achievements are granted high ranks; and those capable of governing the people hold major offices. Therefore, the incompetent dare not assume office, and the competent cannot remain hidden. If Your Majesty deems my words acceptable, I wish to proceed in order to further benefit your cause; if you find them unacceptable, then keeping me here for a long time would be of no use. The saying goes: "An average ruler rewards those he loves and punishes those he dislikes; an enlightened ruler does not act this way. Rewards are given only to those who have rendered service, and punishments are meted out only to the guilty." Now, my chest is not broad enough to bear the punishment of a carpenter's tool, and my waist is not strong enough to endure the executioner's axe. How could I dare to test Your Majesty with uncertain matters! Even if you regard me as a lowly person and slight my disgrace, would not the one who entrusted me to Your Majesty be wronged by your lack of reconsideration?

## 9

Moreover, I have heard that Zhou had Dili, Song had Jielv, Liang had Xuanli, and Chu had Hepu. These four treasures were produced by the land but overlooked by skilled artisans; yet they became renowned artifacts of the world. If this is so, then what a sage king discards would it not still be sufficient to strengthen the state?

## 10

I have heard that those who wish to enrich their families take from the state, and those who wish to enrich the state take from the feudal lords. When there is a wise ruler in the world, the feudal lords cannot arbitrarily enrich themselves—why is this so? Because they are deprived of their glory. A skilled physician knows whether a patient will live or die, and an enlightened ruler understands the success or failure of affairs. If something is beneficial, he implements it; if harmful, he abandons it; if doubtful, he cautiously tests it. Even if Shun and Yu were to be reborn, they could not change this principle. The most profound words, I dare not record in writing; the shallow ones are insufficient to be heeded. Perhaps it is my own ignorance that prevents me from grasping Your Majesty's intentions? Or perhaps the one who recommended me is considered lowly and thus unworthy of trust? If not so, I humbly request a small favor: to be granted some leisure time for observation and the opportunity to glimpse Your Majesty's countenance. Should my words prove ineffective even once, I shall willingly submit to the punishment of the executioner's axe.

## 11

At this, King Zhao of Qin was greatly pleased and thus apologized to Wang Ji, ordering a state carriage to summon Fan Jui.

## 12

Thus, Fan Jui finally gained an audience at the palace pavilion and pretended not to know about Yongxiang, entering it by accident. When the king arrived, the eunuchs became angry and drove him out, shouting, "The king is here!" Fan Jui feigned confusion and said, "Where did Qin get a king? Qin has only the Queen Dowager and Mang Hou." He intended to provoke King Zhao's anger. King Zhao arrived, heard about the argument between Fan Jui and the eunuchs, and immediately went to greet him, apologizing and saying, "I should have personally received your instructions long ago. However, urgent matters concerning Yiqu prevented me from doing so; I had to request permission from the Queen Dowager day and night; Now that the affairs of Yiqu have been settled, I am finally able to receive your instructions. I feel ashamed for my lack of responsiveness and respectfully observe the proper etiquette between host and guest." Fan Jui declined with courtesy. On that day, those who witnessed Fan Jui's audience with the king were all startled and changed their expressions in awe.

## 13

King Qin dismissed his attendants; the palace was empty of people. King Qin knelt upright and asked respectfully, "Master, what wisdom do you offer to enlighten me?" Fan Jui replied, "Yes, yes." After a while, King Qin again knelt upright and asked, "Master, what wisdom do you have to enlighten me?" Fan Jui replied, "Yes, yes." This happened three times. King Qin knelt and said, "Is it that you finally have no words of advice for me?" Fan Jui replied, "I dare not do so. I have heard that in the past, when Lü Shang met King Wen, he was merely a fisherman casting his net by the banks of Wei. At that time, their relationship was distant. After he advised King Wen and was appointed as Taishi (Grand Tutor), and taken back with him in the carriage, it was because his words were profound. Thus, King Wen achieved success through Lü Shang and eventually became ruler of all under heaven. Had King Wen kept Lü Shang at a distance and not engaged in deep conversation with him, then the Zhou dynasty would have lacked the virtue of its Son of Heaven, and Kings Wen and Wu would have had no one to help them achieve their imperial cause. Now I am a wandering minister, distant in relationship to the King, yet what I wish to present are all matters of advising and assisting Your Majesty. These concerns touch upon intimate family affairs; I wish to offer my loyal but foolish counsel, yet I do not know the King's mind. This is why the King has asked three times and I have dared not respond. I am not afraid to speak out of fear. I know that if I speak today, I may be executed tomorrow, yet I dare not avoid it. If the Great King truly follows my words, death would not be a concern for me, nor would exile be a worry. Even if I were to smear pitch on my body and pretend madness by letting out my hair, it would not bring shame upon me. Moreover, even the sage Five Emperors died; the benevolent Three Kings died; the virtuous Five Hegemons died; Wu Huo and Ren Bi, men of great strength, died; Cheng Jing, Meng Ben, Wang Qingji, Xia Yu, men of valor, also died. Death is something no one can escape. If I am in a position where my death could offer some slight benefit to Qin, that is what I most desire. What else should I fear! Wu Zixu carried his belongings and fled through the Guanzhao Pass, traveling at night and hiding by day. He reached Lingshui River with no food to eat, crawling on his knees and belly, bowing repeatedly while bare-chested, beating his stomach and playing the chui flute, begging for food in Wu's market. In the end, he helped establish Wu State, and Helu became a hegemon. If I were allowed to offer all my strategies like Wu Zixu, and even if I were imprisoned in secret and never seen again for the rest of my life, it would mean that my advice was accepted. What else should I worry about? Jizi and Jieyu smeared pitch on their bodies, let out their hair, and pretended to be mad, but it brought no benefit to their rulers. If I could share the same fate as Jizi and thus offer some benefit to a virtuous ruler, that would be my greatest honor. What shame should I feel? What I fear most is that after my death, the world will see me as a man who gave his whole heart to loyalty yet died for it. Because of this, they may close their mouths and refrain from coming forward, no longer willing to face Qin. You, my lord, above fear the strictness of the Empress Dowager, and below are deceived by the flattery of corrupt ministers. Living deep within the palace, you never leave the hands of your attendants, remaining confused all your life without anyone to expose the villains. The worst case is that the ancestral temple will be destroyed and the state ruined; at best, you yourself will face isolation and danger. This is what I fear. As for disgraceful treatment or dangers of death, I dare not fear them. If my death brings order to Qin, then dying is better than living." King Qin knelt upright and said, "Master, what words are these! The state of Qin is remote and uncultivated, and I am foolish and unworthy. Master has condescended to come here in person; this must be Heaven's way of allowing me to trouble you while preserving the ancestral temple of our former kings. I have received your instruction, Master; this is Heaven's way of blessing our late kings and not abandoning their orphaned heir. How can you say such things, Master! No matter how small or great the matter may be, whether it concerns the Empress Dowager above or the ministers below, I wish you would teach me everything without hesitation. Do not doubt me." Fan Jui bowed, and King Qin also bowed.

## 14

Fan Jui said, "Your Majesty's state is surrounded by natural barriers for defense. To the north are Ganshan and Gu Kou; to the south lie Jing and Wei rivers. On the west are Long and Shu regions, while on the east stand the Guan and Ban passes. With a force of one million men and a thousand chariots, we can attack when advantageous or retreat for defense when not. This is the land of a Son of Heaven. The people are timid in private quarrels but brave in public battles; these are the subjects of a Son of Heaven." Your Majesty possesses both of these advantages. With the bravery of Qin's soldiers and the numerous chariots and cavalry, to manage the feudal lords is like releasing a Hanlu hound to chase a lame rabbit; the cause of hegemony or kingship can be achieved. Yet none among your ministers are worthy of their positions. Until now, for fifteen years the passes have remained closed and no one dares to look upon Qin's military forces from east of the mountains. This is because Rang Hou plotted disloyally for Qin, and Your Majesty made a miscalculation." King Qin knelt upright and said: "I wish to hear of my miscalculations."

## 15

However, many eunuchs and attendants were secretly listening. Fearing this, Fan Jui did not dare to speak of internal affairs first; instead, he spoke about external matters first in order to gauge King Qin's reaction. He then continued: "The Rang Hou bypassed the states of Han and Wei to attack Qi's Ganglei. This was not a wise strategy. A small expedition would be insufficient to harm Qi, while a large one would be detrimental to Qin itself. I suspect Your Majesty's plan is to send out only a small force while mobilizing all the troops of Han and Wei, which would be unjust. Now that we see these allied states are not loyal, is it acceptable to cross the territory of another state and launch an attack? This was a miscalculation. Moreover, in the past, King Mian of Qi launched a southern campaign against Chu, defeating their armies and killing their generals. He expanded his territory by thousands of li, yet not an inch of land was actually gained for Qi. Was it that he did not want to gain land? It was simply impossible under the circumstances. The feudal lords, seeing Qi's exhaustion and internal discord, raised their armies to attack Qi and utterly defeated it. Its soldiers were disgraced and its forces shattered; all blamed the king, saying: "Who was responsible for this plan?" The king replied, "It was done by Wenzǐ." The ministers then rebelled, and Wenzǐ fled. The reason Qi was so thoroughly defeated in its attack on the feudal lords was because its campaign against Chu had strengthened Han and Wei. This is what is called "borrowing weapons from thieves and providing food to bandits." Your Majesty should instead form distant alliances while attacking nearby states. Every inch gained is an inch for the King; every chi gained is a chi for the King. Now, to abandon this strategy and launch distant attacks—would that not be mistaken! Moreover, in the past there was the state of Zhongshan, with a territory of five hundred li. Zhao alone annexed it, achieving fame and success while gaining benefits; no one under heaven could harm them. Now, the states of Han and Wei are located at the center of China and serve as the pivot of all under heaven. If Your Majesty wishes to become a hegemon, you must cultivate close relations with these central states in order to control the pivot and thus intimidate Chu and Zhao. If Chu becomes strong, it will ally with Zhao; if Zhao becomes strong, it will ally with Chu. When both Chu and Zhao are allied, Qi must surely be afraid. When Qi is afraid, it will certainly use humble words and generous gifts to serve Qin. Once Qi submits, Han and Wei can then be easily subdued." King Zhao said: "I have long wished to cultivate close relations with Wei, but Wei is a fickle state; I cannot trust it. Please advise me—how can I establish closer ties with Wei?" Fan Jui replied: "Your Majesty should use humble words and generous gifts in your dealings with them; if that does not work, then offer to cede territory as a bribe; if still ineffective, then raise an army and attack them." The King said: "I have respectfully heard your instruction." He then appointed Fan Jui as Keqing (a high-ranking guest official) to plan military affairs. King Qin finally heeded Fan Jui's strategy and dispatched Duke Wan, a Five Great Ministers, to attack Wei, capturing Huai. Two years later, Xingqiu was captured.

## 16

The guest official Fan Jui again advised King Zhao: "The geographical positions of Qin and Han are interwoven like embroidery. For Qin to have Han as a neighbor is like a tree having woodworms, or a person suffering from an internal illness. If there are no changes in the world order, then it is fine; but if there are upheavals, who could be a greater threat to Qin than Han? Your Majesty would do better to bring Han under your control." King Zhao said: "I have long wanted to subdue Han, but Han does not listen. What should I do?" Fan Jui replied: "How could Han possibly refuse? If Your Majesty sends troops to attack Yingyang, then the roads between Gong and Chenggao will be cut off; blocking the northern Taihang Pass route would prevent Shangdang's forces from descending. If Your Majesty raises an army to attack Yingyang, Han's territory will be divided into three isolated parts. When Han sees that its state is doomed, how could it possibly refuse?" If Han complies, then the cause of hegemony can be realized." The King said: "Well spoken." He was about to send envoys to Han.

## 17

Fan Jui had grown increasingly close to the King and, after advising for several years, requested a private audience. He said: "When I lived east of the mountains, I heard of Tian Wen in Qi but not of its king; I heard of Qin's Empress Dowager, Rang Hou, Huayang, Gaoling, and Jingyang, but not of its King. "He who controls the state is called a king; he who can bring benefit or harm is called a king; and he who holds the power of life and death is called a king. Now, the Empress Dowager acts arbitrarily without regard; Rang Hou sends envoys abroad without reporting back. Huayang and Jingyang wield power recklessly without restraint, while Gaoling moves freely in and out of court without seeking permission. When four powerful figures hold such unchecked authority yet the state remains unthreatened, I have never heard of such a case. To be beneath these four influential men is to say there is no king at all. If this is the case, how can power not tilt away from you? How can commands still come from Your Majesty? I have heard that a wise ruler of the state strengthens his authority internally and consolidates power externally. Rang Hou's envoys wield the King's authority, making decisions among the feudal lords and issuing decrees to all under heaven. When he leads military campaigns against states, none dare refuse him. When battles are won and territories captured, the benefits go to Tao (Rang Hou), while the state suffers exhaustion from defending against the feudal lords; when defeated in battle, resentment is sown among the people and disaster falls upon the state. The Book of Songs says: "When a tree bears too much fruit, its branches break; when the branches break, the heart of the tree is wounded; when a capital grows too large, the state becomes endangered; and when ministers are overly honored, the ruler's authority is diminished." Cui Zhu and Nao Chi once controlled Qi. They shot the king in the thigh, pulled out his tendons, and hung him from a beam in the ancestral temple, where he died within a day or two. Li Dui controlled Zhao and imprisoned King Zhu of Zhao at Shachu, where the king starved to death after a hundred days. Now I have heard that the Empress Dowager and Rang Hou hold power in Qin, with Gaoling, Huayang, and Jingyang assisting them. In effect, there is no King of Qin—this is just like Nao Chi and Li Dui. Moreover, the reason why states fell in the Three Dynasties was because their kings delegated power entirely to others, indulged themselves in drinking and hunting, and neglected state affairs. Those to whom they entrusted power were envious of the virtuous and talented, oppressed their subordinates while concealing information from above, acting for their own private interests rather than for the ruler's benefit. The ruler failed to realize this, so he lost his state. Now, from officials of rank Zhi Shi and above down to the major ministers, as well as those close to the King, none are not Rang Hou's men. I see the King standing alone in court; I secretly fear for Your Majesty. After a thousand generations, if Qin still exists, its ruler will not be of your descendants." Zhaowang heard this and was greatly alarmed, saying: "Well said." Thus he deposed the Empress Dowager, expelled Rang Hou, Gaoling, Huayyang, and Jingyang Jun beyond the Guan Pass. The King of Qin then appointed Fan Jui as Chancellor. He took back Rang Hou's seal and ordered him to return to Tao, while also arranging for government officials to provide carts and oxen for his move, with more than a thousand vehicles. When he arrived at the Guan Pass, the customs officials examined his treasures and found that the number of rare and precious objects was greater than those in the royal palace.

## 18

Qin granted Fan Jui the fiefdom of Ying, bestowing upon him the title "Ying Hou." At that time, it was the forty-first year of King Zhaowang's reign in Qin.

## 19

After Fan Jui became Chancellor of Qin, he was known by the name Zhang Lu; Wei did not know this and believed that Fan Jui had been dead for a long time. When Wei heard that Qin was about to launch an eastern campaign against Han and Wei, it sent Xu Jia as envoy to Qin. Fan Jui heard of this and took a secret trip, dressed in tattered clothes and walking incognito to the Wei envoy's residence, where he met Xu Jia. Xu Jia saw him and was startled, saying: "Is it really you, Uncle Fan? You are well!" Fan Jui said, "Yes." Xu Jia laughed and asked: "Have you made a proposal to Qin?" He replied, "No." "Recently I committed an offense before the Prime Minister of Wei and thus fled here; how could I dare to make proposals?"!" Xu Jia asked, "Now then, what are you doing, Uncle?" Fan Jui said: "I am merely a hired laborer." Xu Jia felt compassion for him, invited him to sit together and share meals, saying: "Uncle Fan, how impoverished you are now!" He then took one of his silk robes and gave it to him. Xu Jia then asked: "Do you know the Qin Chancellor Zhang Jun?" I have heard that he is favored by the King, and all affairs of the world are decided by the Chancellor. Now whether my mission succeeds or fails depends on Zhang Jun. Could a poor wretch like me have any acquaintance who is familiar with the Chancellor?" Fan Jui said: "My master knows him well. I too may request an audience; I will arrange for you to meet the Chancellor." Xu Jia said: "My horse is sick, and my cart's axle is broken. Without a large carriage drawn by four horses, I will not go out." Fan Jui said: "I will borrow for you a large carriage and four horses from my master."

## 20

Fan Jui returned to obtain the large carriage with four horses, drove it for Xu Jia himself, and entered the Chancellor's residence in Qin. Those inside the mansion who saw them recognized him and all avoided or hid. Xu Jia found this strange. When they reached the Chancellor's gate, Fan Jui told Xu Jia: "Wait here; I will go in first to inform the Chancellor of your arrival." Xu Jia waited at the gate for a long time, holding his carriage there. He asked those under the gate: "Why hasn't Uncle Fan come out yet?" Those under the gate replied: "There is no one named Fan Shu here." Xu Jia said: "The man who entered with me just now." Those under the gate replied: "That was our Chancellor, Zhang Jun himself." Xu Jia was greatly startled and realized he had been deceived. He then prostrated himself bare-chested, crawled on his knees, and through the gate attendants apologized for his offense. At this time, Fan Jui arranged a grand reception with rich curtains and many attendants waiting in attendance before receiving him. Xu Jia kowtowed and declared himself guilty of death, saying: "I never expected that you could rise to such lofty positions in the heavens. I dare no longer read books from all under heaven, nor participate in any affairs of the world. I have committed crimes deserving to be boiled alive; I beg you to banish me to a remote land like Huhe, where my fate lies entirely in your hands!" Fan Jui asked: "What crimes have you committed?" Xu Jia replied: "If I plucked out all my hair to count my crimes, it still would not be enough." Fan Jui said: "You have committed only three crimes. In the time of King Zhaowang of Chu, Shen Baoxu repelled Wu's army for Chu. The King of Chu granted him 5000 households in Jing as a fief, but Baoxu declined it and instead requested only to have his tomb built there. Now my ancestors' tombs are also in Wei, yet you previously accused me of disloyalty to Qi and slandered me before Wei Qi—this is your first crime. When Wei Qi humiliated me in the latrine, you did not stop him—this is your second crime. He made me drunk and then urinated on me; how could you bear to watch this?" This is your third crime. "Yet you were spared death because of the silk robe I gave you, which showed lingering affection for an old friend; thus I forgave you." Then he dismissed Xu Jia with a formal apology. He then reported to King Zhaowang, who ordered Xu Jia's dismissal and return home.

## 21

Xu Jia bid farewell to Fan Jui, who prepared lavish provisions and invited all the envoys from the feudal lords to sit with them in the hall, providing an extravagant feast of food and drink. He seated Xu Jia beneath the hall, placed fodder and beans before him, and ordered two tattooed convicts to stand beside him while he ate like a horse. For several days, Fan Jui declared: "Tell the King of Wei for me—hurry and bring the head of Wei Qi!" Otherwise, I will massacre Daliang." Xu Jia returned and informed Wei Qi of this. Wei Qi was terrified, fled to Zhao. He took refuge at the residence of Pingyuan Jun.

## 22

After Fan Jui became chancellor, Wang Ji said to him: "There are three things that cannot be predicted, and also three things one can do nothing about. The sudden death of the King in his palace chariot is one thing that cannot be foreseen. Your sudden passing away and leaving this world is a second unforeseeable event. If I were to suddenly fall into a ditch or ravine, that would be the third thing beyond our knowledge. Should the King die unexpectedly in his chariot, even if you were to resent me, there would be nothing that could be done about it. Should you suddenly pass away and leave this world, even if I were to resent you, there would also be nothing that could be done. Should I suddenly fall into a ravine and die, even if you were to hate me, there would still be nothing that could be done." Fan Jui was displeased and thus reported to the King: "If not for Wang Ji's loyalty, no one could have brought me into Hangu Pass; and if not for Your Majesty's wisdom and virtue, I would never have been honored. Now my position has risen to that of Chancellor and my title is among the marquises, yet Wang Ji's rank remains only that of a Yeshi—this does not reflect his original intention in bringing me here." King Zhaowang summoned Wang Ji and appointed him Governor of Hedong, but for three years he did not submit the required annual reports. He also recommended Zheng Anping, who was appointed general by King Zhaowang. Fan Jui then distributed his family's wealth to repay those who had once helped him in times of hardship. Even a debt for one meal must be repaid, and even the resentment from a glance or glare must be settled.

## 23

In his second year as Chancellor of Qin, during the forty-second year of King Zhaowang's reign, Qin launched an eastern campaign against the Han strongholds Shaoqu and Gaoping, capturing both.

## 24

King Zhaowang of Qin learned that Wei Qi was in the residence of Pingyuan Jun, and wishing to help Fan Jui avenge his old grudge, he sent an elaborate letter to Pingyuan Jun saying: "I have heard of your noble virtue and wish to be friends with you as commoners. If you would deign to visit me, I hope we may drink together for ten days." Pingyuan Jun feared Qin and believed the offer sincere, so he entered Qin to meet King Zhaowang. King Zhaowang drank with Pingyuan Jun for several days. Then King Zhaowang said to Pingyuan Jun: "In the past, King Wen of Zhou gained Lu Shang and made him Tai Gong; Duke Huan of Qi gained Guan Yiwu and made him Zhong Fu. Now Fan Jui is also like an uncle to me. The enemy of my Chancellor resides in your household; I wish for you to send someone back and bring his head here; otherwise, I will not let you pass through the Guan Pass." Pingyuan Jun said: "Those who form friendships with high-ranking individuals do so for lowly reasons; those who make friends when they are wealthy do so to help the poor. Wei Qi is my friend; if he were here, I would not let him go. But now he is no longer in my household." King Zhaowang then sent a letter to King Zhao, saying: "Your Majesty's brother is in Qin. The enemy of Chancellor Fan Jui, Wei Qi, resides at Pingyuan Jun's household. Send someone quickly to bring his head here; otherwise, I will raise an army and attack Zhao, and also prevent Your Majesty's brother from leaving the Guan Pass." King Xiaocheng of Zhao then dispatched soldiers to surround Pingyuan Jun's residence. In the urgency, Wei Qi fled at night and sought out Yu Qing, Chancellor of Zhao. Yu Qing judged that King Zhao would never be persuaded, so he resigned his chancellor's seal and fled with Wei Qi. They traveled incognito, but finding no feudal lord who could offer immediate refuge, they returned to Daliang, hoping to seek asylum in Chu through Xinling Jun. Xinling Jun heard of this and, fearing Qin, hesitated to meet them, asking: "What kind of man is Yu Qing?" At that time Hou Ying was nearby and said: "People are indeed not easy to understand, and understanding people is also no simple matter. Yu Qing, with straw sandals on his feet and an umbrella in hand, had only one audience with King Zhao and was bestowed a pair of white jade disks and 100 jin of gold; after another meeting, he was appointed as an Upper Minister. After a third audience, he finally received the chancellor's seal and was enfeoffed as a marquis with ten thousand households under his fief. At that time, people throughout the land vied to know him. Now Wei Qi is in dire straits and has passed through Yu Qing's territory, yet Yu Qing dares not to value the dignity of his high rank and official salary; he resigns as chancellor, abandons his marquis title over ten thousand households, and travels incognito. He is hastening to assist a man in dire need by returning to you, yet you ask, 'What kind of person is he?' "People are indeed not easy to know; knowing people is also no simple task!" Xinling Jun was deeply ashamed and drove out into the wilderness to meet him. Wei Qi, having heard of Xinling Jun's initial reluctance to see him, became furious and committed suicide by cutting his throat. King Zhao of Zhao, upon hearing this, finally took Wei Qi's head and sent it to Qin. King Zhaowang of Qin then released Pingyuan Jun and allowed him to return to Zhao.

## 25

In the thirteenth year of King Zhaowang's reign, Qin attacked Fanxing in the state of Yan and captured it. They then built a city at Guangwu on the Yellow River.

## 26

Five years later, King Zhaowang employed a strategy proposed by Yinghou, sending spies to sow discord in Zhao. Because of this, Zhao appointed Ma Fu Zi to replace Lian Po as general. Qin heavily defeated Zhao at Changping and then proceeded to besiege Handan. Later, he fell out with General Wu'an Jun Bai Qi and had him executed on a charge. He appointed Zheng Anping to lead an attack against Zhao. Zheng Anping was surrounded by Zhao's forces and, in desperation, surrendered with his 20,000 troops to Zhao. Yinghou knelt on straw mats and pleaded for punishment. According to Qin law, if a person recommended someone who proved untrustworthy or incompetent, both the recommender and the recommendee would be punished according to their respective crimes. Therefore, Yinghou's crime warranted the punishment of executing three generations of his family. King Zhaowang feared that this would harm Yinghou's feelings, so he issued a decree throughout the state: "Anyone who dares to speak of Zheng Anping's matter shall be punished according to his crime." Yet he increased the food and gifts given daily to Chancellor Yinghou, in order to appease him. Two years later, Wang Ji, who was the governor of Hedong, conspired with feudal lords and was executed for breaking the law. As a result, Yinghou became increasingly discontented.

## 27

King Zhaowang sighed while presiding over court, and Yinghou stepped forward and said: "I have heard that 'When the ruler is worried, his ministers are shamed; when the ruler is humiliated, his ministers should die.'" Now Your Majesty appears in court and yet feels sorrow. I dare to ask for my punishment." King Zhaowang said: "I have heard that the iron swords of Chu are sharp, but their musicians and entertainers are clumsy. If the swords are sharp, then the soldiers will be brave; if the musicians and entertainers are unskilled, it means their thoughts and strategies are far-reaching. To govern brave warriors with deep strategic thinking, I fear that Chu may be plotting against Qin. If things are not prepared in advance, one cannot respond to sudden emergencies. Now that General Wu'an Jun has died and Zheng Anping has defected, we have no capable generals within the state while many hostile states surround us on the outside—this is why I am worried." He wanted to inspire Yinghou. Yinghou was frightened and did not know what course of action to take. Cai Ze heard about this, and went to enter Qin.

## 28

Cai Ze was a native of Yan. He traveled and studied, seeking audiences with many feudal lords both great and small, but found no opportunity for advancement. He once followed Tang Ju to have his fortune read, and asked: "I heard that Master, you told Li Dui, 'Within a hundred days, you will hold the reins of state power.' Is this true?" Tang Ju said: "Yes." Cai Ze asked: "And what of me?" Tang Ju looked at him carefully and laughed, saying: "Master, you have a flat nose, broad shoulders, a protruding forehead, a sunken mouth, and bent knees. I have heard that sages cannot be judged by appearance—perhaps you are such a person?" Cai Ze knew that Tang Ju was teasing him, so he said: "Wealth and rank are things I already possess. What I do not know is my lifespan; I would like to hear your judgment on that." Tang Ju said: "Master's life span, from now onward, will be forty-three years." Cai Ze smiled and thanked him, then left. He said to his charioteer: "I will chew on steamed millet with my teeth full of fat, ride a galloping horse at great speed, carry the golden seal in my bosom, fasten the purple sash around my waist, bow before rulers, and enjoy meat and wealth—forty-three years is more than enough." He went to Zhao but was driven away. When he went to Han and Wei, he encountered a theft of his cooking pots on the road. He heard that Yinghou had been involved in serious crimes through his appointments of Zheng Anping and Wang Ji, which caused him inner shame. Hearing this, Cai Ze went westward to enter Qin.

## 29

Before meeting King Zhaowang, he had someone make a public declaration to provoke Yinghou's anger: "The Yan guest Cai Ze is an outstanding and eloquent wise man of the world. Once he meets King Qin, the king will surely trouble you and take your position." Yinghou heard this and said: "I know the affairs of the Five Emperors and Three Sage Kings, as well as all schools of thought. I have refuted every argument made by many tongues—how could such a man possibly trouble me or take my position?" He then sent someone to summon Cai Ze. When Cai Ze entered, he bowed respectfully to Yinghou. Yinghou was already displeased, and when he saw Cai Ze's arrogant manner, he rebuked him, saying: "Have you ever publicly declared that you wished to replace me as chancellor of Qin?" Cai Ze replied: "Yes." Yinghou said: "Then please explain your reasoning." Cai Ze said: "Ah, how late you are in recognizing this! The order of the four seasons is such that when a task is completed, those who have succeeded must step aside. When a man is born with strong limbs, nimble hands and feet, sharp eyes and ears, and a wise mind, isn't this what scholars desire?" Yinghou said: "Yes." Cai Ze said: "If one possesses benevolence and upholds righteousness, practices the Way and spreads virtue, achieves his ambitions in the world, and is loved, respected, and revered by all under heaven, who wish to make him their ruler—would this not be the goal of wisdom and eloquence?" Yinghou said: "Yes." Cai Ze continued, saying: "Wealth and honor, prominence and glory—when one governs all things well, enabling each to find its proper place; a long life in which one lives out the full span of heaven's allotment without premature death or injury; the world continues his lineage, upholds his achievements, and transmits them endlessly; a name and reality of pure virtue, whose blessings extend for a thousand miles, being praised by generations without end, lasting from the beginning to the end of heaven and earth—would this not be in harmony with morality and what sages call an auspicious good fortune?" Yinghou said: "Yes."

## 30

Cai Ze said: "As for Qin's Shang Jun, Chu's Wu Qi, and Yue's Daifu Zhong, would their fates be something one could wish for?" Yinghou realized that Cai Ze was trying to trap him with his arguments, so he replied evasively: "Why not?" "Regarding Gongsun Yang's service to King Xiao of Qin, he devoted himself entirely without a second thought for his own interests, giving completely to the public good and disregarding personal gain; he established strict punishments with the threat of execution or amputation to deter corruption and evil, and ensured rewards and penalties were credible in order to achieve good governance; he laid bare his loyalty, revealed sincerity and purity of heart, bore resentment and blame, betrayed old friends, captured Wei Gongzi Yang, secured the safety of Qin's state, benefited its people, and ultimately led Qin to capture enemy generals, defeat their armies, and expand territory by thousands of li. Wu Qi's service to King Dao of Chu ensured that private interests could not harm the public good, and slander could not obscure loyalty. His words did not seek mere agreement, nor his actions mere acceptance; he did not change his conduct in times of danger, and upheld righteousness even when it brought hardship. Thus, he became a dominant ruler who strengthened his state without shying away from misfortune or peril. Daifu Zhong's service to King Goujian of Yue was such that even when the ruler faced hardship and humiliation, he remained completely loyal without wavering; even if the king were to perish or be lost, he would not abandon his duties but give all his abilities. He achieved success without pride, and despite wealth and rank, did not become arrogant or lazy. These three men were the very embodiment of righteousness and loyalty at its highest level. Therefore, a gentleman dies for righteousness in times of hardship, regarding death as returning home; To live in disgrace is worse than to die with honor. A scholar may indeed sacrifice his life for the sake of a good reputation; if it is where righteousness lies, even death brings no regret. What could possibly be wrong with that?"

## 31

Cai Ze said: "A wise ruler and virtuous ministers are the greatest blessings of the world; a discerning lord and upright ministers bring fortune to a state; a kind father and filial son, a faithful husband and chaste wife—these are the blessings of a family. Therefore, Bi Gan was loyal but could not save Yin; Wu Zixu was wise but could not preserve Wu; Shen Sheng was filial yet the state of Jin fell into chaos. These were all cases where there were loyal ministers and filial sons, yet their states still perished or fell into disorder—why is this so? It was because there were no wise rulers or virtuous fathers to listen to them. Hence, the world regarded their lords and fathers as dishonored while feeling pity for their ministers and sons. Now Shang Jun, Wu Qi, and Daifu Zhong were all such ministers; their rulers, however, were not. Therefore, the world speaks of these three men achieving great accomplishments but receiving no virtue in return—was it not because they died without meeting a worthy ruler? If one must wait until death to establish loyalty and gain fame, then Wei Zi would not be considered benevolent enough, Confucius not virtuous enough as a sage, and Guan Zhong not great enough. When people establish achievements, would they not hope for them to be completed in full? Those who preserve both their lives and reputations are the best. Those whose names may be emulated but who die, come next. Those whose reputations are disgraced while their lives remain intact are the lowest." At this, Ying Hou praised it as excellent.

## 32

Cai Ze, having a moment of leisure, said: "The loyalty and accomplishments achieved by Shang Jun, Wu Qi and Daifu Zhong are admirable indeed. Hong Yao served King Wen, and the Duke of Zhou assisted King Cheng—were they not also loyal and sage-like? In terms of the relationship between ruler and minister, which is more admirable: Shang Jun, Wu Qi Daifu Zhong or Hong Yao and the Duke of Zhou?" Ying Hou said: "Shang Jun, Wu Qi, Daifu Zhong cannot compare to them." Cai Ze said: "Then, in terms of a ruler's kindness and benevolence toward loyal ministers, his sincerity and loyalty to old friends, and how virtuous and wise men bond with him as glue and lacquer, without betraying meritorious subjects—how does your lord compare to Duke Xiao of Qin, King Dao of Chu, or the King of Yue?" Ying Hou said: "I do not know how they might compare." Cai Ze said: "Now, your lord's closeness to loyal ministers is no greater than Duke Xiao of Qin, King Dao Wang of Chu, or the king of Yue. If you apply your wisdom and ability to ensure your ruler's safety, rectify governance, manage disorder, strengthen the military, eliminate dangers, expand territory, increase grain production, enrich the state, provide for families, empower the sovereign, honor the ancestral temple, and make the world dare not defy or violate his authority—so that the ruler's power shakes the realm, achievements are recognized beyond ten thousand li, and your name shines through a thousand generations—who among you compares to Shang Jun, Wu Qi or Daifu Zhong?" Ying Hou said: "I cannot compare." Cai Ze said: "Now, your ruler's closeness to loyal subjects and remembrance of old friends is less than Duke Xiao of Qin, the King Dao Wang, or Gou Jian. Moreover, your achievements, love, trust, and favor are also not comparable to Shang Jun, Wu Qi nor Daifu Zhong. Yet your salary, rank, and wealth in private life surpass those three men. If you do not retire from power, I fear that the dangers you face will be even greater than theirs. I secretly regard this as a grave risk for you." The saying goes: "When the sun reaches its zenith, it moves on; when the moon is full, it wanes." When things reach their peak, they decline—this is a constant law of heaven and earth. Advancing or retreating, expanding or contracting in accordance with the times and changing accordingly—is this not the way of a sage? Therefore, "When a state is governed justly, one serves; when it is unjust, one withdraws." Sages say: "A flying dragon in the sky—favorable to meet a great man." "Unjust wealth and rank are as fleeting as floating clouds to me." Now, your grievances have been avenged and your virtues rewarded; you have achieved all that you desired. Yet you remain unchanged in plan—I secretly think this is unwise for you. Moreover, the green parrot, the heron, the rhinoceros and the elephant are not in positions where they are close to death; yet they die because they are deceived by bait. Su Qin and Zhi Bo were not lacking in wisdom to avoid disgrace or death; yet they died because their greed for profit led them on without restraint. Therefore, sages establish rites to restrain desires, take from the people in moderation, govern them at appropriate times, and use resources with limits. Thus their aspirations do not overflow, their conduct does not become arrogant, they remain aligned with the Way without deviation—hence the world supports them unceasingly. In ancient times, Duke Huan of Qi convened the feudal lords nine times and once stabilized the realm. However, at the Kuichiu assembly, he displayed arrogance and pride, leading to rebellion by nine states. King Fuchai of Wu had an army unmatched in the world, and his bravery and strength led him to look down upon the feudal lords, defying Qi and Jin. Thus he ended up with death and the destruction of his state. Xia Yu and Taishi Jiao could terrify entire armies with their shouts, yet they themselves died at the hands of ordinary men. All these figures reached the height of power but failed to return to reason and righteousness, avoiding the dangers of humility, retreat, and frugality. Shang Jun established clear laws and regulations for Duke Xiao of Qin, suppressed the roots of corruption, ensured that ranks were granted only with merit, and punishments given where there was guilt. He standardized weights and measures, adjusted taxation, broke up land boundaries, stabilized the livelihoods of common people, unified customs, encouraged farming to benefit the soil, ensured each household had a single occupation, promoted agricultural production for storage, and trained in military formations. Thus, when Qin mobilized its troops, it expanded territory; when at rest, the state grew wealthy. Hence, Qin was unmatched under heaven, established authority over feudal lords, and laid the foundation of the State of Qin's achievements. His accomplishments were complete, yet he ended up being torn apart by chariots. The State of Chu controlled thousands of square li, with a million men armed with halberds. Bai Qi led an army of tens of thousands to fight against Chu; in one battle he captured Yan and Ying and burned Yiling, and in another campaign he annexed the southern regions of Shu and Han. He then crossed through the states of Han and Wei to attack the powerful Zhao, encircling its forces at the northern battlefield of Machifu, killing or slaughtering over 400,000 troops in Changping. Blood flowed like rivers, and the sound of slaughter roared like thunder, leading to a siege on Handan, which enabled Qin to establish imperial ambitions. Chu and Zhao were the strongest states in the world and Qin's most formidable enemies. After this, both Chu and Zhao submitted in fear and dared not attack Qin—this was due to Bai Qi's power. He personally subdued more than seventy walled cities; his achievements were complete, yet he was ordered to commit suicide with a sword at Duyou. Wu Qi established laws for King Dao of Chu. He reduced the power and prestige of high-ranking ministers, dismissed the incompetent, abolished unnecessary officials, curbed private petitions, unified the customs of the state of Chu, restricted itinerant civilians, strengthened soldiers skilled in farming and warfare, expanded southward to subdue Yangyue, annexed Chen and Cai in the north, broke up horizontal alliances and scattered vertical ones, leaving traveling orators with no opportunity to speak. He suppressed factionalism to encourage the people, stabilized governance in Chu, made its military feared throughout the world, and brought feudal lords under submission. His achievements were complete, yet ultimately he was dismembered. Daifu Zhong devised long-term strategies for King Goujian of Yue, rescuing the state from the crisis at Kuaiji. He transformed a fallen state into one reborn and turned disgrace into glory. He cultivated wastelands to build cities, expanded territory and increased grain production, led men from all directions, unified efforts from top to bottom, assisted the virtuous Goujian, avenged Fuchai of Wu, and ultimately captured the powerful State of Wu. He made Yue a dominant power. His achievements were evident and trustworthy, yet Goujian ultimately betrayed him and had him killed. These four men achieved great success but failed to retire from power; thus misfortune befell them. This is what is meant by being faithful yet unable to bend, going forward yet unable to return. Fan Li understood this and withdrew from the world with ease, becoming known for life as Tao Zhu Gong. Have you not observed gamblers? Some wish to make a big gamble, others prefer to divide the gains—this is something your lord clearly understands. Now, as your lord serves as prime minister of Qin, he plans without leaving his seat and devises strategies within the palace halls. He sits in control over feudal lords, extends benefits to the Three Rivers region, strengthens Yiyang, conquers the perilous Yangchang Pass, blocks the Taihang Road, cuts off the routes of Fan and Zhonghang, preventing the six states from forming a vertical alliance. He constructs 栈道 (stacked roads) for thousands of li, connecting Shu and Han, making all under heaven fear Qin. Qin's ambitions have been fulfilled; your achievements are at their peak—this is precisely when Qin should divide its gains. If you do not step back now, then you will be no different from Shang Jun, Bai Gong, Wu Qi and Daifu Zhong. I have heard it said: "Those who look into water see their reflection; those who observe others understand fortune and misfortune." The Book says, "Under great success, one cannot remain for long." Disaster befell those four men—where will you stand, my lord? Why does your lord not return the seal of prime minister at this time, yield to a worthy man and pass it on, withdraw from power to live in seclusion by rivers and mountains? You would surely possess the integrity of Boyi and remain forever Ying Hou. To be honored as a solitary sage through generations, to possess the humility of Xu You and Ji Zisi of Yanling, and enjoy the longevity of Qiaosong—how does this compare with ending in disaster? Where then will you stand, my lord?" If you lack the resolve to distance yourself and cannot make a decisive choice, disaster like that of those four men is inevitable. The Yijing says, "A dragon at the zenith will have regrets." This means one who rises to the top but cannot descend, remains faithful yet cannot bend, and advances without being able to return. I hope you carefully consider this!" Ying Hou said: "Well said. I have heard, "Desire without knowing when to stop leads to losing the very thing one desires; possession without knowing contentment results in losing what one possesses." Your kind instruction is appreciated, Master Fan Jui respectfully accepts the command.' At this, he invited Cai Ze in to sit and treated him as a distinguished guest.

## 33

A few days later, he entered court and said to King Zhao of Qin: "There is a guest who has recently arrived from the east of Mount Tai named Cai Ze. He is an eloquent man, well-versed in the affairs of the Three Kings, the achievements of the Five Hegemons, and changes in worldly customs. He is capable enough to be entrusted with the governance of Qin. I have met many people, none can match him; I am not his equal." With your permission, I dare to report this." King Zhao of Qin summoned him for an audience. After speaking with him, he was greatly pleased and appointed Cai Ze as a guest high official. Ying Hou then feigned illness and requested to return the seal of prime ministership. King Zhao strongly urged Ying Hou to remain, but Ying Hou persisted in claiming illness and finally retired. Fan Jui was relieved of his prime ministership; King Zhao, now favoring Cai Ze's strategies, appointed him as the new prime minister of Qin and in the east he captured the Zhou royal house.

## 34

After serving as prime minister of Qin for several months, Cai Ze was criticized by some and feared punishment. He therefore claimed illness and returned the seal of office, being known thereafter as Gangcheng Jun. He remained in Qin for more than ten years, serving King Zhao, King Xiaowen and King Zhuangxiang. He ultimately served the First Emperor of Qin. As an envoy to Yan, after three years, Yan sent Crown Prince Dan as a hostage to Qin.

## 35

Grand Historian said: Han Feizi remarked, "Long sleeves are good for dancing; much money is good for trade." Indeed, these words are true! Fan Jui and Cai Ze are what the world calls versatile orators. Yet there were those who traveled from state to state, persuading feudal lords until old age without finding an opportunity—not because their strategies were poor, but because they lacked sufficient influence in persuasion. When these two men arrived as foreign travelers to Qin, they succeeded one after another in attaining high ministerial positions and left their achievements known throughout the world. This was indeed due to differences in strength and opportunity. Yet there are also cases of chance encounters for scholars; many virtuous men, like these two, failed to fully realize their ambitions—how could such stories be exhausted in recounting! Yet without hardship and adversity, how could the two men have been inspired?
