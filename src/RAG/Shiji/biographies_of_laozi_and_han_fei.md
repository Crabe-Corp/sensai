# Biographies of Laozi and Han Fei

## 1

Laozi was a native of Qu Renli, Lixiang, Kuxian, Chu. His surname was Li, his given name Er, and his courtesy name Rong; he served as the historian in charge of the Zhou dynasty's Shoucangshi archives.

## 2

Confucius traveled to the state of Zhou and was about to inquire about rites from Laozi. Laozi said, "What you are talking about, both the person and his bones have long since decayed; only his words remain. Moreover, when a man of virtue meets an auspicious time, he takes the reins and acts; but when he does not meet such a time, he travels about like a tumbleweed. I have heard that a good merchant conceals his wares as if they were nonexistent, and a man of virtue with great moral excellence appears foolish in manner. Get rid of your arrogance and excessive desires, your haughty expressions and lustful ambitions; all these are detrimental to you. This is all I have to tell you." Confucius left and said to his disciples, "Birds—I know they can fly; fish—I know they can swim; beasts—I know they can run. Those that run may be caught in nets, those that swim may be ensnared with lines, and those that fly may be shot down by arrows. As for the dragon, I cannot know how it rides on wind and clouds to ascend into heaven. I saw Laozi today, and he is like a dragon!"

## 3

Laozi cultivated morality and the Dao; his teachings emphasized self-effacement and remaining unknown. After staying in Zhou for a long time, Laozi observed its decline and thus decided to leave. When he reached the Guan Pass, Guan Ling Yin Xi said, "You are about to retire into seclusion; please write a book for me." Thus Laozi composed the upper and lower chapters of his book, consisting of more than 5,000 words expounding the meaning of Dao and De, then departed; no one knows where he finally ended up.

## 4

Some say: Lao Laizi was also a native of Chu, and he composed fifteen chapters discussing the applications of Daoist thought; it is said that he lived at the same time as Confucius.

## 5

It is said that Laozi lived to be over 160 years old, or even more than 200; this was because he cultivated the Dao and thus prolonged his life.

## 6

One hundred and twenty-nine years after the death of Confucius, according to historical records, Zhou Taishi Dan appeared before Duke Xian of Qin and said: "Initially, Qin and Zhou were united; after five hundred years they would separate, and seventy years later a hegemon or emperor would emerge." Some say Dan was Laozi himself; others say he was not. The world does not know which is true. Laozi was a reclusive man of virtue.

## 7

Laozi's son was named Zong; Zong became a general of the state of Wei and was enfeoffed at Duangan. Zong's son was Zhu, whose son was Gong; Gong's great-grandson was Jia, who served under Emperor Xiaowen of the Han dynasty. Jia's son Jie became the Tai Fu (Grand Tutor) to Wang Ang, King of Jiaoxi; thus his family settled in Qi.

## 8

Those in later generations who studied Laozi tended to look down on Confucian learning, while Confucian scholars also looked down on Laozi. "The Dao of those who differ cannot be shared in planning"—was this not referring to this? Li Er practiced non-action and allowed things to transform naturally, maintaining tranquility and purity so that order arose by itself.

## 9

Zhuangzi was a native of Meng; his name was Zhou. Zhou once served as an official in charge of the lacquer garden at Meng, and lived during the time of King Hui of Liang and King Xuan of Qi. His learning touched upon all fields, yet its fundamental essence ultimately returned to the teachings of Laozi. Therefore, his writings totaled more than 100,000 characters, most of which were largely composed of allegorical stories. He wrote "The Old Fisherman," "Dazhi," and "Opening the Chest," to satirize Confucius's disciples and thereby clarify Laozi's teachings. Figures such as Weilv Xu and Kangsang Zi were all empty words without factual basis. Nevertheless, he was skilled in composing writings and using elaborate rhetoric; by pointing out events and drawing analogies from human nature, he criticized Confucianism and Mohism so effectively that even the most learned scholars of his time could not defend themselves or escape criticism. His words were expansive, unrestrained, and self-indulgent, thus even kings and high officials could not regard him as a person of value.

## 10

King Wei of Chu heard that Zhuang Zhou was virtuous, so he sent envoys with generous gifts to invite him, offering the position of prime minister. Zhuang Zhou laughed and said to the envoy from Chu: "A thousand gold pieces is a great profit; the position of noble minister is an exalted rank. Have you not seen the sacrificial oxen for suburban rites? They are raised and fed for several years, dressed in embroidered garments, then led into the great temple. At that time, even if they wished to be a small piglet, could they possibly do so? You had better leave quickly and not defile me. I would rather play freely in the ditches, enjoying myself, than be bound by those who rule a state. I will never hold office for life, so that my desires may remain untroubled."

## 11

Shen Buhai was a native of Jing; he had been a low-ranking official in the former state of Zheng. He studied legalist doctrines and sought an audience with King Zhao Hou of Han, who appointed him as prime minister. Internally he reformed government and education; externally he responded to the feudal lords—for fifteen years. During Shenzi's lifetime, the state was well governed and its military strong, so no one dared to invade Han. Shenzi's learning originated from Huang-Lao thought but emphasized legalism and the doctrine of names and functions. He composed two chapters, known as "Shenzi."

## 12

Han Fei was a member of the royal family of Han. He favored the study of legalism, names and functions, yet ultimately traced its foundation back to Huang-Lao thought. Han Fei had a speech impediment; he could not speak well orally, but was skilled at writing books. He and Li Si both studied under Xunzi, and Li Si himself considered himself inferior to Han Fei.

## 13

Han Fei observed the weakening of Han and repeatedly advised King of Han in writing, but the king did not heed his advice. Therefore, Han Fei criticized the governance of states for not focusing on clarifying and improving their legal systems, holding power firmly to control subordinates, enriching the country and strengthening its military forces through reliance on capable individuals. Instead, rulers wrongly promoted corrupt and frivolous officials over those who had actually contributed to national achievements. He believed that Confucian scholars used rhetoric to confuse the law, while chivalrous individuals used violence to violate prohibitions. In times of leniency, rulers favored those with reputations for virtue; in times of urgency, they relied on armored warriors. Nowadays, what is nurtured is not what is used, and what is used is not what has been nurtured. Saddened by the fact that upright and incorruptible men could not find a place in a court dominated by corrupt and crooked ministers, he observed the gains and losses of past events. Thus, he composed works such as "Gufen," "Wudu," "Neiwaichu," "Shuolin," and "Shunan," totaling more than 100, words.

## 14

However, Han Fei understood the difficulty of persuasion; he wrote an extensive treatise on this subject in his "Shunan" chapter. In the end, he died in Qin and could not free himself from his predicament.

## 15

The "Shunan" chapter says:

## 16

The difficulty in persuasion does not lie in my lack of knowledge or ability to persuade, but rather in the following challenges: nor is it because I am unable to argue clearly and express my meaning effectively; nor does it lie in my hesitation or fear of making bold statements that prevent me from fully expressing myself. The difficulty in persuasion lies in understanding the mind of the person being persuaded, so as to determine whether my arguments are appropriate for them.

## 17

If the person being persuaded is one who seeks high reputation, yet you try to persuade them with promises of great profit, they will see your approach as lowly and vulgar, and certainly reject it. If the person being persuaded values material gain, and you try to persuade them by appealing to reputation and honor, they will regard your words as insincere and detached from reality, and certainly not accept them. If the person being persuaded actually seeks great profit but outwardly claims to value reputation and honor, yet you attempt to persuade them by emphasizing reputation and honor, then they will appear to accept your words while in reality distancing themselves from you; if instead you try to persuade them through promises of profit, they may secretly use your advice but publicly discard and reject you. This is something that cannot be overlooked.

## 18

Matters succeed through secrecy, and fail when spoken of openly. It may not necessarily be the person himself who reveals a secret; if what is said touches upon matters that are supposed to remain hidden, then in such cases one's own life will be endangered. If a powerful person has committed an error and the speaker openly criticizes or clearly discusses their faults, then one's own life is in danger. If the speaker has not yet received sufficient favor from a ruler and speaks with excessive knowledge or insight, then if his advice is followed and achieves success, he will be seen as having no virtue; if it fails to produce results, he will face suspicion. In such cases, one's life is in danger. If a powerful person devises a plan and wishes to take credit for it as his own achievement, yet the speaker is known to have shared in that knowledge, then one's life will be in danger. If a powerful person publicly takes credit for something he did not do and claims it as his own accomplishment, yet the speaker is known to possess knowledge of this matter, then one's life will also be in danger. Forcing someone to do something they absolutely refuse to do, or preventing them from doing something they cannot help but do—this puts one's life in danger. Therefore it is said: If you discuss matters with a powerful person, he will think you are interfering in his affairs; if you speak of minor officials or common people, he will believe you are trying to manipulate authority. If you discuss what they love, they will think you are seeking favor; if you mention what they hate, they will believe you are testing them. If your words are direct and concise, the person may not understand and thus reject or suppress you; if your speech is overly verbose and filled with erudition, it will be seen as excessive and take too long. If you present your ideas in a smooth and orderly manner, they may say you are timid and not fully committed; if you think broadly and speak freely about matters, they will call you coarse and disrespectful. These are the difficulties of persuasion; one cannot afford to be unaware of them.

## 19

The essential task in persuasion lies in knowing how to flatter what the person being persuaded respects, while concealing or downplaying what they find shameful. If they are aware of their own plans, do not use their mistakes to corner them; if they take pride in their decisions, do not provoke their anger by opposing them with their adversaries' arguments; if they overestimate their own abilities, do not overwhelm them with difficult tasks or challenges. If you compare different matters to similar plans, and praise other people who share the same actions as them, this kind of flattery will not harm them. If there are others who have made the same mistakes, clearly emphasize that they themselves have committed no errors. The greatest loyalty does not provoke or enlighten forcefully; words and arguments do not attack or oppose directly—only then can one gradually present their reasoning and wisdom. This is why it is so difficult to gain the trust of others without suspicion, and to fully convey one's knowledge. Only after a long period of time, when the ruler's favor has become deep and enduring, when one can make far-reaching plans without being doubted, and when rivalries with others do not lead to punishment—only then should one clearly analyze benefits and harms in order to achieve success, directly point out right and wrong to protect oneself. With such a foundation, persuasion will succeed.

## 20

Yi Yin was once a cook, and Bai Li Xi was once a captive—both of them used these humble positions as means to gain access to their superiors. Therefore, these two men were both sages; yet they still could not avoid serving in lowly positions and enduring such disgraceful circumstances when entering the world. Thus, it is clear that those who are truly capable of holding office do not seek to do so lightly.

## 21

In the state of Song there was a wealthy man; on a rainy day, his wall collapsed. His son said, "If we don't repair the wall, there will be thieves," and his neighbor's father also said as much. That evening, indeed, a great theft occurred in his house; the family greatly praised their own son but suspected the neighbor's father. In ancient times, Duke Wu of Zheng wanted to attack Hu, so he gave his daughter in marriage to a man from Hu. He then asked his ministers: "I want to wage war; who is suitable to attack?" Guan Qisi said, "Hu can be attacked." He then executed Guan Qisi and said: "Hu is a country of our brothers; you say we should attack it, why?" The ruler of Hu heard about this and thought that the state of Zheng was friendly toward him, so he did not prepare for defense against Zheng. The people of Zheng launched a surprise attack on Hu and captured it. In these two cases, the knowledge or advice given was appropriate in both instances; yet one advisor was executed and the other suspected. It is not difficult to have knowledge, but it is difficult to handle that knowledge properly.

## 22

In the past, Mi Zixia was loved by the ruler of Wei. According to the laws of the state of Wei, anyone who stole and drove the ruler's chariot would be punished with amputation of a foot. Later, when Mi Zixia's mother fell ill and the news spread, someone went to inform him at night. Mi Zixia then forged an order and drove out in the ruler's chariot. The ruler heard about it and praised him, saying: "How filial! He committed a crime punishable by amputation for the sake of his mother!" When he went with the ruler to tour an orchard, Mi Zixia tasted a peach and found it sweet; before finishing it, he offered the rest to the ruler. The ruler said: "How much he loves me! He forgot his own taste and thought of me!" Later, when Mi Zixia's beauty faded and the ruler's affection waned, he incurred the ruler's displeasure. The ruler said: "He once forged an order to drive my chariot and also once gave me the remaining half of a peach he had eaten." Therefore, Mi Zixia's actions did not change from the beginning; yet he was praised as virtuous at first and later punished with a crime—this was due to the extreme shift in affection and hatred. Therefore, when someone is loved by their ruler, even appropriate advice will be met with greater closeness; but if they are disliked by the ruler, then even a just punishment will lead to increased estrangement. Therefore, those who offer advice or persuasion must carefully observe whether their ruler is inclined toward love or hatred before proceeding with their arguments. The dragon, as a creature, can be tamed and ridden. Yet beneath its throat is a patch of backward-facing scales about a chi in diameter; if anyone touches this area, the dragon will surely kill them. A ruler also has his own "backward-facing scales"—if an advisor can avoid touching the ruler's sensitive points, then he is nearly successful.

## 23

Some people transmitted their writings to the state of Qin. The King of Qin read the books Gu Fen and Wu Du, and said: "Alas! If I could meet this man and associate with him, I would have no regrets even if I were to die!" Li Si said, "These are the writings of Han Fei." Qin therefore launched an urgent attack on the state of Han. The King of Han initially did not employ Han Fei, but when in dire straits, he sent Han Fei as an envoy to Qin. The King of Qin was pleased with him, but had yet to fully trust and employ him. Li Si and Yao Jia were jealous of Han Fei and slandered him, saying: "Han Fei is a prince of the royal family in Han. Now that Your Majesty wishes to conquer all the feudal states, Han Fei will ultimately remain loyal to Han and not to Qin; this is human nature. If Your Majesty does not employ him, but keeps him here for a long time and then sends him back to Han, this will leave behind a hidden danger. It would be better to put him to death under the pretense of some legal offense." The King of Qin agreed and handed Han Fei over to the officials for punishment. Li Si sent someone to give Han Fei poison, ordering him to commit suicide. Han Fei wanted to plead his case in person but was denied an audience. Later, the King of Qin regretted this and sent someone to pardon Han Fei—but by then, Han Fei had already died. Shenzi and Hanzidi both wrote books that were passed down to later generations, and many scholars studied them. I alone feel sorrow for Han Fei, who was unable to free himself despite writing about the difficulties of persuasion.

## 24

Grand Historian said: Laozi valued Dao, which is empty and formless; he acted without deliberate effort and responded to changes accordingly. Therefore, his writings are filled with subtle and difficult-to-understand expressions. Zhuangzi scattered the principles of morality and freedom in his discussions; yet ultimately he returned to the idea of naturalness. Shenzi emphasized humility, applying it to the relationship between names and realities. Hanzi used strict standards like a plumb line or measuring rope; he analyzed practical matters, clarified right and wrong, and in the end his approach was harsh and lacking in mercy. All of these ideas originated from the principles of Dao and De; yet Laozi's understanding was the most profound and far-reaching.
